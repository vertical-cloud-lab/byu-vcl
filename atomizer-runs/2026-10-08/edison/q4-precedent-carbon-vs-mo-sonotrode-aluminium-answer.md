## Query

Has anyone reported ultrasonic atomization of aluminium or aluminium alloys in which the melt failed to atomize and instead built up, froze or dripped off the sonotrode, and attributed it to insufficient vibration amplitude, a cold sonotrode or poor wetting of a carbon or graphite sonotrode? Has anyone compared carbon (graphite or carbon-fibre-reinforced carbon) versus molybdenum or titanium sonotrode plates for aluminium ultrasonic atomization (3D Lab ATO, AMAZEMET rePowder or research rigs)?

## Answer

Yes. ## Short answer

**Yes, partial atomization failures and relevant mechanisms have been reported—but not as one documented case with all the proposed causes.** An aluminium-matrix composite has been observed falling from an ultrasonic atomizer in large, **unatomized drops**. Its authors attributed this principally to excessive melt delivery relative to what the ultrasonic system could process, **not** specifically to a cold or poorly wetted carbon plate. Separately, an aluminium-alloy study explicitly attributed *initial spattering* to inadequate wetting of a carbon-fibre-composite (CFC) plate; atomization then became stable. I found **no published, controlled comparison** of carbon/graphite/CFC against molybdenum or titanium atomizing plates for aluminium on a 3D Lab ATO, AMAZEMET rePowder, or research rig. (jedynak2024processabilityofaluminummatrix pages 3-6, singh2026synthesisandcharacterization pages 1-3, jedynak2024processabilityofaluminummatrix pages 1-3, gomell2307microstructureofa pages 1-3)

### What was actually observed

**Large drops rather than powder.** Jedynak *et al.* atomized AlSi9Mg containing 20 vol.% SiC using induction and plasma melting on a rePowder and arc melting on a 3D Lab ATOLab+. In induction trials, increasing the pressure difference or nozzle size lowered powder-production efficiency; the authors report that excessive poured volume exceeded the ultrasonic system's capacity and that much of the melt **fell off in large drops without atomizing**. Their Figure 4a documents the high-volume flow. Arc/plasma trials had a different problem: irregular fragments detached during melting, disrupted liquid coverage of the sonotrode and became scrap. They also report approximately twice the efficiency at 40 rather than 60 kHz, saying the lower frequency allowed higher ultrasonic operating parameters; they do **not** supply a measured plate-amplitude threshold or identify the plate material. The graphite identified in their methods is the *melting crucible*, not an identified graphite atomizing plate. (jedynak2024processabilityofaluminummatrix pages 1-3, jedynak2024processabilityofaluminummatrix pages 3-6, jedynak2024processabilityofaluminummatrix media 1e5c04e7)

**Carbon-plate wetting, but successful subsequent atomization.** Singh *et al.* used an AMAZEMET system with a CFC plate on a 60-kHz sonotrode for pure Al and Al–Cu, Al–Si and Al–Mg melts. They expressly attribute startup *spattering* to insufficient wetting of the CFC surface, but state that stable atomization followed quickly once wetting developed. They also associate coarse startup flakes/particles with incomplete breakup or sonotrode wetting. This is evidence for a **transient wetting-related disturbance**, not a report of sustained melt buildup, freezing or dripping from that plate. (singh2026synthesisandcharacterization pages 1-3, singh2026synthesisandcharacterization pages 3-7)

**Amplitude, temperature and poor wetting as mechanisms.** Lierke and Griesshammer's research on ultrasonic atomization, which included aluminium, describes a critical vibration amplitude below which droplet ejection does not occur. They additionally state that a poorly wettable *alumina–titania protective cover* would let melt drop off a downward-facing resonator rather than atomize, and describe delivering melt to a preheated atomizing surface. That dripping statement is **not identified as a graphite-plate aluminium trial**. An AMAZEMET patent states that alloy crystallizes on a sonotrode surface held below its melting point and argues that carbon/SiC ceramic surfaces' low wettability is disadvantageous for atomization; it proposes wettable metal coatings including titanium or molybdenum. Those are technically pertinent **patent assertions**, not a documented experiment diagnosing a particular frozen aluminium buildup or proving a comparative plate performance advantage. (lierke1967theformationof pages 1-2, lierke1967theformationof pages 3-4, EP4192985B1 pages 1-2, EP4192985B1 pages 4-5)

### Carbon versus molybdenum or titanium

The sources establish **use of different materials**, not a head-to-head aluminium test. Singh *et al.* demonstrate successful Al-based atomization using CFC. A rePowder study reports a **molybdenum** sonotrode at 40 kHz and 30 µm amplitude, but its melt is the **Fe-based** alloy Fe₂VTa₀.₀₅Al₀.₉₅, not an aluminium-base alloy. Another rePowder study uses a **TZM molybdenum-alloy** sonotrode for Nb-based C103, likewise not an aluminium-base alloy. Titanium appears among the patent's proposed *coating* materials and as a component elsewhere in ultrasonic assemblies; neither constitutes a reported carbon-versus-titanium plate trial with Al. Cross-study differences in feedstock, melt delivery and operating conditions cannot establish which plate performs better. (singh2026synthesisandcharacterization pages 1-3, gomell2307microstructureofa pages 1-3, ciftcli2025alloydevelopmentfrom pages 1-3, EP4192985B1 pages 4-5, EP4192985B1 pages 6-7)

**Conclusion:** The most direct published analogue of "melt drips off instead of atomizing" is Jedynak *et al.*'s AlSi9Mg–SiC **overfeed** observation; the most direct evidence for **poor CFC wetting** is Singh *et al.*'s **short-lived startup spattering**. A cold sonotrode causing solidification and inadequate amplitude preventing breakup are supported as plausible mechanisms by the patent and atomization literature, respectively, but I did not find a documented aluminium run that assigns its observed frozen buildup to either cause, or a controlled carbon-versus-Mo/Ti plate comparison for aluminium. (jedynak2024processabilityofaluminummatrix pages 3-6, jedynak2024processabilityofaluminummatrix media 1e5c04e7, singh2026synthesisandcharacterization pages 1-3, EP4192985B1 pages 1-2, lierke1967theformationof pages 1-2)
