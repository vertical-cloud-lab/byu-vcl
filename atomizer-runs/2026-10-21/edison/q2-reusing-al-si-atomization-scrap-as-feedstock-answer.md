## Remelting Al–Si atomization returns: what the evidence supports

**Clean, compositionally matched returns can be remelted, but they are not equivalent to clean bar stock.** Chamber-wall splats, plate deposits, skull and off-specification powder bring pre-existing oxide skins into the charge; fines and thin splats present particularly large oxidized area per kilogram. Moisture can additionally supply hydrogen. The likely consequences are more dross and entrained oxide-film inclusions (*bifilms*), less reliable melt flow through restrictions, and potentially more metal lost during cleaning. These are metallurgical risks, **not measured percentage penalties for an Al 4047/AlSi12 or AlSi10Mg revert run**: the sources located do not provide a matched virgin-versus-revert atomization trial measuring melt oxygen, dissolved hydrogen, nozzle failures, powder morphology and final saleable yield together. Reuse of powder in a laser-printing machine is a different experiment from remelting that powder to make new powder. (becker2021proofofprincipleofrecycling pages 2-4, becker2021proofofprincipleofrecycling pages 4-6, gyarmati2025analysisofdifferent pages 5-7, cordova2019revealingtheeffects pages 4-5)

### Quantitative reference points—and their limits

| Outcome | Reported observation | Interpretation for a *new atomization run* |
|---|---|---|
| **Melt oxygen and oxide films** | Salt-refining **off-specification Al–Mg–Sc powder**, not Al–Si, produced remelt blocks containing **192 and 454 ppm oxygen**, both below that alloy’s stated **500-ppm** limit. Metal recovery exceeded **85%** in both trials. Feed-powder oxygen was not reported, so neither an oxygen-removal percentage nor an untreated-revert comparison can be calculated. | Remelting fine powder *with effective oxide separation* can yield low-oxygen consolidated metal; simple remelting cannot be assumed to do so. Oxygen measured in a metal sample also does not independently count entrained bifilms. (becker2021proofofprincipleofrecycling pages 4-6) |
| **Hydrogen** | An industrial **AlSi9Cu3(Fe)** study found that rotary degassing reduced reduced-pressure-test density indices across the fluxes tested, consistent with hydrogen removal; it did **not** report a before/after dissolved-hydrogen concentration applicable to this revert charge. Its oxide-cleaning response depended on flux selection. | There is **no supported universal increment in mL H₂/100 g** attributable to remelting these returns. Measure dissolved hydrogen separately: a reduced-pressure porosity or bifilm index responds to *both* hydrogen and inclusions. (gyarmati2025analysisofdifferent pages 2-4, gyarmati2025analysisofdifferent pages 5-7, malisano2018quantitativestudiesof pages 46-51) |
| **Bifilms and restricted flow** | In that industrial study, starting inclusion-related melt quality varied with charge material; selected fluxes improved both filtration-based cleanliness and bifilm-related reduced-pressure-test measures, whereas one flux **worsened** the filtration-based result. Its **15-pores-per-inch** ceramic-filter test registers clogging and assigns a Quality Temperature Index from **1 (clean)** to **10 (contaminated)**. | Additional skins can become entrained films and obstruct a filter or narrow passage. This supports **increased clogging risk**, not a measured nozzle-clog frequency or a quantified change in intrinsic liquid viscosity. (gyarmati2025analysisofdifferent pages 4-5, gyarmati2025analysisofdifferent pages 5-7) |
| **Casting fluidity** | A published discussion of an **A356** casting comparison reports that **20% and 50% scrap** charges had shorter fluidity than **0% scrap**, with no apparent difference between the two scrap levels. The available text supplies **no defensible fill-length values**. | Reduced *casting fill length* is plausible with dirty returns; the example does not establish a 20% safe limit, a monotonic dose–response, or a value for AlSi12 atomization. Temperature, alloy chemistry and inclusion-mediated flow restriction must be distinguished. (mugeri2018optimisationofcasting pages 31-35) |
| **Oxygen of newly made AlSi10Mg powder** | In an **ingot-fed**, rather than revert-fed, gas-atomization comparison, laboratory powder contained **0.0287 ± 0.0007 wt% O (287 ± 7 ppm)** and commercial powder **0.0878 ± 0.0159 wt% O (878 ± 159 ppm)**. Their measured powder hydrogen contents were **20.1 ± 22.7 ppm** and **54.9 ± 0.942 ppm**, respectively. Finer size, passivation and subsequent handling were possible contributors; feedstock reuse was **not** the comparison. | Re-atomization does not necessarily reproduce either value. Specify and assay the *new powder*, including separate fine and target-size fractions; do not present the difference between these two suppliers as a revert effect. (marinucci2023developmentofalsi10mg pages 96-102) |
| **Satellites, irregulars and yield** | The same ingot-fed AlSi10Mg trials produced mostly spherical powder **with satellites even without revert**. Total collected-powder yield was **62%, 79%, 88% and 78%** of the respective **1.5-, 3.1-, 1.5- and 3.1-kg** charges. The usable **20–50 µm** fraction was **67%, 46%, 49% and 43% of collected powder**, respectively—approximately **42%, 36%, 43% and 34% of the starting charges**, multiplying the reported fractions. A pressure discrepancy, not revert, helped explain the first run’s low total yield. | Satellites and reduced yield **cannot be attributed to revert solely because they appear in the next run**. No source establishes a numerical revert-induced increase in satellites, irregular particles, clogging events or loss of target-size yield across gas, ultrasonic and centrifugal atomizers. (marinucci2023developmentofalsi10mg pages 70-73, marinucci2023developmentofalsi10mg pages 84-90) |

For comparison **only**, AlSi10Mg powder subjected to laser-printing use or deliberate aging—not remelted into a fresh atomization melt—rose from **0.067 to 0.072 wt% O** after one processing condition and from **0.067 ± 0.03 to 0.257 ± 0.07 wt% O** after **96 h at 400 °C in air**. Another reuse study found AlSi10Mg powder oxygen *almost doubled*. These measurements demonstrate the sensitivity of exposed powder to its history; they are **not forecasts of oxygen in powder re-atomized from it**. Aging did not necessarily change particle shape, while oxidized spatter can add coarse irregular particles. (cordova2019revealingtheeffects pages 4-5, fedina2022influenceofalsi10mg pages 5-6, fedina2022influenceofalsi10mg pages 6-8)

### How much oxide can powder or thin splats carry?

Electron microscopy measured approximately **3–5 nm** alumina shells on aluminum powders. For an **illustrative geometric estimate**, assume an intact, uniformly coated **30-µm-diameter sphere**, 3-nm Al₂O₃ film, aluminum density **2.7 g/cm³** and oxide density **3.05 g/cm³**. Shell mass is then approximately **0.068% of the particle**, containing approximately **0.032 wt% oxygen, or 320 ppm**, *from that idealized film alone*. Its surface-to-mass ratio is approximately **0.074 m²/kg**. A **100-mm-diameter spherical bar/ingot**, given the *same* film, has about **3,300-fold less surface and pre-existing film per unit mass**; a **0.1-mm sheet or splat** coated on both broad faces has approximately **100-fold** the coated area per mass of the 100-mm sphere. These are **geometry-derived comparisons, not measured oxide ratios**: irregular splats, roughness, thicker films, hydroxide and adhered dross change the loading. Measured oxygen in an actual powder can differ substantially from this simple-shell calculation. (rufino2007influenceofparticles pages 4-5, marinucci2023developmentofalsi10mg pages 96-102)

### Practical disposition of returns

1. **Segregate and clean.** Keep same-alloy dense, clean returns separate from wall/plate deposits, crucible skull bearing refractory, dross, foreign-alloy particles and off-specification powder. Remove adherent dross, refractory and obvious contamination; clean the crucible, atomization chamber, collector and feed path between runs. In AlSi10Mg trials, particles of **304L steel** from a preceding run were detected in the next powder despite the changeover. Check alloy composition, particularly Mg, after refining: in the Al–Mg–Sc powder-recovery demonstration it fell from **4.6 wt%** in the feed to **0.25 or 0.95 wt%** in the recovered blocks. (marinucci2023developmentofalsi10mg pages 66-70, becker2021proofofprincipleofrecycling pages 4-6, marinucci2023developmentofalsi10mg pages 84-90)
2. **Keep returns dry and control fines.** Store covered and dry; verify drying/preheating appropriate to the equipment before charging, rather than introducing damp, loosely packed powder to molten aluminum. Separate very fine powder from dense returns and process it only by a validated route for high-surface-area material. In the AlSi10Mg atomization study, the **<20 µm** fraction carried substantially more oxygen than coarser fractions; off-spec **<25 µm** Al–Mg–Sc powder required a purpose-designed molten-salt recovery experiment. Fine aluminum powder also requires appropriate combustible-powder controls. (marinucci2023developmentofalsi10mg pages 84-90, becker2021proofofprincipleofrecycling pages 2-4, marinucci2023developmentofalsi10mg pages 66-70)
3. **Treat hydrogen and oxides as different problems.** If hydrogen is above the product-specific limit, use controlled inert-gas rotary degassing and **verify dissolved hydrogen**. Skim separated dross and, where compatible with the alloy and powder specification, evaluate a qualified cleaning flux and/or melt filtration against inclusion measurements. Degassing alone is not a guaranteed bifilm-removal treatment: the industrial Al–Si casting study found flux-dependent cleaning, with the most effective tested formulations containing approximately **11 mol% fluorides** and melting below **600 °C**; another tested flux impaired cleanliness. Salt-refined fine-powder recovery used a **4:1 salt-to-powder** mass ratio, **2 wt% CaF₂** in equimolar NaCl/KCl and **750 °C**—an experimental recovery process, **not** a recommended routine atomizer charge recipe. (gyarmati2025analysisofdifferent pages 2-4, gyarmati2025analysisofdifferent pages 10-13, becker2021proofofprincipleofrecycling pages 2-4, becker2021proofofprincipleofrecycling pages 4-6)
4. **Set revert fraction by qualification, not an invented maximum.** No credible general **maximum percentage of Al 4047/AlSi12 or AlSi10Mg atomization revert** is established by these studies. A fixed claim such as “never exceed 20%” or “50% is safe” would misread the A356 fluidity observation. Begin with a separately characterized, preferably dense/clean return class and a conservative trial blend; increase its fraction **only while** melt hydrogen and inclusion/filtration tests, alloy chemistry, nozzle-flow continuity, new-powder oxygen and size-specific saleable yield remain within the operation’s documented virgin-charge limits. Account for dross, skull, uncollected powder and out-of-size powder separately rather than calling all collected material product yield. (marinucci2023developmentofalsi10mg pages 70-73, marinucci2023developmentofalsi10mg pages 84-90, gyarmati2025analysisofdifferent pages 5-7, mugeri2018optimisationofcasting pages 31-35)

**Bottom line:** Reverting clean Al–Si metal is feasible, but oxidized high-area returns make melt cleanliness and recovery a qualification issue. The numerical oxygen, hydrogen, bifilm, clogging, satellite and yield *changes caused specifically by re-atomizing the listed returns* remain unquantified in the located evidence; baseline atomization numbers and results from other alloys or powder-aging experiments must not be mistaken for those missing measurements. (marinucci2023developmentofalsi10mg pages 70-73, becker2021proofofprincipleofrecycling pages 4-6, marinucci2023developmentofalsi10mg pages 96-102, fedina2022influenceofalsi10mg pages 5-6)
