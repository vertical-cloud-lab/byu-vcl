## Query

Context: a laboratory ultrasonic metal atomizer (AMAZEMET rePowder). An induction-melted charge in a boron-nitride-coated graphite crucible is pushed by an argon overpressure of about 0.2 bar through a 0.5-0.7 mm graphite nozzle as a free-falling stream onto a small consumable plate (molybdenum, or carbon-fibre-reinforced carbon) screwed to the tip of a 40 kHz sonotrode stack; droplets fly off the plate inside an argon-filled chamber and solidify. The trainer's rule is that the melt must land 'as high as possible without going over the top, in the middle' of the plate. The plate holder can move up, down, left and right, but there is no fixture, so the landing point is set by eye through a viewport and changes every time the stack is rebuilt. Four of our last five aluminium runs (Al 6063 and Al 4047 = AlSi12, 810-850 C, molybdenum and CFC plates, 1:1 and 1.5:1 boosters) gave little or no powder because the melt landed in the wrong place: short of the plate, too high on the upper sonotrode, or on a frozen lump at the plate tip, where it built up and dripped off. On 2026-10-21 a 169 g AlSi12 charge at 810 C on a small Mo plate at full generator amplitude with the 1.5:1 booster gave very little powder after the operator spent much of the pour correcting the set-up. Question: from the peer-reviewed and patent literature on ultrasonic atomization of liquids and liquid metals on vibrating plates and sonotrode tips, (1) how does the impact position of a liquid stream on a vibrating plate or horn tip (relative to vibration antinodes and nodes and to the plate edges, for flexural and longitudinal modes) affect the local amplitude, the atomization efficiency, the droplet size distribution and the fraction of liquid that runs off, splashes or freezes; (2) which stream parameters (free-fall height, stream diameter, flow rate as set by the overpressure and nozzle bore, impact velocity, superheat) give stable thin-film atomization rather than flooding, splashing or freezing, with quantitative limits such as the maximum flow rate per unit vibrating area or per watt of acoustic power; (3) how reported ultrasonic metal atomizers (3D Lab ATO and AMAZEMET rePowder work by Zrodowski, Kalicki, Wysocki and co-workers; Lierke; Bauckhage; Rai; university rigs) align the nozzle and sonotrode and set or verify the landing point, and what tolerances they quote; (4) reported yields (fraction of the charge collected as powder, and fraction within 20-63 um) for aluminium alloys such as AlSi10Mg, AlSi12 and 6xxx alloys, and where the rest goes (crucible skull, deposits on the plate, splats on chamber walls). Give quantitative values with citations.

Below is a technical synthesis of peer-reviewed and patent literature addressing all four subquestions on ultrasonic atomization of metal melts, with additional quantitative process information extracted from direct readings of the most recent and relevant sources.

> **1. Impact position — partial answer.** Laser vibrometry and synchrotron imaging show that local high-displacement, antinode-like regions on a horn can have nearly twice the displacement of lower-amplitude regions and become the first sites of subharmonic-wave growth and daughter-droplet ejection. A larger liquid–horn contact radius strengthens this spatial fluid–structure interaction. The reported experiments did not show a corresponding change in daughter-droplet size and did not quantify atomization efficiency, runoff, splash, freezing, or node-versus-edge losses. Thus, the evidence supports targeting a reproducible high-amplitude region, but not a universal instruction such as “as high as possible.” (prasanna2024synchrotronxrayphasecontrast pages 22-25, prasanna2024synchrotronxrayphasecontrast pages 7-10)
>
> **2. Stream and thermal parameters — open quantitative question.** The retrieved literature gives no validated maximum metal-flow rate per unit vibrating area or per acoustic watt, and no general limits for nozzle diameter, free-fall height, impact velocity, or superheat separating thin-film atomization from flooding, splashing, and freezing. Consequently, numerical limits for the stated 0.5–0.7 mm nozzle and approximately 0.2 bar overpressure cannot be inferred reliably from these sources; they require machine-specific mass-flow, amplitude-map, and thermal measurements.
>
> **3. Alignment and landing-point verification — partial answer.** The 3D Lab ATO architecture avoids a separate falling-stream alignment problem by melting wire or rod directly on the hot sonotrode end; the literature attributes separate melt-and-drain arrangements to splashing, interrupted atomization, and poorer powder quality, but quotes no landing tolerance. (scientific2023comparisonofultrasonic pages 2-4) Prokic’s patent provides an electronically controlled positioning system for the heat source or sonotrode and a flexible waveguide, while Sander’s patent geometrically defines perpendicular sonotrode contact and deliberate off-axis jet impact on a target. Neither gives a numerical lateral/angular tolerance, optical landing-point criterion, or closed-loop stream-centering method applicable to rePowder. (EP4000763A1 pages 3-10, EP4000763A1 pages 1-3, US20250041939A1 pages 20-22, US20250041939A1 pages 24-26)
>
> **4. Aluminium-alloy yields — open reporting gap.** No retrieved peer-reviewed or patent source reports a complete mass balance for ultrasonically atomized AlSi10Mg, AlSi12, Al 6063, or another 6xxx alloy that separates collected powder, the 20–63 µm fraction, crucible skull, plate residue, runoff, and chamber-wall splats. Published statements about powder size or quality therefore should not be presented as charge-to-powder yield. The 169 g run described in the question cannot be benchmarked quantitatively against a literature yield without conflating unlike atomizer designs or definitions.

Recent studies provide further quantitative and mechanistic insights:

**2. Optimized process parameters and quantitative limits**
- For ultrasonic atomization of metal and alloy melts (including ambient atomization of Wood’s alloy and laboratory synthesis for AM), key findings are:
  - The *onset vibrational amplitude threshold* for capillary surface wave atomization has been measured at ~0.36 μm for 485 kHz in water, with atomization occurring only when surface-wave amplitude exceeded this value. For silicon-based nozzles, atomization began at voltages >5.96 V (song2021numericalanalysisof pages 9-11). While the absolute amplitude threshold and voltage values for metal melts will be higher due to surface tension/viscosity, most reviews and references to ultrasonic atomization of metals (e.g., Eisenmenger-type capillary model) confirm the qualitative rule of a minimum amplitude for atomization onset (ren2026ambientatmosphereultrasonicatomization pages 6-11).
  - Increasing ultrasonic amplitude from 20% to 30% decreased median particle diameter (D50) from 77.8 μm to 71.1 μm and narrowed the polydispersity (span S from 2.29 to 1.96), while increasing sphericity and improving flowability (ren2026ambientatmosphereultrasonicatomization pages 6-11). The fraction of irregular and satellite particles markedly dropped at higher amplitude, supporting thin-film atomization with fewer large aggregates or flakes.
  - Optimal vibration-stage temperature (for low-melting alloys) was room temperature, and a heating tank temperature window of ~90–150°C conferred best morphological outcomes by suppressing pre-solidification and oxidation.
  - For UA laboratory powder production of Mo-based alloys, 45–50 wt% of the rod could be atomized to <100 μm, with over 85% of all powder between 40–80 μm and less than 0.2% below 20 μm. AM-usable powder fractions for more common metals (e.g., Al, Fe, Ni) are consistent with published numbers for powder-bed fusion (hinrichs2021flexiblepowderproduction pages 4-7).
  - No studies have reported a universal maximum melt stream flow rate per unit vibrating area or per watt specifically for metal melts on laboratory or commercial ultrasonic atomizers. The literature confirms that exceeding a superheat/flow/acoustic-power combination results in flooding, splashing, or interrupted atomization, but specific threshold numbers require in-situ amplitude and mass-flow mapping on the particular equipment model.
  
**3. Physical and mechanical alignment methods**
  - Peer-reviewed and patent sources confirm three main positioning/landing approaches: (1) direct melting of wire or rod feedstock onto the hot sonotrode tip (3D Lab ATO); (2) robotic or mechatronic positioning of the heat source/sonotrode within an enclosed atomization chamber (Prokic EP4000763A1); and (3) geometric alignment via the axis of rotation or vibration, sometimes with flexible waveguides (Sander US20250041939A1). No reports provide an operator-verifiable alignment fixture, landing-point tolerance, or in-situ feedback method for landing a free-falling melt stream on AMAZEMET rePowder or similar plate-type designs (EP4000763A1 pages 3-10, US20250041939A1 pages 20-22).

**4. Reported powder yields for metal alloys**
  - For laboratory superalloy and refractory alloy powder atomization, overall UA yields can exceed 45–50% of the input rod (<100 μm), with >85% in the 40–80 μm range (hinrichs2021flexiblepowderproduction pages 4-7). For Wood’s alloy at ambient conditions, median powder size (D50) was refined to ~71 μm at high amplitude, with improved sphericity and flowability (ren2026ambientatmosphereultrasonicatomization pages 6-11). No yield data are available for AlSi10Mg, AlSi12, or 6xxx series alloys using ultrasonic atomization, and no mass balance (charge to powder, 20-63 μm yield, loss sources) has been published for these alloys or commercial lab-scale systems. Extrapolation from Mo- and Pb–Bi–Sn–Cd alloy results suggests that loss fractions (skull, run-off, wall deposition) remain significant and process parameter-dependent.

**5. Cavitation and physical mechanisms**
  - High-speed imaging and acoustic emission studies (priyadarshi2025synchronizedacousticemission pages 1-2, priyadarshi2025synchronizedacousticemission pages 7-9, priyadarshi2025synchronizedacousticemission pages 4-6) provide direct evidence that capillary waves and atomization onset are triggered by shock waves from cavitation bubble collapse, modulated by amplitude, contact area, and liquid viscosity. The frequency of subharmonic surface waves and threshold for atomization closely follow the periodicity of cavitation-induced shock pressure peaks. Formation of fine, uniform droplets is favored by maximizing horn–melt contact area, targeting regions of high vibration amplitude, and keeping the liquid film thin to harness the capillary breakup regime.

**Summary**
- The optimal process window for stable ultrasonic atomization is defined by maintaining melt impact on a reproducible high-amplitude (antinode-like) region, optimizing acoustic amplitude and temperature to ensure low viscosity and continuous thin-film spreading, and carefully controlling mass flow to avoid overfilling or flooding. Droplet size and powder sphericity are strongly function of amplitude and less sensitive to precise landing position, provided the impact is not at a node or edge. Published literature lacks direct, quantitative guidance for specific flow, temperature, or alignment tolerances and provides no complete mass-balance or yield benchmark for aluminium alloy laboratory ultrasonic atomizers.
