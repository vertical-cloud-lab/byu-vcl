## Aluminium Cup Charging Strategy for Ultrasonic Atomization: Literature Evidence Assessment

### 1. ALUMINIUM ENCAPSULATION OF ALLOY ADDITIONS

Encapsulating alloying additions in aluminium is an established industrial and laboratory practice, though published quantitative recovery data specifically for Al-foil-wrapped additions to aluminium melts are surprisingly sparse.

**Compact briquettes (Al-clad powder compacts).** The most directly relevant study is Najafabadi (1996), a doctoral investigation at McGill University on the dissolution kinetics of high-melting-point alloying elements in molten aluminium. Iron and manganese powders were studied both as discrete particles wrapped in aluminium foil and plunged into the melt, and as compact briquettes consisting of alloying-element particles mixed with aluminium powder (najafabadi1996thekineticsof pages 33-37, najafabadi1996thekineticsof pages 21-25). For briquettes, the aluminium powder melts first within the compact, after which exothermic reactions between the alloying particles and the surrounding liquid aluminium raise the briquette temperature substantially above the bath temperature, accelerating intermetallic formation and dissolution. The dominant intermetallic phases formed were MnAl₄ (for Mn) and Fe₂Al₅ (for Fe), growing by parabolic diffusion kinetics (najafabadi1996thekineticsof pages 37-41, najafabadi1996thekineticsof pages 41-44). Briquettes disintegrated within approximately 25–35 seconds at 720°C, after which the saturated liquid dispersed into the bath, producing a sharp concentration increase (najafabadi1996thekineticsof pages 48-51). Complete dissolution took approximately 420 seconds for Mn briquettes and 330 seconds for Fe briquettes (najafabadi1996thekineticsof pages 51-54). For discrete particles, powders were classified by size, wrapped in aluminium foil, and plunged into molten aluminium for dissolution-time comparison in an intensively stirred melt at 1100°C (najafabadi1996thekineticsof pages 33-37).

**Recovery considerations.** The literature establishes that fine powders added loosely to molten aluminium suffer from trapping in surface dross, burning, and low recovery because they lack sufficient kinetic energy to penetrate the gas–liquid interface and may resurface with carrier bubbles (najafabadi1996thekineticsof pages 21-25). Coarse powders penetrate more effectively but dissolve slowly and may settle, forming sludge. An optimal particle size range of 40–140 mesh (100–400 μm) was identified for manganese additions (najafabadi1996thekineticsof pages 21-25). Encapsulation in aluminium (foil, briquette, or compact) serves as a recognized mitigation by submerging the additions below the melt surface, preventing direct contact with the atmosphere, and promoting exothermic dissolution reactions that accelerate assimilation.

**Scandium and rare earth additions.** Scandium is introduced into aluminium primarily through Al–Sc master alloys (typically 2 wt.% Sc, range 1–20 wt.%), rather than as elemental powder. Direct dissolution of metallic Sc in aluminium at 800°C is slow, requiring more than one hour for gram-size pellets (røyset2005scandiuminaluminium pages 1-3). Commercial practice uses aluminothermic reduction of ScF₃ to produce Al–Sc master alloy; the process begins around 810°C and completes near 930°C, with one report indicating ~90% scandium recovery in the reduction step (mukhachov2016scandiumandits pages 3-5, mukhachov2016scandiumanditsa pages 3-5). An Al–10%Sc master alloy is added to molten aluminium at ~900°C, followed by superheating to 1150°C for 30 minutes (mukhachov2016scandiumandits pages 3-5).

**Magnesium losses.** Magnesium is particularly susceptible to oxidation loss. Approximately 1% of aluminium is oxidized per minute while uncooled dross remains exposed to air, and increasing Mg from 0 to 2.5 wt.% in thin (0.5 mm) sheet increased melt loss by 4–8% (osoba2018reviewonoxide pages 1-2, osoba2018reviewonoxide pages 2-3). The literature consistently recommends submerging Mg additions below the melt surface to minimize oxidation, which is functionally equivalent to the proposed Al-cup encapsulation strategy.

**Quantitative recovery data for specific elements (Cr, Ti, Zr, Er, Ce, Li, Zn, Sn).** The retrieved literature does not provide element-specific quantitative recovery percentages for Al-foil-wrapped additions of these elements. Manganese, chromium, and titanium are described as comparatively persistent during remelting, while magnesium and zinc are more volatile and subject to greater loss (pereira2026rotaryfurnacerecyclingof pages 30-32). The literature is effectively silent on quantitative foil-wrapping recovery data for Zr, Sc, Er, Ce, Li, and Sn in aluminium melts.

### 2. FINE POWDER ADDED TO MOLTEN ALUMINIUM: WETTING AND ENTRAPMENT

**Poor wetting and agglomeration.** The aluminium composite fabrication literature extensively documents the difficulty of incorporating fine particles into molten aluminium. Fine powders have large specific surface area, promoting agglomeration through van der Waals forces (li2026designinnovationsand pages 11-14). Particle surfaces are commonly covered by adsorbed gas layers that prevent molten aluminium from contacting the particle; at sufficiently high particle concentration, these layers may bridge and cause total rejection from the melt (sarkar2009investigationonwetting pages 41-46). Contact angles above 90° indicate non-wetting conditions, which are typical for many ceramics and oxides in contact with liquid aluminium below ~1000°C (sarkar2009investigationonwetting pages 35-41, yunlei2022researchprogresson pages 10-11). Wetting measurements for aluminium are highly sensitive to oxygen contamination because the tenacious oxide skin on the droplet obstructs spreading; oxygen partial pressure must typically be reduced to 10⁻⁷–10⁻⁹ atm for meaningful measurements (sarkar2009investigationonwetting pages 35-41).

**Stir-casting practice.** Standard practice for incorporating particles involves melting aluminium at 700–800°C, creating a vortex with a mechanical stirrer, preheating particles at 200–500°C for 30–60 minutes to remove moisture and gases, and gradually feeding particles at rates of 8–10 mg/min while stirring at 700–800 rpm for 10–40 minutes (kumar2024investigationofaluminum pages 7-10, kumar2024investigationofaluminum pages 1-4). Excessive feeding rates promote particle agglomeration, while inadequate stirring leads to non-uniform dispersion, weak interfacial bonding, and porosity (kumar2024investigationofaluminum pages 7-10, kareem2021areviewon pages 4-5).

**Oxide film entrapment (bifilm defects).** According to Campbell's bifilm theory, extensively documented in the retrieved literature, molten aluminium rapidly forms a tenacious oxide surface film. When this surface is disturbed—by stirring, pouring, or charging—the oxide can fold into the melt, producing double oxide-film (bifilm) defects that behave like internal cracks (dispinar2006determinationofmetal pages 20-23, campbell2021apersonalview pages 1-2, yousefian2017poreformationin pages 42-47). Bifilm populations of approximately 10⁶–10⁹ m⁻³ may occur in ordinary aluminium melts, with 0.1–2.0 m² of oxide-film surface entrained per 10 kg of poured metal (gyarmati2026understandingtheinteractions pages 1-2). These defects reduce tensile and fatigue properties and increase property scatter (campbell2021apersonalview pages 1-2, dispinar2006determinationofmetal pages 95-98). A critical surface-breaking velocity of approximately 0.35–0.5 m/s is identified above which oxide entrainment becomes likely (yousefian2017poreformationin pages 42-47).

**Encapsulation as mitigation.** Pre-encapsulation in aluminium (foil, compact, briquette) is a recognized laboratory mitigation for adding fine particles or powders. By wrapping additions in Al foil, the powder is submerged below the melt surface without direct contact with air, reducing oxidation and dross formation (najafabadi1996thekineticsof pages 33-37, najafabadi1996thekineticsof pages 21-25). However, the oxide skin on the aluminium foil itself introduces oxide into the melt when it melts. Coarse granules or master-alloy chunks are generally preferred in industrial practice because they have lower surface-area-to-volume ratios, producing less oxide per unit of addition, dissolve more predictably, and can be plunged below the melt surface more reliably.

### 3. MELT-IN-PLACE / IN-SITU ALLOYING FROM BLENDED CHARGES

**Arc melting of multi-component alloys.** Arc melting is the most common laboratory method for small-batch alloy preparation from elemental pieces. Hinte et al. (2024) compared arc melting (AM) and vacuum induction melting (VIM) for a senary Ti₁₆.₆Zr₁₆.₆Hf₁₆.₆Co₁₀Ni₂₀Cu₂₀ high-entropy alloy. Using binary pre-alloys, compositional deviation was below 1.5 atomic percent per element after a single melting step (hinte2024acomparativestudy pages 4-7). However, arc-melted material remained comparatively inhomogeneous: even homogenization at 900°C for 100 hours produced only slight improvements due to slow diffusion of Hf and Zr (hinte2024acomparativestudy pages 7-9). The study concluded that VIM combined with pre-alloys produced more homogeneous material with shorter heat-treatment requirements.

**In-situ alloying via LPBF.** Hou et al. (2021) demonstrated in-situ alloying of FeCoCrNi HEA via selective laser melting from elemental powders. Composition deviation could be controlled within 20 wt.%, and heat treatment at 1200°C for 20 hours was required to achieve a homogeneous microstructure. This approach eliminates pre-alloying but introduces compositional heterogeneity that must be resolved by post-processing.

**Implications for the proposed workflow.** For the proposed ~100 g aluminium alloy batches melted in a single step for ultrasonic atomization, the literature suggests that: (i) a single melt-and-atomize step without intermediate stirring or remelting carries a significant risk of compositional inhomogeneity, especially for slow-dissolving elements like Ti, Zr, and Cr; (ii) electromagnetic stirring or superheat hold times would be beneficial; and (iii) the small batch size (~100 g) may partially mitigate inhomogeneity due to shorter diffusion distances and the vigorous convection induced by induction heating. The literature is largely silent on specific compositional accuracy data for small-batch ultrasonic atomization of aluminium alloys from blended elemental charges.

### 4. GRAPHITE CRUCIBLES AND MOLTEN ALUMINIUM

**Al₄C₃ formation.** The formation of Al₄C₃ from molten aluminium and graphite is thermodynamically favorable. At 970°C, the standard Gibbs free energy is approximately −35.1 kcal/mol (schilling1988laboratorytestingof pages 31-35). In a test where a graphite cube coated with TiB₂-based paint was soaked in molten aluminium at ~970°C for four weeks under argon, the coating cracked, aluminium penetrated, and Al₄C₃ formed with a 3.8:1 volume expansion relative to graphite, further disrupting the coating. No unreacted graphite remained after exposure (schilling1988laboratorytestingof pages 31-35). The reaction is thus aggressive given sufficient time and temperature.

**Wetting behavior.** The retrieved literature does not provide a precise contact angle for molten aluminium on graphite at specific temperatures. General wetting studies show that molten aluminium exhibits non-wetting behavior (contact angles >90°) on many substrates below ~1000°C due to the persistent oxide skin, but contact angles can decrease below 90° above 1000°C when the oxide degrades (sarkar2009investigationonwetting pages 35-41). For boron nitride, the contact angle is approximately 160° up to 900°C, decreasing between 900–1000°C but remaining higher than for alumina and silicon nitride (rudolph2013boronnitriderelease pages 4-7).

**Crucible and coating recommendations.** Hexagonal boron nitride (BN) is the most consistently recommended release coating/barrier for aluminium contact. BN is described as poorly wetted by aluminium (contact angle ~160°), stable in air to ~900°C and under reducing conditions to ~1650°C, and effective as a non-sticking release agent for aluminium foundry equipment (rudolph2013boronnitriderelease pages 4-7, rudolph2013boronnitriderelease pages 1-4). Hot-pressed BN (HPBN) has been identified as more suitable than refractory-coated graphite for high-temperature applications involving aluminium vapor (liu2004thedurabilityof pages 1-2). SiC coatings on graphite eventually degrade through sublimation and aluminium diffusion, leading to spalling (liu2004thedurabilityof pages 1-2).

**Li/Mg aggressiveness to oxide crucibles.** The retrieved literature confirms that lithium is "extremely corrosive, attacking most metals and oxides" at elevated temperatures (from Johnson et al., 1976, retrieved but not deeply cited). Magnesium-bearing aluminium alloys promote breakaway oxidation through MgO and MgAl₂O₄ (spinel) formation, increasing dross and potentially attacking oxide crucible materials (osoba2018reviewonoxide pages 1-2, osoba2018reviewonoxide pages 2-3). The literature is silent on specific quantitative attack rates of Li- or Mg-bearing Al melts on alumina, yttria, or zirconia crucibles in the context of small-batch atomization.

**TiC/ZrC formation.** When Ti or Zr are present in an Al melt in contact with graphite, the formation of TiC and ZrC is thermodynamically more favorable than Al₄C₃. This is noted in the composite literature where Ti additions are used specifically to protect B₄C particles from decomposition by forming a TiC barrier layer. The implication for the proposed workflow is that alloys containing Ti and Zr in contact with graphite crucibles would preferentially form TiC/ZrC, consuming the alloying additions intended for Al₃(Sc,Zr) precipitation and potentially generating carbide inclusions harmful to LPBF powder quality.

### 5. DOES THE ALUMINIUM CUP HELP OR HURT AT THE CRUCIBLE INTERFACE?

**Potential benefits.** The solid aluminium cup would initially act as a physical barrier between the graphite crucible and the alloying-element powders, delaying direct Al-melt contact with graphite during the early heating phase. This could reduce Al₄C₃ formation time modestly. The cup also encapsulates the non-aluminium powders, preventing their oxidation during heating and ensuring they are submerged in molten aluminium once the cup melts.

**Oxide skin concerns.** When the aluminium cup melts (~660°C), its oxide skin does not disappear. The literature consistently shows that the oxide detaches, floats free, and becomes suspended as film-like inclusions in the melt (dispinar2006determinationofmetal pages 20-23). The oxide can persist as a collapsed shell or fragments that may fold into bifilm defects (pereira2026rotaryfurnacerecyclingof pages 16-19, osoba2018reviewonoxide pages 2-3). For a machined cup with relatively low surface-area-to-volume ratio and thick walls, the oxide burden would be proportionally small compared with the same mass of aluminium in powder form. However, the cup geometry creates a closed oxide envelope that could trap the powdered charge as it melts, forming a transient "oxide bag" containing the alloying powders before the bag ruptures. This is qualitatively analogous to the persistent oxide envelopes described around scrap pieces during secondary smelting that hinder coalescence and trap metal (pereira2026rotaryfurnacerecyclingof pages 16-19, osoba2018reviewonoxide pages 2-3).

**Crucible interface.** Once the aluminium melts, it will contact the graphite crucible directly, and Al₄C₃ formation proceeds. An aluminium liner does not provide a permanent barrier—it simply delays direct contact until the cup melts. The literature does not document the use of "sacrificial aluminium containers" inside graphite crucibles as a recognized contamination-mitigation strategy. A BN wash coating on the graphite would be a more effective and established approach (rudolph2013boronnitriderelease pages 1-4).

**Net assessment.** The aluminium cup approach is a reasonable compromise for automated charging of small batches: it provides a convenient solid container for robotically dispensing powders, ensures submersion of additions, and introduces relatively little oxide compared with the equivalent mass of aluminium powder. However, it does not solve the graphite crucible problem and introduces a transient oxide bag that may trap powders. The literature is silent on this specific "sacrificial container" approach.

### 6. HEATING SEQUENCE

**Intermetallic shell formation.** When high-melting-point solute powders (Ti, Zr, Cr, Fe, Ni) are immersed in molten aluminium, dissolution proceeds through intermetallic-layer formation rather than simple melting. For Fe, Fe₂Al₅ forms as the dominant layer; for Mn, MnAl₄ forms (najafabadi1996thekineticsof pages 37-41). Tunca et al. (referenced in Najafabadi) observed diffusion-controlled intermetallic growth on Mo, Nb, Cr, and Y immersed in liquid aluminium, confirming that all these elements form intermetallic shells (najafabadi1996thekineticsof pages 41-44). These shells grow parabolically with time and can retard further dissolution if not disrupted by mechanical stirring.

**Risks before aluminium melts.** If reactive powders (Ti, Zr, Cr) are heated inside the aluminium cup from room temperature to 660°C, several risks arise: (i) oxidation of the powders if the atmosphere contains residual oxygen; (ii) sintering of fine metal powders at temperatures approaching their oxide-reduction or diffusion-bonding temperatures; (iii) solid-state reactions between dissimilar metal powders at points of contact. However, in the proposed system (argon atmosphere <50 ppm O₂, induction heating with rapid ramp rates), these risks are somewhat mitigated. The aluminium cup provides physical separation from the atmosphere and melts at 660°C, which is below the temperatures at which most solid-state intermetallic reactions become rapid for the listed elements.

**Recommended practice.** The literature recommends: (i) rapid heating through the melting range to minimize hold time in the semi-solid state where powders can oxidize and sinter; (ii) superheat of 100–200°C above the liquidus to increase solubility and diffusion rates; (iii) mechanical or electromagnetic stirring to enhance dissolution by reducing boundary-layer thickness; and (iv) holding time sufficient for complete dissolution—at 720°C, Mn briquettes required ~420 s and Fe briquettes ~330 s for complete dissolution (najafabadi1996thekineticsof pages 51-54). For very slow-dissolving elements such as Sc (røyset2005scandiuminaluminium pages 1-3), higher superheat temperatures (up to 1150°C for Al–Sc) are standard practice (mukhachov2016scandiumandits pages 3-5). Stirring beyond full particle suspension provides negligible additional benefit (najafabadi1996thekineticsof pages 1-6).

**Key concern for the proposed workflow.** In the AMAZEMET rePOWDER system, electromagnetic stirring from the induction coil provides some convection, but dedicated mechanical stirring is not available. The small melt volume (~100 g) and strong induction stirring may partially compensate. However, for slow-dissolving elements (Ti, Zr, Cr at low superheat), incomplete dissolution is a genuine risk without adequate hold time and superheat. The literature suggests that temperatures of 900–1100°C and hold times of several minutes to tens of minutes would be needed for complete dissolution of these refractory additions.

### Summary Assessment

The proposed aluminium-cup charging strategy is technically sound in principle and draws on established practices from aluminium foundry and composite fabrication. The key advantages are: (i) convenient robotic dispensing of powders into a solid container; (ii) submersion of additions below the melt surface, reducing oxidation loss; (iii) exothermic reaction between aluminium and alloying powders that accelerates dissolution; and (iv) lower oxide-area-to-volume ratio than the equivalent charge of aluminium powder.

The key risks and limitations are: (i) the oxide skin on the aluminium cup will form a transient oxide bag that may trap powders; (ii) the graphite crucible will react with molten aluminium to form Al₄C₃, with carbide-forming elements (Ti, Zr) being preferentially consumed; (iii) a BN wash coating on the graphite crucible is strongly recommended; (iv) slow-dissolving elements require adequate superheat and hold time; (v) without mechanical stirring, compositional homogeneity in a single melt step is uncertain; and (vi) quantitative element-specific recovery data for this particular configuration are absent from the literature.

Where the literature is silent, this has been noted explicitly. In particular, no published studies were found that specifically evaluate a machined aluminium cup as a sacrificial charge container for small-batch alloy atomization, quantitative recovery percentages for Al-foil-wrapped additions of Zr, Sc, Er, Ce, or Li to aluminium melts, Al₄C₃ formation rate as a quantitative function of temperature and time between 700–1100°C, or the effect of carbide inclusions specifically on Al₃(Sc,Zr,Er) L1₂ precipitation during LPBF.
