A 3-D animation of a design study at BYU's Vertical Cloud Lab, not footage of a working lab: two Kardex vertical lift modules hold the instruments on powered trays, and UR12e arms (the UR10e, renamed in 2025) on MiR250 mobile bases work at their openings and at the floor machines. Panels that aren't measured are badged computed or illustrative.
The digital twin's results are simulated: synthetic truths, simulated instruments, and assumed capacities, prices, lead times and schedules. Its atomizer rates are design targets, not the lab's record. Round 5 is its latest finished round (16 runs an arm, like for like); round 6 is running.

In the real lab (https://github.com/vertical-cloud-lab/digital-alloy-lab): the AMAZEMET rePowder logged 10 runs from 2 Oct to 13 Nov 2026, five of aluminium alloys and five of 630 nickel aluminium bronze. The bronze of 27 and 28 Oct, sieved together, gave 1,015 g under 250 um from 1,938 g charged (DAL PR 286). It hasn't run since 13 Nov, when a run burned the collection cup's seals; the next runs wait on a new cup (DAL #269). The lab's own powder hasn't been printed yet; AlSi10Mg printed at the University of Utah from bought powder gave a yield strength of 228 +/- 13 MPa and a UTS of 376 +/- 10 MPa (DAL PR 288).

An open question, in PR #410 (under review): is the vertical lift worth it? In the twin's own logs the mobile manipulators are 1-2 % busy, so transport doesn't bind; the gain is floor space (29 % less for today's 43 instruments, 51 % at the Shuttles' full 45 trays). https://github.com/vertical-cloud-lab/byu-vcl/pull/410
