Question: Mobile manipulators (a robot arm on an autonomous mobile base) working in chemistry, materials and life-science laboratories and in manufacturing machine tending, for example the Liverpool mobile robotic chemist (Burger et al. 2020, KUKA KMR iiwa), CELISCA Rostock's H20 robots, Fraunhofer's Kevin, Biosero and Thermo Fisher mobile robots, Astech's and others' MiR or Omron base with a UR arm: reported driving speeds and the speed limits set where people share the space, time to dock and localize at a station (touch-off, fiducial or camera calibration) and the positioning accuracy reached, pick-and-place cycle times, success rates and failures per number of operations, uptime, share of time spent charging, and what share of total workflow time is robot transport as opposed to instrument time. Include the requirements of ISO 3691-4, ISO 10218, ISO/TS 15066 and ANSI/RIA R15.08 on speed and separation where they are discussed.

## Mobile Manipulators in Laboratory and Manufacturing Settings: Operational Performance and Safety Standards

### 1. Driving Speeds and Speed Limits

The most extensively documented laboratory mobile manipulator is the Liverpool KUKA KMR iiwa. The Burger et al. (2020) Nature paper reported a maximum robot driving velocity of 0.5 m/s during the photocatalysis campaign (burger2020amobilerobotic pages 4-6). The KUKA KMR iiwa platform specification lists a maximum base speed of 3 km/h (0.83 m/s) (lunt2024aroboticworkflow pages 42-49). In practice, however, accuracy considerations dictate slower operation. Quantitative manufacturing-accuracy tests at 0.08, 0.16, and 0.24 m/s showed that the 0.08–0.16 m/s range provided the best positional accuracy, with displacement errors of approximately 1.6–2.3 mm when feature mapping was enabled. The fastest tested speed (0.24 m/s) produced substantially larger errors, including a 22.4 mm outlier (hifi2025aquantitativeinvestigation pages 10-11). The Brass et al. (2026) mobile robotic process chemist was described as "moving relatively slowly for safety reasons," without a numerical speed value being reported (brass2026amobilerobotic pages 15-19).

For the Fraunhofer IPA Kevin robot, the design employs an omnidirectional Care-O-Bot-derived base with a footprint of approximately 70 × 70 cm, capable of linear movement in all directions and rotation around its center axis (kleinewechelmann2022designingthemobile pages 2-3). No numerical driving speed was reported in the available publications. Similarly, the CELISCA Rostock H20 robots used autonomous multi-floor navigation, but specific speed values were not reported in the located sources (janssen2022labautomationbuildingaa pages 27-31, janssen2022labautomationbuildinga pages 27-31).

### 2. Docking, Localization, and Calibration Times

**Touch-off / tactile calibration.** The original Liverpool KMR iiwa system used a six-point (6P) touch calibration procedure, in which six contacts on perpendicular faces of a calibration cube established the coordinate frame. Each tactile calibration required approximately 53 seconds (cooper2025liralocalizationinspection pages 9-10, zhou2025localizationinspectionand pages 7-8). Tests involving 100 calibrations with simulated initial-position noise of up to ±15 mm and ±2.5° demonstrated the cube was located within 0.12 mm and orientation error was reduced below 0.005° (burger2023amobilerobotic pages 52-56).

**Fiducial / camera calibration (LIRA).** The subsequently developed LIRA (Localization, Inspection, and Reasoning) system reduced each visual calibration to approximately 5 seconds using ArUco fiducial markers—a tenfold improvement over tactile calibration (cooper2025liralocalizationinspection pages 9-10, zhou2025localizationinspectionand pages 7-8). With 11 calibration steps per workflow loop, the LIRA-enhanced arm-manipulation loop required 873 seconds versus 1,367 seconds previously, a 36.1% reduction (zhou2025localizationinspectionand pages 7-8).

**Manufacturing docking.** In the THOMAS mobile manipulator project for manufacturing, dynamic visual docking stabilized to less than 1 cm relative error within approximately 2 seconds of tracking onset. Initial tracking error could reach 4 cm owing to reactive control and actuation delays (outon2019innovativemobilemanipulator pages 18-21). For AMR docking with the FiMa-Reader system, the fiducial-guided approach reduced x-axis positioning spread from 61.9 mm to 8.4 mm (an 86% improvement) and angular spread from 1.9° to 0.30° across 1,500 docking trials (bian2023fimareaderacosteffective pages 10-11).

### 3. Positioning Accuracy at the Station

The KMR iiwa's laser-based navigation returns to taught positions with approximately ±10 mm translational and ±0.5° rotational accuracy—sufficient for avoiding obstacles and reaching the charging floor plate but insufficient for manipulating small laboratory objects (carruthers2025amobilerobotic pages 24-27). Once at a station, the 6P touch calibration achieves 0.12 mm and <0.005° (burger2023amobilerobotic pages 52-56). The LIRA ArUco-based localization achieves 1.51 mm and 1.54° with filtered 20 mm markers, improving to 1.02 mm and 1.01° with 75 mm markers. Cube-based calibration is slightly better at 0.92 mm and 0.90° (cooper2025liralocalizationinspection pages 3-6).

In manufacturing, mapping-enabled KUKA KMR iiwa repeatability was under 7 mm and approximately 2.5 mm in practical scenarios, with feature mapping improving performance by over 90% compared with encoder-only operation (hifi2025aquantitativeinvestigation pages 10-11, hifi2025aquantitativeinvestigation pages 1-3). Without mapping, encoder drift accumulated to 30–177 mm depending on movement type (hifi2025aquantitativeinvestigation pages 10-11). A dual-arm mobile robot using SLAM alone exhibited 50–100 mm displacement, reduced to ≤10 mm after adding AR Tags and a vision system (hifi2025aquantitativeinvestigation pages 3-4).

For chemistry-lab syringe injection, visual servoing with deep-learning needle detection achieved sub-millimeter alignment accuracy and was 11–12× more accurate than AprilTag-only positioning (angelopoulos2023highaccuracyinjectionusing pages 7-8, angelopoulos2023highaccuracyinjectionusing pages 4-6).

### 4. Pick-and-Place Cycle Times

Explicit pick-and-place cycle times are rarely reported as isolated metrics for laboratory mobile manipulators. For the Liverpool PXRD workflow, video timestamp analysis of a 12-step robotic workflow sequence showed the full cycle from sample-rack collection to post-measurement retrieval took approximately 5 minutes 51 seconds, with individual transport steps (e.g., KUKA delivery from Chemspeed area to preparation station) occupying approximately 19–42 seconds each (lunt2023modularmultirobotintegration pages 12-16). Each batch of 16 experiments in the Burger 2020 system required an average of 183 minutes for sample preparation and photolysis, plus 232 minutes for GC analysis (burger2020amobilerobotic pages 4-6). Loading and unloading averaged 28 minutes per batch, with photolysis itself lasting 60 minutes (burger2020amobilerobotic pages 12-20).

### 5. Success Rates and Failures per Number of Operations

The Burger et al. (2020) eight-day autonomous campaign produced 688 experiments totaling over 6,500 individual manipulations. Eleven results were discarded owing to workflow errors or faulty vial sealing, corresponding to a 1.6% discard rate (approximately 3.2 per 200 samples) (burger2020amobilerobotic pages 4-6). Over a subsequent 61-day period with 3,180 measured samples, the monthly error rate declined to approximately 0.5%, or about 1 error per 200 samples (burger2023amobilerobotic pages 184-187). Error-time attribution showed liquid dispensing and cap crimping accounted for 60% of errors, GC 15%, the mobile robot 17%, solid dispensing 6%, and process management 2% (burger2023amobilerobotic pages 184-187).

The LIRA system demonstrated 600 consecutive vial pickup-and-insertion manipulations with a 100% success rate over 8 hours of continuous operation without failure (cooper2025liralocalizationinspection pages 6-9). Its inspection and reasoning module achieved 97.5% accuracy across 200 manual tests (5 failures, primarily involving PXRD plates and NMR racks) (zhou2025localizationinspectionand pages 7-8). The PREVENT safety framework, also tested on the KUKA KMR iiwa, achieved 100% success (60 runs, 20 per skill) for proactive hazard detection, with 90% deployment accuracy for manipulation tasks (veeramani2025preventproactiverisk pages 17-22).

The Angelopoulos et al. (2023) chemistry-lab injection system achieved 21/21 (100%) injection success, compared with 9.5% and 4.8% for AprilTag-only methods (angelopoulos2023highaccuracyinjectionusing pages 4-6). The THOMAS manufacturing mobile manipulator demonstrated 29/30 (96.7%) static docking success, with the single failure attributed to sunlight reflections on the marker (outon2019innovativemobilemanipulator pages 18-21). The CELISCA H20 robots achieved a 91% labware-transport success rate (janssen2022labautomationbuildingaa pages 27-31, janssen2022labautomationbuildinga pages 27-31).

### 6. Uptime and Charging Time Share

The Burger 2020 system was designed to operate up to 21.6 hours per day, with automatic recharging initiated at a 25% battery threshold (burger2020amobilerobotic pages 1-3). During the 8-day search, the robot was charged but idle for approximately 32% of the experiment time, primarily while waiting for the GC analysis (the slowest workflow step) (burger2020amobilerobotic pages 4-6, burger2020amobilerobotic pages 6-12). Over a longer 61-day record, the robot spent 16 days performing manipulation tasks, 17 days idle, and 16.5 days charging or waiting at the charging station, with an overall working-time share of 32.6% and time in error of 4.5% (burger2023amobilerobotic pages 184-187).

For the Brass et al. (2026) mobile robotic process chemist, the robot was actively operating for approximately 20% of the workflow runtime, spending most of the remaining 80% waiting for other operations (synthesis reactions, analysis) to complete (brass2026amobilerobotic pages 15-19).

### 7. Transport Time versus Instrument Time

Separating mobile-robot transport time from instrument processing time proves difficult, as most publications do not report this split explicitly. In the Burger 2023 extended analysis, subsystem error categories attributed 17% to the mobile robot and 81% to instrument operations (dispensing, capping, and GC), but this represents an error-time distribution rather than a direct workflow-time split (burger2023amobilerobotic pages 184-187). During the eight-day search, the robot made 319 moves between stations and travelled 2.17 km (burger2020amobilerobotic pages 6-12). Each batch processed two operations in parallel—sample preparation/photolysis and GC analysis—with the GC being the rate-limiting step and the robot being idle during analysis periods (burger2020amobilerobotic pages 4-6, burger2020amobilerobotic pages 6-12).

For the Brass 2026 process chemistry robot, the robot was active only about 20% of the total workflow runtime, with the remaining 80% dominated by reaction, work-up, and analysis instrument time. The authors noted that this idle fraction could be exploited by having one robot serve multiple reactors simultaneously (brass2026amobilerobotic pages 15-19). In the LIRA-enhanced PXRD workflow, video timestamps showed transport segments totaling approximately 61 seconds within a roughly 6-minute workflow cycle (lunt2023modularmultirobotintegration pages 12-16).

**ISO 10218-1/2 (2011)** defines four collaborative operating modes for industrial robots. Under speed-and-separation monitoring (SSM), the robot must continuously maintain a calculated protective separation distance. The distance accounts for human and robot approach speeds, sensing/response time, stopping distance, and position uncertainty. If the threshold is violated, a protective stop must be initiated (rathmair2021safetyasbad pages 1-3, belzile2022safetyfirston pages 14-17). No single universal speed limit is mandated; the permissible speed is application-specific.

**ISO/TS 15066 (2016)** supplements ISO 10218 with detailed guidance on collaborative operation. For the power-and-force-limiting (PFL) mode, one published implementation calculated the maximum permissible relative contact speed as v_rel,max = √(F_max / (m_red · k)), where m_red = 1/(1/m_h + 1/m_r), yielding body-region-dependent limits ranging from 0.12 m/s for the face to 1.33 m/s for the hands (vitolo2022mobilerobotsand pages 15-17). Because human posture cannot be guaranteed, one conservative assessment adopted the face value of 0.12 m/s as the safety speed limit for a mobile manipulator interface (vitolo2022mobilerobotsand pages 15-17).

**ISO 3691-4 (2020)** governs driverless industrial trucks, including AGVs and AMRs. It is commonly applied to the mobile base during navigation when the arm is static or stowed. It requires risk-based speed control, personnel-detection protective fields, braking performance verification, and emergency stopping, but does not specify a single universal speed cap (roa2021mobilemanipulationhackathon pages 5-6, alexandra2019safetyofmobile pages 3-4).

**ANSI/RIA R15.08 (2020)** is the US standard specifically targeting industrial mobile robots and mobile manipulators. Its three-part structure addresses manufacturers, system integrators, and end users (belzile2025fromsafetystandards pages 2-3). In practice, many integrators apply ISO 10218/ISO TS 15066 when the arm is operating collaboratively and ISO 3691-4 when the mobile base navigates with the arm safely stowed, to prevent conflicts between the governing norms (roa2021mobilemanipulationhackathon pages 5-6).

### 10. Key Observations

Several cross-cutting findings emerge from the literature. First, coarse mobile-base navigation (typically ±10 mm) is universally insufficient for laboratory manipulation tasks requiring sub-millimeter precision. All successful systems employ a two-stage approach: coarse navigation followed by station-level calibration using either tactile touch-off or fiducial-marker-based visual localization (carruthers2025amobilerobotic pages 24-27, burger2023amobilerobotic pages 52-56, cooper2025liralocalizationinspection pages 3-6). Second, the transition from tactile to vision-based calibration (e.g., LIRA) reduces per-station localization time by roughly an order of magnitude (from ~53 s to ~5 s), with only modest accuracy trade-offs (from 0.12 mm to approximately 1 mm) (cooper2025liralocalizationinspection pages 9-10, zhou2025localizationinspectionand pages 7-8). Third, the mobile robot is typically idle or charging for a large fraction of the workflow (60–80% of total time), because instrument processing—particularly analysis—dominates the workflow duration (burger2023amobilerobotic pages 184-187, brass2026amobilerobotic pages 15-19). This creates opportunities for a single mobile manipulator to service multiple instruments or reactors in parallel (brass2026amobilerobotic pages 15-19). Fourth, error rates for mature systems converge toward approximately 0.5% per sample (1 in 200), with the mobile robot itself contributing roughly one-sixth of total errors, the remainder being attributable to dispensing, capping, and analytical instruments (burger2023amobilerobotic pages 184-187). Fifth, commercial systems such as the CELISCA H20 robots, Fraunhofer Kevin, and configurations using MiR/Omron bases with UR arms have significantly fewer published quantitative performance benchmarks compared with research platforms, reflecting both the proprietary nature of commercial deployments and the limited peer-reviewed literature addressing these specific system configurations (janssen2022labautomationbuildingaa pages 27-31, kleinewechelmann2022designingthemobile pages 2-3).
