Question: How have metallographic sample preparation steps (sectioning, hot compression mounting, grinding, polishing) been automated with robots or fully automatic systems for metal alloys, e.g. RoboMet.3D, Struers/Buehler/LECO automatic systems, self-driving labs? Specifically: how are grinding papers and polishing cloths exchanged automatically (magnetic or adhesive-backed discs, retaining rings, disc changers), how is polish quality checked in-line, how are samples held (single-specimen vs central-force holders), and what failure modes and throughputs are reported? Cite specific systems and papers.

# Automation of metallographic preparation for metal alloys

**The distinction is between automating a preparation *recipe* and automating the entire specimen workflow.** Commercial polishers can control force, speed, time and abrasive dosing while still requiring an operator to cut and mount samples, load holders, or replace worn paper. Robotic serial-sectioning cells additionally move one specimen among polishing, cleaning and imaging stations. The clearest documented automatic *abrasive-disc exchange* in the sources examined is the QATM QPOL 250 Bot in BAM’s RASI cell; it changes preloaded discs, rather than peeling and applying a new sheet of paper or cloth. (lemiasheuski2023developmentofan pages 7-10, madison2017acquisitionofrealtime pages 1-4, gerhatova2024analysisofbiocompatible pages 6-11)

## Where automation begins and ends

**Sectioning and mounting.** A published stainless-steel workflow uses a Buehler IsoMet 5000 precision saw for cooled sectioning, a separate hot press for resin mounting, and an EcoMet/AutoMet 250 automatic head for grinding and polishing. It does not report robotic transfer between these machines. A brass-alloy study likewise reports Struers Secotom-20 automatic cutting followed by separate mounting and preparation equipment. Thus, an *automatic saw* does not establish a fully automatic cutting-to-microscopy line. (gerhatova2024analysisofbiocompatible pages 3-6, knaislova2025analysisofthe pages 2-4) RoboMet.3D and BAM RASI perform repeated destructive **serial sectioning by grinding/polishing a specimen that has already been mounted**; neither cited implementation demonstrates automatically cutting a bulk alloy and hot-mounting it before loading. RASI accepts either cold- or hot-mounted 30-mm specimens. (madison2017acquisitionofrealtime pages 1-4, lemiasheuski2023developmentofan pages 7-10)

An explicit route toward automated hot compression mounting is ATM Qness’s patent **US 12,263,548 B2**: an operator loads a rotary table with loose specimens; the proposed system indexes each to a heated press, meters mounting granules, closes and presses the mold, ejects the cylindrical mount, and conveys it to a grinder/polisher. The patent contrasts this with conventional presses that automatically execute heating/cooling but still require manual loading and unloading. Its approximately **15 min per mount, or 32 mounts in eight hours**, is a design example—not a measured production result or evidence that this complete line has been deployed. (US12263548B2 pages 1-4, US12263548B2 pages 4-7, US12263548B2 pages 7-9, US12263548B2 pages 17-19)

**Robotic serial sections.** Sandia’s RoboMet.3D carries one mounted specimen between a motorized polishing station, ultrasonic cleaning baths and an inverted optical microscope; its repeated polish–image cycles yield a three-dimensional image stack. BAM’s independently developed **RASI** couples a QPOL 250 Bot, UR5e robot, cleaning/etching station and Zeiss Axio Observer microscope. In the cited RASI work, interchangeable station fixtures locate the grooved specimen carrier, while a three-jaw chuck clamps it rigidly for central-pressure grinding. The published photographs show the cell and its single-mount carrier. (madison2017acquisitionofrealtime pages 1-4, lemiasheuski2023developmentofan pages 7-10, lemiasheuski2023developmentofan media 3cef1a4a, lemiasheuski2023developmentofan media 50804529)

## What actually happens to papers, cloths and holders?

There are **three different ways to advance from coarse grinding to fine polishing**, which should not be conflated:

1. **Change the complete prepared disc.** RASI’s QPOL 250 Bot has a magazine for **16 preloaded, 250-mm grinding/polishing discs** and automatically selects discs in a programmable sequence; six fluid reservoirs supply agents through a nozzle. This verifies machine exchange of prepared discs, *not* automatic replacement of the worn SiC sheet or cloth attached to an individual disc, and its paper-to-disc attachment method is not specified in the paper. A Qness patent also describes, as background, a 16-disc automatic changer on another grinder/polisher. (lemiasheuski2023developmentofan pages 7-10, US12263548B2 pages 1-4)
2. **Keep separate media at separate stations.** The *proposed* Qness single-mount line moves its specimen-gripping head among grinding and polishing platens already equipped with their respective grits or cloths. Consequently, **no disc exchange is needed between recipe steps**; a worn disc would still require replacement. In multi-holder systems discussed in the same patent, a magazine can instead exchange whole specimen holders, but individual specimens must first be clamped and aligned by hand. (US12263548B2 pages 12-14, US12263548B2 pages 14-17, US12263548B2 pages 1-4)
3. **Attach and periodically replace media at the platen.** Vander Voort’s Buehler-assigned **US 6,224,474 B1** describes peripheral retaining rings, pressure-sensitive-adhesive-backed (PSA) papers or cloths, and polishing cloth or SiC paper attached to removable steel discs held on a magnetic platen. Its proposed magnetic barrier reduces the field reaching the working surface. These are *attachment and quick-change technologies*, **not evidence that an AutoMet or RoboMet robot autonomously peels PSA or changes a retaining ring**. The patent warns of paper slippage or ring contact, adhesive that detaches too easily or is difficult to remove, cloth damage, and magnetically trapped ferrous swarf scratching the specimen. Buehler’s technical note adds a practical consumable bottleneck: SiC paper in its fully automatic VANGUARD preparation system should be **changed after each holder**; longer-lived bonded-diamond discs can reduce that intervention. (US6224474B1 pages 1-5, US6224474B1 pages 5-6, US6224474B1 pages 6-7, blannUnknownyearcontemporaryconceptsina pages 1-2)

**Holding geometry matters.** RoboMet.3D and RASI transport **one mounted specimen at a time**; RASI applies central pressure to that *one rigidly clamped carrier*. That is distinct from a **central-force multi-specimen holder**, where several mounts are clamped flush in one holder and pressed together. In **individual-force** heads, each specimen instead receives its own pneumatic or spring-loaded pressure, permitting differing specimen heights but not necessarily equal planarity. The Qness patent explicitly distinguishes these mechanisms and proposes gripping each finished cylindrical hot mount directly with radial fingers to avoid manually loading a multi-specimen holder. A Buehler technical note documents several hard-metal specimens prepared together in one central-force EcoMet/AutoMet holder; changing to a mixed-hardness holder produced pitting in the softer brass despite satisfactory flatness. (madison2017acquisitionofrealtime pages 1-4, lemiasheuski2023developmentofan pages 7-10, US12263548B2 pages 1-4, US12263548B2 pages 12-14, blannUnknownyearcontemporaryconceptsina pages 2-3)

**Manufacturer-specific evidence is narrower than the word “automatic.”** Published alloy studies identify Struers Tegramin 30 as an automatic grinder/polisher, Buehler AutoMet 250 as an automatic preparation head, and LECO GPX-300 as an automatic final polisher. In the cited LECO application, sectioning and 240–600-grit grinding preceded GPX-300 polishing on *other* equipment; the Buehler application inspected the surface by light microscope between SiC grits. None of these reports establishes automatic paper/cloth exchange, and the retrieved sources do **not** verify a mechanism or throughput for a **Struers MAPS** disc changer. Product-level claims about those functions would require a model-specific manual or test, rather than inference from “automatic polisher.” (mouralova2019theinfluenceof pages 5-9, gerhatova2024analysisofbiocompatible pages 6-11, barzi2015synthesisofsuperconducting pages 4-5)

## In-line checks, failures and rates

**Depth control is not the same as polish-quality inspection.** RoboMet.3D estimates the thickness removed per slice from differences in the microscope’s autofocus heights. Madison and colleagues additionally integrated a non-contact Keyence laser height sensor referenced to the holder and a monitor that flags anomalous removal, machine errors and pauses. They documented an autofocus failure mode: if the imaged specimen’s cross-sectional area changes, the estimated removal can even become *negative*. Oakley and colleagues subsequently linked a run-to-run model-predictive controller to RoboMet.3D’s PLC: it adjusts the next slice’s polishing times from measured removal, while constraining the fine-polish time to preserve an imaging-quality finish. **Neither height measurement alone proves an automated scratch-free or EBSD-ready surface check.** (madison2017acquisitionofrealtime pages 1-4, madison2017acquisitionofrealtime pages 4-11, oakley2025arobustdatadriven pages 3-7, oakley2025arobustdatadriven pages 7-11, oakley2025arobustdatadriven pages 11-14)

A direct example of **online surface-finish measurement**, though **not a mounted metallographic-sectioning system**, is Lee, Huang and Shaw’s 2025 robotic finishing experiment on curved 304 stainless steel. A robot-mounted microscope collected images after polishing; a CNN predicted areal roughness, *Sa*, against independently measured 3-D topography. Across 400 collected images, its validation mean absolute error was **0.081 µm**. The study reports cloth-wheel burning and surface oxidation when contact force exceeded **15 N** and used **5 N** force feedback instead. This illustrates a feasible optical inspection loop, not a capability demonstrated for the cited RoboMet, RASI, Struers, Buehler or LECO cells. (lee2025integrationofrobotic pages 1-3, lee2025integrationofrobotic pages 3-5, lee2025integrationofrobotic pages 5-8)

Reported limitations include pad wear, settling abrasive fluid, changing material hardness or exposed area, polish scratches and edge rounding, consumable contamination, and changing etch response. With an unchanged RoboMet recipe, only **25.5% of 145 historical slices** fell within ±1.5 µm of a 10-µm removal target. Oakley’s controller tracked **8 µm per slice over 950 slices** of a copper–steel braze, but required about **30 slices to converge**. In a separate capacitor comparison, an open-loop run produced about **100 redundant slices**; at the authors’ conservative **≈40 min per slice**, that represented nearly **67 hours** of unnecessary machine time. These are slice and data-efficiency results, not alloy specimens per hour. (oakley2025arobustdatadriven pages 3-7, oakley2025arobustdatadriven pages 11-14) BAM RASI reports **4–10 good-resolution images/section planes per hour**, material- and depth-step-dependent; obtaining suitable recipes could first require **one to two working days**. Poorly chosen force or polishing duration produced visibly poor sections, while consistent etching remained under development. (lemiasheuski2023developmentofan pages 10-12, lemiasheuski2023developmentofan pages 12-14)

Finally, **self-driving alloy characterization need not imply self-driving metallographic preparation**. Vecchio and colleagues’ HT-READ study printed a ring of **16 Inconel-625-derived alloys**, cut and resin-mounted it as one 32-mm library, and ground/polished its faces together; the authors explicitly describe that preparation as **semi-automated**. Rotation of the prepared ring subsequently enabled automated SEM-EDS/EBSD of all **16 alloy positions in under an hour**. That figure is *microscopy throughput*, not a demonstrated rate for robotic sectioning, mounting, or exchanging polishing cloths. (vecchio2021highthroughputrapidexperimental pages 7-11, vecchio2021highthroughputrapidexperimental pages 11-13)
