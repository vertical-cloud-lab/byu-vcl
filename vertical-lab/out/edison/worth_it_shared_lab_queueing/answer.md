Question: Queueing and scheduling in shared automated laboratories and in flexible job shops with transport robots: how waiting time and throughput depend on the number of transport robots or AGVs, on a single shared transfer point, and on instruments that block for hours; effect of adding external or multi-user load to an automated lab or cloud lab (utilization against waiting, scheduling policies, priority between internal and external work); evidence on whether transport or instrument time is the bottleneck in laboratory automation, and published rules for sizing the number of mobile robots.

# Queueing and capacity in shared automated laboratories

**The bottleneck is workload-dependent, not intrinsically “the robot.”** Too few transport vehicles can leave instruments waiting for samples; once transport demand is met, adding vehicles produces diminishing returns and can worsen congestion at a shared loading point. Conversely, a long operation that occupies a scarce instrument can determine laboratory throughput even when transfers are fast. The most useful design question is therefore which resource—vehicle, transfer point, instrument, incubator, or operator—accumulates a queue *under the expected mix and timing of work*. Laboratory scheduling studies and AGV simulations support these mechanisms, but their numerical results should not be treated as measurements of one common laboratory. (foit2020integrationandevaluation pages 8-10, itoh2021optimalschedulingfor pages 5-8, ribino2018agentbasedsimulationstudy pages 15-18, croxatto2016laboratoryautomationin pages 16-17)

## Vehicle count, waiting, and the shared transfer point

In a job-shop simulation, Kesen and Baykoç compared matched settings with **two versus three AGVs**: completed jobs increased from **174.6 to 230.6**, while the mean number of jobs *waiting for an AGV* fell from **6.736 to 1.05**. This is evidence of transport scarcity at that operating point, not a universal percentage improvement in end-to-end job time; other experimental factors, including demand and kanban count, interacted with fleet size. In Foit and colleagues’ separate simulation, output rose from **183.66 pieces/24 h with one AGV** to **439.8 with five**, but reached only **443.6 with eight**. Their initial experiment omitted vehicle charging and failures, and its zero-AGV reference assumes instantaneous transport rather than a workable fleet. (kesen2007simulationofautomated pages 8-10, foit2020integrationandevaluation pages 8-10)

A **single shared transfer point is its own capacity-limited server**, even if several robots can travel simultaneously. Berman and colleagues describe a two-AGV laboratory demonstrator in which both robots must share a load port, but do not report a throughput-versus-fleet experiment for that port. More directly, Ribino and colleagues simulated warehouse gates and sorter positions that each admit only one AGV: occupying a station delayed unloading, diverted vehicles onto longer paths, and blocked traffic. Their layout reached **50 pallets/h with eight AGVs**, **52 with nine**, and a maximum of **59 with 13**; more vehicles did not improve that maximum. This is strong evidence for the *mechanism*, not a measured ceiling for a laboratory with exactly one port. In a related shared picking-bay model, serving the maximum recorded demand with six AGVs and one heavily loaded picker entailed almost **150 seconds of AGV queueing**; five AGVs and two pickers were an alternative. Increasing vehicles cannot remove a loading or instrument service limit. (berman2009evaluationofautomatic pages 3-4, ribino2018agentbasedsimulationstudy pages 15-18, lolli2022orderpickingsystems pages 11-12)

## Instruments that occupy capacity for hours

Distinguish **elapsed assay time** from **exclusive occupancy**. If an operation holds the only compatible instrument for hours, its approximate maximum completion rate is one operation per holding time, absent batching; an extra transporter does not shorten that hold. If a sample instead incubates in a separately capacitated incubator, the loading instrument may serve another job, although the incubator and its later retrieval/reading steps can become bottlenecks. Clinical-bacteriology automation explicitly routes inoculated plates to incubation and retrieves them later; published laboratory-planning guidance calls for sizing specimen processors *and* incubators against peak hourly arrivals. A reported bacterial-cultivation workflow took **more than 20 hours** to reach an induction threshold, but its paper does **not** establish that a robot or a particular instrument was occupied for those 20 hours. It would be incorrect to count biological elapsed time automatically as robot demand or single-instrument occupancy. (croxatto2016laboratoryautomationin pages 8-9, croxatto2016laboratoryautomationin pages 5-7, croxatto2016laboratoryautomationin pages 16-17, spannenkrebs2025closingtheloop pages 8-11)

There is direct laboratory evidence for an **instrument-side** constraint. Itoh and colleagues modeled three jobs containing a **60-minute operation on Instrument C**: completion time fell from **122 minutes with one C** to **91 with two** and **82 with three**, remaining **82 with four**. These are fixed-workload scheduling simulations, not steady-state jobs-per-hour measurements; the fourth copy ceased helping because other steps became limiting. In a separate multi-task self-driving-lab study, a heating/stirring operation was encoded as **25 minutes of station processing plus 5 minutes of robotic transfer**, and a UV–Vis operation as **9 plus 2 minutes**. Those examples show how to account for both demands, but do not prove that instruments dominate in every lab. A cloud-hosted synthesis robot with six reactors similarly reports that shared liquid-handling and analytical resources can serialize work despite available reactor vessels. (itoh2021optimalschedulingfor pages 5-8, zhou2026multitaskschedulingof pages 8-9, manica2026lessonslearnedfrom pages 17-21)

## External users, utilization, and scheduling policy

Adding external orders increases the arrival rate and can improve utilization when capacity would otherwise be idle. Once orders compete for the same constrained module, higher utilization comes with longer queues and potentially little additional **completed** throughput. In the elementary *single-server, Poisson-arrival/exponential-service, FCFS* benchmark, expected queue wait is **Wq = ρ/(μ−λ)**, with ρ = λ/μ: it rises sharply as total arrival rate λ approaches service rate μ. This is an explanatory benchmark, **not a fitted formula for a cloud lab**; multi-step protocols, varying operation times, finite buffers, priorities and time-window constraints require richer models or simulation. AGV fleet models likewise size vehicles against an explicit target wait rather than merely maximizing utilization, while clinical-laboratory guidance warns that hourly demand above processing throughput causes delay. (kovtun2025decentralizedqueuecontrol pages 8-9, ganesharajah1998designandoperational pages 13-16, croxatto2016laboratoryautomationin pages 16-17)

**Policy determines whose waiting grows.** In the multi-user OCTOPUS laboratory, FCFS gives earlier submissions first access to overlapping modules; later jobs can wait while an earlier job has reserved work in progress. Parallelizing compatible operations during device standby and packing batches into free module capacity reduced reported waits for particular jobs from **8.92 to 0.63 hours**, **10.21 to 0.72 hours**, and **10.97 to zero hours**. Its scheduler also checks shared-arm conflicts before allowing simultaneous tasks: nominally free instruments do not make an unsafe transfer feasible. The study demonstrates an alternative to blanket serialization, **not** a measured comparison of internal versus paying external customers. (yoo2024octopusoperationcontrol pages 5-7, yoo2024octopusoperationcontrol pages 7-9, yoo2024octopusoperationcontrol pages 2-3)

For an institution considering external cloud-lab work, the defensible policy options are to **reserve capacity or set admission limits for internal work**, give urgent or scientifically time-critical steps explicit priority, and allow lower-priority external jobs into otherwise idle capacity. These are design implications of the documented resource conflicts and time-window constraints, **not empirically validated internal-versus-external priority rules from the cited lab studies**. Nonpreemptive priority also cannot interrupt an hours-long run already occupying an instrument; retaining capacity for urgent work or controlling when long runs start matters more than relabeling a queue. Schedule performance should be reported separately by user class—submission-to-start wait, turnaround, deadline compliance and instrument utilization—rather than as one pooled utilization figure. (yoo2024octopusoperationcontrol pages 5-7, itoh2021optimalschedulingfor pages 5-8, zhou2026multitaskschedulingof pages 8-9, tsai2019acriticalreview pages 7-8)

## Published robot-sizing rules and a practical test

A published **first-pass lower bound** is the total required vehicle work over a planning period divided by usable vehicle time: estimate loaded trips from the workflow’s origin–destination flows; add dispatch-dependent **empty travel**, loading/unloading and handling; then account for availability. Ganesharajah and colleagues emphasize that this is only a starting point because routing conflicts and pickup/drop-off buffer sizes change actual delays. In their reviewed stochastic approach, expected trip service time comprises loaded travel, empty travel, loading and unloading, vehicle utilization is **ρ = ΛE(s)/c** for request rate Λ and **c** AGVs, and **c** is chosen to satisfy management’s desired transport wait. A later uncertainty-aware calculation explicitly includes vehicle availability and a traffic factor: changing assumed empty-trip length from **795 m to 100 m** changed its estimated mean fleet from **11.191 to 3.250**. These are example-specific estimates, not fleet prescriptions for laboratories. (ganesharajah1998designandoperational pages 10-13, ganesharajah1998designandoperational pages 13-16, balseraltanis2025improvingfleetsize pages 16-19)

For a **shared gate or sorter**, Ribino and colleagues also derive a no-queue screening condition: the vehicle round-trip cycle divided by the number assigned to that station must not fall below the longer of gate- and sorter-occupancy times. Their case yielded approximately **9.25 minutes of cycle time versus 5 minutes of station service**, illustrating how a transfer station can limit useful vehicles per route; traffic and cost still require simulation. A separate flexible-cell study found little further completion-time benefit beyond an AGV count of about **one-fifth of its workpiece count**, but that is an *instance-dependent observation*, not a general “one robot per five experiments” rule. (ribino2018agentbasedsimulationstudy pages 15-18, li2022multiagvflexiblemanufacturing pages 10-11)

**Recommended sizing procedure:** calculate vehicle work and each instrument/transfer point’s service demand at the *combined internal-plus-external peak*, explicitly separate passive incubation from exclusive equipment occupation, and simulate adjacent fleet sizes with actual queues, port access, charging and scheduling priorities. Choose the smallest fleet meeting specified **per-class waiting/deadline targets** at an acceptable cost; if adding the next robot barely changes completions while a port or instrument queue persists, expand or reschedule that constrained resource instead. This procedure follows the published analytical-lower-bound-plus-simulation approach, but the available evidence does not supply a universal laboratory robot number or a direct experiment contrasting multiple robot counts with an instrument blocked for several hours. (yifei2010anestimateand pages 1-3, ganesharajah1998designandoperational pages 10-13, foit2020integrationandevaluation pages 8-10, itoh2021optimalschedulingfor pages 5-8, ribino2018agentbasedsimulationstudy pages 15-18)
