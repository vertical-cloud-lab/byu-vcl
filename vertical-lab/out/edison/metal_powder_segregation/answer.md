# Preventing cross-alloy contamination in metal-powder facilities

**The most reliable control is segregation, not reliance on a final cleaning step.** A powder’s identity must remain traceable through atomization, sieving, storage, loading, recovery and reuse. For critical hardware, dedicate the printer or powder-contact train to one alloy where practicable: Brandão *et al.* recommended one powder type per AM machine after finding tungsten in Ti-6Al-4V parts made on equipment previously used for tungsten. A study of AlSi10Mg mirrors likewise recommended dedicated printers after identifying titanium-alloy particles associated with powder exchanges. These are evidence-based recommendations, **not a rule that every laboratory must own a complete machine for every alloy**. (brandao2017challengesinadditive pages 12-15, atkins2026mirrorfabricationusing pages 1-3, atkins2026mirrorfabricationusing pages 15-17)

## Equipment allocation and identification

A practical facility rule is to assign **one identified powder-contact kit per alloy, or at minimum per compatible alloy family**. Include sieve mesh, frame, lid and collection pan; sealed powder containers and liners; scoops, funnels and brushes; vacuum nozzle, hose, filter and collection vessel; and removable printer or DED-feeder components. Keep returned powder with its original alloy and documented lot/reuse history, rather than treating a successful sieve pass as evidence of chemical purity. For dissimilar families such as Al, Cu, Ni/steel and Ti, dedicated contact parts are preferable because residual particles can survive in mesh joints, hoses, feed lines and machine recesses. Powder from other batches, machines and recycling equipment is explicitly identified as a contamination risk in process-qualification literature. A NIOSH field investigation found vacuums dedicated to particular printer/feedstock combinations, yet detected unexpected metals on their nozzles—evidence that dedication alone does not eliminate transfer by personnel or the surrounding facility. (calignano2019ametalpowder pages 7-9, stefaniak2025healthhazardevaluation pages 29-31, stefaniak2026skinclothingand pages 16-18, gibbons2024metalpowderfeedstock pages 22-23)

**Label the kit as well as colour-code it.** For example, a locally chosen Al/Cu/Ni/Ti colour scheme can make a mismatch conspicuous, but each item should also carry an unambiguous alloy or approved-family designation, equipment ID and status such as *clean*, *in use* or *quarantined*. Containers additionally need powder identity, lot, receipt and reuse records; apply the same identification to removable sieve parts, vacuum attachments and stored changeover parts. **This is a recommended visual-management design, not a specified ISO/ASTM colour scheme:** the reviewed sources document powder-specific vacuums and the need for SOPs and traceability, but do not establish prescribed colours for scoops, sieves or brushes. Gloves, carts and workwear also need controlled movement: NIOSH recommended changing nitrile gloves between printers, sieve stations, rooms and tasks, and restricting movement of a DED-room vacuum. (stefaniak2025healthhazardevaluation pages 13-16, gibbons2024metalpowderfeedstock pages 22-23, stefaniak2025healthhazardevaluation pages 29-31)

For **laser powder-bed fusion (LPBF)**, treat the build chamber, recoater, feed and overflow hoppers, powder-recovery path, sieving station and vacuum train as one changeover boundary. For **powder-fed directed energy deposition (DED)**, include the bucket-loading station, feeder hopper, metering components, hoses, splitter and nozzle, plus the enclosure and part-cleaning implements. In the investigated LPBF/DED facility, newer printers used enclosed pneumatic transport and separate sieve stations, while powder-specific vacuums, an enclosed depowdering glove box and DED cleaning operations were observed; investigators nevertheless found evidence of metal migration beyond powder-handling rooms. For **conventional powder metallurgy**, apply the same allocation principle to blending vessels, die cavities and punches, ejectors, lubricant applicators and collection trays. The specific proposed die-cleaning and verification steps below are engineering extrapolations of the documented powder-contact pathways, **not a claim that an AM standard prescribes PM-die changeover**. (stefaniak2026skinclothingand pages 4-5, stefaniak2025healthhazardevaluation pages 23-29, stefaniak2026skinclothingand pages 16-18, calignano2019ametalpowder pages 7-9)

For **gas or ultrasonic atomization**, control the *upstream melt-contact and downstream powder-contact paths*: crucible or melt vessel, nozzle/sonotrode as applicable, chamber deposits, cyclone or collector, transfer piping and classification sieves. Changing only the collection container is not sufficient. In an investigated atomization incident, a NiCrCuP alloy processed before 316L left a fine contaminant embedded *inside* subsequent 316L powder particles; Balart, Hao and Davis traced the likely carryover beyond the collection chamber to the melting vessel or nozzle and recommended not atomizing NiCrCuP immediately before 316L. If these parts cannot be demonstrably cleaned, replace the contact parts, segregate production campaigns or qualify the sequence and its first-run powder separately. (balart2022automatedsemedsanalysis pages 10-12, balart2022automatedsemedsanalysis pages 1-2)

## Documented changeover and proof of cleanliness

A defensible **facility SOP**, adapted to the alloy and equipment rather than asserted as a universal standard clause, is:

1. **Close and reconcile the old lot.** Seal and identify recoverable powder; quarantine material whose identity is uncertain. Record the machine, tool-kit and vacuum IDs, previous and next alloys, recovered quantity and operator. Do not combine residues from two alloys into a reusable lot. Powder-quality guidance calls for control and traceability across receipt, handling, reuse and disposal. (gomes2023analysisanddevelopmentb pages 38-42, gibbons2024metalpowderfeedstock pages 22-23)
2. **Remove retained powder before wiping.** Empty and disassemble accessible hoppers, sieves, ducts, feeder lines, chamber inserts and—for PM—die/punch assemblies. Use an appropriately selected, powder-dedicated metal-dust collection system and inspect seams, mesh rims, threads, blind pockets and exhaust/collection paths. Replace inexpensive porous or difficult-to-clean contact items where cleaning cannot be demonstrated. Residual material in AM equipment and powder-recycling equipment is a documented route for cross-contamination. (montazeri2018inprocessmonitoringof pages 2-3, calignano2019ametalpowder pages 7-9, brandao2017challengesinadditive pages 12-15)
3. **Clean without redistributing or reacting with the powder.** Follow the equipment maker’s instructions, alloy safety-data sheet and site fire-risk assessment for wipes, approved solvents, static control, dust capture and waste. An investigated facility cleaned printer interiors and exteriors with paper towels and isopropyl alcohol; NIOSH separately recommended frequent wet cleaning of appropriate facility surfaces and PPE to reduce *migration*. Neither observation makes water or a solvent appropriate for every reactive powder or for an uncleared chamber. Assess combustible-metal, moisture and ignition hazards before wet cleaning, blowing out equipment or choosing a vacuum. (stefaniak2025healthhazardevaluation pages 16-23, stefaniak2025healthhazardevaluation pages 9-13, gomes2023analysisanddevelopmentd pages 38-42, bruceUnknownyeartechnicalcommitteeon pages 1-7)
4. **Inspect and verify before release.** Record visual inspection and sample the hardest-to-clean sites with a defined-area wipe or other suitable collection method; analyse the wipe for markers of the *previous* alloy. Where risk warrants it, examine a representative first-run or witness powder by automated SEM/EDS, including polished powder cross-sections when embedded contaminants are plausible. Compare with an uncontaminated baseline, procedural blanks, the applicable incoming-powder specification and an **alloy- and product-specific** foreign-particle acceptance criterion. Quarantine the first new-alloy output until released; investigate and reclean or replace parts after a failure. This validation sequence is a proposed risk-based protocol, **not a published universal swab limit**. NIOSH demonstrated quantitative elemental surface wipes and qualitative SEM/EDX particle sampling; Balart *et al.* demonstrated why surface-only SEM can miss embedded carryover. (stefaniak2026skinclothingand pages 5-7, stefaniak2026skinclothingand pages 16-18, balart2022automatedsemedsanalysis pages 1-2, gibbons2024metalpowderfeedstock pages 22-23)

A clean-looking sieve or powder sample is not an analytical clearance. Sieving controls size and removes some oversize debris, but a foreign particle in the same size range as the product passes with it. **Automated backscattered-electron SEM/EDS** can locate and chemically classify individual dissimilar particles; map multiple, representative powder subsamples and, where appropriate, polished cross-sections. Santecchia *et al.* deliberately mixed Ti-6Al-4V and maraging steel at **0.5, 1 and 2.5 wt.%** and used SEM/BSE images and EDS maps to identify and quantify cross-particles; they found limitations in large-area EDS quantification. Bulk elemental assays are complementary, not substitutes for a rare-particle search: in Brandão *et al.*, bulk ICP-OES reported tungsten below **0.005 wt.%**, while X-ray CT revealed discrete W inclusions in powder and parts and SEM/EDS identified them. In-process optical monitoring and CT of finished parts can provide additional checks, but do not retroactively establish that a recovered powder lot is uncontaminated. (santecchia2019crosscontaminationquantificationin pages 3-6, santecchia2019crosscontaminationquantificationin pages 6-9, brandao2017challengesinadditive pages 12-15, montazeri2018inprocessmonitoringof pages 2-3)

The following studies distinguish **observed production contamination** from **deliberately mixed experimental powders**. In particular, Brandão *et al.* measured **0.03–0.04 vol.%** high-density inclusions in examined contaminated specimens; vertically built contaminated specimens fractured at approximately **4% elongation**, versus **more than 10%** for uncontaminated counterparts. Balart *et al.* reported microcracking in **two of three** AM 316L samples with local Ni–Cu–P enrichment, without establishing a tensile-property decrement. Horn *et al.* found that CuCr1Zr additions to AlSi10Mg produced brittle Al₂Cu-rich regions and generally reduced elongation; their separate copper-in-maraging-steel experiment found cracks particularly at higher deliberately introduced particle fractions. Atkins *et al.* used SEM/EDS of scratch-initiating inclusions in AlSi10Mg mirrors to distinguish Ti6Al4V associated with printer powder changes from Ti6Al4V/316L associated with the production environment. These results explain why acceptable *bulk* chemistry, normal sieve flow and apparently clean equipment cannot alone establish particulate cleanliness. (brandao2017challengesinadditive pages 6-9, balart2022automatedsemedsanalysis pages 1-2, horn2019metalpowdercrosscontaminations pages 5-7, horn2020influenceofmetal pages 2-3, atkins2026mirrorfabricationusing pages 1-3)

## Where the named standards fit

- **ASTM F3049/F3049M**, *Standard Guide for Characterizing Properties of Metal Powders Used for Additive Manufacturing Processes*, provides a powder-characterization framework: relevant characteristics include size distribution, morphology, composition, flow and density. It is a **guide**, not a demonstrated prescription for tool colours or an alloy-changeover cleaning limit. Brandão *et al.* cite it while showing that conventional feedstock characterization missed sparse tungsten particles. (brandao2017challengesinadditive pages 12-15)
- **ISO/ASTM 52907:2019**, *Additive manufacturing—Feedstock materials—Methods to characterize metal powders*, addresses measurements including particle-size distribution, chemical composition, morphology, flowability and characteristic densities. Apply its characterization methods to incoming, suspect or reused powder; it does not, on the evidence reviewed, supply a single foreign-particle tolerance or a complete chamber-cleaning SOP. (gomes2023analysisanddevelopment pages 38-42, gomes2023analysisanddevelopmentb pages 38-42)
- **ISO/ASTM 52931:2023** addresses *environment, health and safety—general principles for use of metallic materials* in metal AM. Use it with the powder SDS and the facility hazard assessment for handling and cleaning safety; do not mistake EHS guidance for proof that two alloy lots are chemically segregated. (gomes2023analysisanddevelopment pages 38-42, gomes2023analysisanddevelopmentd pages 38-42)
- **NASA MSFC-SPEC-3717**, *Specification for Control and Qualification of Laser Powder Bed Fusion Metallurgical Processes*, concerns control and qualification of the **LPBF metallurgical process** for NASA hardware. It supports treating feedstock and changeover as controlled elements of a qualified process, but the material retrieved here does **not** establish a particular mandatory cleaning method, colour code, PM-die requirement or numeric particle limit under that specification. (morgan2017additivemanufacturingoverview pages 19-29)
- **NFPA 484**, *Standard for Combustible Metals*, is relevant to the fire/explosion side of metal-powder storage, housekeeping, dust collection and vacuum selection, especially for reactive or combustible fines. The NFPA committee material examined identifies separate attention to **additive manufacturing** and **portable vacuum cleaners**, including their operational and design aspects; it is committee/draft material, **not a verified quotation of a current edition’s enforceable vacuum clause**. Verify the locally adopted edition and the equipment’s suitability for the particular powder before using a shared vacuum or a wet-cleaning method. Contamination prevention and combustible-dust safety must both be satisfied; HEPA filtration alone is not a substitute for that hazard assessment. (bruceUnknownyeartechnicalcommitteeon pages 1-7, bruceUnknownyeartechnicalcommitteeon pages 22-26, gomes2023analysisanddevelopmentd pages 38-42)

**Bottom line:** dedicate or uniquely identify powder-contact equipment first; if a machine or contact part must change alloys, use a recorded, alloy-specific disassembly and cleaning procedure, then demonstrate clearance with targeted surface and *particle-sensitive powder* testing before releasing the next lot. The published cases show that the critical carryover may be sparse, embedded inside atomized particles, trapped in machine blind spots or transported between otherwise dedicated stations. The cited standards establish characterization, process-control and safety frameworks; site-specific cleanliness limits and a validated changeover procedure still have to be set for the part’s risk and alloy combination. (brandao2017challengesinadditive pages 12-15, balart2022automatedsemedsanalysis pages 10-12, atkins2026mirrorfabricationusing pages 15-17, stefaniak2025healthhazardevaluation pages 29-31, gomes2023analysisanddevelopmentb pages 38-42)
