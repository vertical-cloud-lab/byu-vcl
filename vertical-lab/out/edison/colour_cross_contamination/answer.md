# Colour-coded segregation and cross-contamination: evidence review

**Bottom line.** Assigning tools, containers and cleaning equipment to defined zones or materials is a well-supported *control principle*: it makes an otherwise invisible boundary easier to follow and audit. But the evidence that **colour itself**, rather than physical segregation, effective cleaning, training and supervision, reduces contamination is limited. The strongest studies measure failures of segregation or the effects of *bundled* interventions; they do not isolate a colour-coding effect. ((who2023generalprinciplesof pages 23-25, faourklingbeil2016microbiologicalqualityof pages 9-13, browne2023multimodalenvironmentalcleaning pages 1-2)

## Food processing: allergens and hygienic zoning

Codex *General Principles of Food Hygiene* (CXC 1-1969, 2023 edition), §11.1, calls for **separate, suitably designated cleaning equipment and utensils** for different hygiene zones, such as food-contact and non-food-contact surfaces. It requires cleaning equipment to be kept clean, maintained, stored to avoid contamination and replaced periodically. Codex does **not** prescribe particular colours. It calls for monitoring and verification, including audits and, where appropriate, microbial, protein or allergen swabs; cleaning must remove allergenic residues, not merely look satisfactory. These are hygiene-programme controls that support HACCP, not proof that colouring alone is an effective critical control point. ((who2023generalprinciplesof pages 23-25)

For allergen-containing lines, Jackson *et al.* (*Journal of Food Protection*, 2008) specifically recommend **dedicated, colour-coded brushes and other cleaning devices** so that they are not used on non-allergen lines. They caution that cleaning can disperse dust and recontaminate adjacent surfaces. Validation should establish that a defined cleaning procedure reproducibly removes the relevant allergen; visual cleanliness alone cannot verify absence of allergenic residue. In FDA-associated inspections reviewed by the authors, undeclared egg protein was found in **5/45 samples (11%)** and peanut protein in **18/73 (25%)**; inadequate cleaning or product sequencing were implicated in some positives. Those figures demonstrate the hazard of carry-over, **not** an effect estimate for colour coding. (jackson2008cleaningandother pages 9-10, jackson2008cleaningandother pages 2-3, jackson2008cleaningandother pages 10-11)

An observational study by Faour-Klingbeil *et al.* (*LWT*, 2016) inspected **50 restaurants**, sampled **118 ready-to-eat salads** and swabbed **49 knives or cutting boards**. **18% of premises misused colour-coded boards for several food types**, and cross-contamination risks were recorded at **48%**. *E. coli* was recovered from **15/49 surfaces (30.6%)** and coliforms from **26/49 (53.0%)**. The authors did **not** report contamination separately for correctly versus incorrectly colour-coded boards; overall visual inspection scores were not reliably correlated with bacterial counts. This is direct evidence of a compliance failure, not a quantified protective effect. (faourklingbeil2016microbiologicalqualityof pages 1-5, faourklingbeil2016microbiologicalqualityof pages 9-13, faourklingbeil2016microbiologicalqualityof pages 17-20)

A **2026 preprint** of a randomized trial in **302 Ethiopian butcher shops** tested training, nudges and hygiene supplies—including different-coloured wiping towels and buckets—against controls, with an additional arm receiving inspection and public ratings. Intervention shops had lower adjusted odds of *E. coli* contamination (**aOR 0.60, 95% CI 0.44–0.84**) and higher odds of observed proper practices (**aOR 1.35, 95% CI 1.235–1.45**). This is promising evidence for the **package**, not a colour-specific estimate; it is also a preprint rather than a confirmed peer-reviewed result. (dinede2026…ofa pages 3-4, dinede2026…offood pages 1-5)

## Healthcare: the NHS cleaning scheme

The *NHSScotland National Cleaning Services Specification*, version 3 (July 2014), documents adoption of the National Patient Safety Agency national colour-coding scheme in Scotland in **2008**. It assigns **red** to bathrooms and toilets, **blue** to general ward and office areas, **green** to ward kitchens and patient food service, and **yellow** to isolation areas. Cloths, mops, microfibre equipment, buckets, aprons and gloves are covered; the marking must be **clear and permanent**, and designated materials must remain in their designated area. This is a specified **four-colour healthcare scheme**, not a universal meaning for those colours in food factories or laboratories. The specification describes risk-weighted observational cleaning audits, but does not supply a measured infection reduction attributable to adopting its colours. (force2009thenhsscotlandnationala pages 10-13, force2009thenhsscotlandnationala pages 7-10)

The rationale is credible because a cloth or mop can carry contamination between surfaces. Browne and Mitchell (*Antimicrobial Resistance & Infection Control*, 2023) recommend changing cloths and mop heads **between rooms and bathrooms**, using one cloth for one site where appropriate, and using colour-coded equipment to distinguish zones. Colour is an adjunct to these handling and reprocessing rules: a correctly coloured but contaminated cloth is not safe. In six Nigerian maternity facilities studied by Buxton *et al.* (*Journal of Hospital Infection*, 2019), only **2/6** used segregated cleaning equipment for clinical waste and **3/6** had visibly dirty mop heads. These observations show implementation vulnerabilities; they are **not** a trial of the NHS scheme. (browne2023multimodalenvironmentalcleaning pages 2-4, buxton2019barriersandopportunities pages 3-5, buxton2019barriersandopportunities pages 5-6)

Measured improvements from broader cleaning must not be misattributed to the colours. In a UK two-ward crossover study, Dancer *et al.* (*BMC Medicine*, 2009) found **32.5% less contamination** at hand-touch sites with an additional cleaner (*P*<0.0001), alongside fewer new MRSA infections; colour coding was **not the tested intervention**. Likewise, a Ballarat Health Services poster reported an increase in UV-gel cleaning-audit compliance from **40% to approximately 80%** after education, checklists, feedback and a risk-tiered **“Red Clean/Green Clean” process**. Those names classified *cleaning procedures*, not red and green utensils; there was no controlled, colour-specific microbial outcome. (dancer2009measuringtheeffect pages 1-2, flockhart2016implementationofared pages 1-1)

## Pharmaceutical GMP: dedication, status and verified cleanliness

PIC/S *Guide to GMP for Medicinal Products*, Part II (**PE 009-14**, §§4.40–4.42, 5.21–5.26), calls for dedicated production areas for highly sensitising materials, controls on movement between areas, written equipment-cleaning and release procedures, cleaning of non-dedicated equipment between different materials, justified residue-acceptance criteria, and identification of equipment by its **contents and cleanliness status**. Thus a legible **“CLEAN”/“TO BE CLEANED”** tag can implement a status-control system, but those exact words and particular tag **colours are not prescribed by §5.26**. US **21 CFR §211.67** similarly requires documented cleaning, protection of clean equipment, removal of prior batch identification and inspection for cleanliness; it does not make a coloured tag a substitute for these requirements. (scheme2009guidetogooda pages 12-15, scheme2009guidetogooda pages 15-18, b.2023reviewoncurrent pages 8-10)

For an efficacy endpoint, PIC/S Part II **§12.7** requires risk-based cleaning validation with defined acceptable levels, suitable **swab or rinse sampling**, sufficiently sensitive validated analytical methods and, where relevant, microbiological or endotoxin evaluation; continued monitoring follows validation. A status label records or communicates a controlled state—it does **not measure drug residue**. The retrieved GMP provisions do not report an incident-rate or chemical-residue reduction caused specifically by colour-coded status tags. (scheme2009guidetogooda pages 35-37, scheme2009guidetogooda pages 37-40)

## Research laboratories

Molecular laboratories face analogous but especially sensitive carry-over risks: amplified DNA can produce false positives. A tuberculosis line-probe-assay laboratory manual specifies separate reagent-preparation, extraction, amplification and post-amplification areas, with **dedicated pipettes, instruments and supplies** and a clean-to-dirty, one-way workflow; one passage explicitly calls for extraction pipettes to be **colour-coded and numbered**. Negative and reagent controls reveal failures that visual identification cannot. No comparative experiment in that manual establishes how many false positives colouring prevents. (barnard2012moleculardetectionof pages 16-21, somoskovi2013moleculardetectionof pages 29-33, somoskovi2013moleculardetectionofa pages 266-270, somoskovi2013moleculardetectionofa pages 203-208, barnard2012moleculardetectionof pages 21-25)

Laboratory measurements reinforce the importance of **actual cleanliness and dedication**, rather than assuming a colour indicates either. Berthelsen *et al.* (*Royal Society Open Science*, 2025) compared washed, reused microtitre plates: microsatellite genotyping discrepancies were approximately **0.5%** with new-plate controls, **0.4%** with reused fragment-detection plates, but **2.8%** with reused **PCR** plates, consistent with residual DNA causing errors. Plate colours in figures are not an intervention tested for contamination prevention. In a separate ancient-DNA 96-column extraction study, Gilardet *et al.* (2025) monitored sequenced negative controls and found **no significant cross-contamination** among samples sharing plates; its figure colours distinguished **taxa**, not clean and dirty equipment. (berthelsen2025sustainabilityinthe pages 4-5, gilardet2025ahigh‐throughputancient pages 6-9, berthelsen2025sustainabilityinthe pages 2-4)

## Failure modes and choice of palette

* **Use drifts despite a visible code.** The restaurant study's **18% board misuse** is a measured example. Missing or shared equipment, inadequate training, dirty mops, and returning tools across a clean/dirty boundary can defeat the system. Record the designated *zone and material* in an SOP and audit actual tool use as well as whether tools are present. (faourklingbeil2016microbiologicalqualityof pages 9-13, buxton2019barriersandopportunities pages 5-6, somoskovi2013moleculardetectionofa pages 266-270)
* **Colour degrades or ceases to match.** Fading, staining, replacement parts in slightly different shades and locally invented meanings are plausible failure modes, but the sources reviewed did **not quantify their incidence**. NHSScotland's requirement for a **clear, permanent** designation and Codex's maintenance-and-replacement requirement provide defensible inspection criteria: retire illegible items rather than guessing their assignment. (force2009thenhsscotlandnationala pages 10-13, (who2023generalprinciplesof pages 23-25)
* **Colour alone is inaccessible or ambiguous.** Thomas *et al.* (*Journal of Patient Safety*, 2019) report congenital colour-vision deficiency in approximately **8% of men** and show that some clinical code pairs become confusable. They recommend discriminable palettes and redundant **written labels, patterns or other non-colour cues**. A printed zone name, pictogram or number also helps when lighting or wear alters appearance. (thomas2019colorvisiondeficiency pages 2-5, thomas2019colorvisiondeficiency pages 5-5, thomas2019colorvisiondeficiency pages 1-1)
* **Schemes conflict.** The NHS's **yellow = isolation** and **green = ward food service** should not be transferred to a restaurant example that uses **yellow = raw poultry**, **green = produce**, and **purple = high-allergen products**. Zaporozhan and Kuzmin's restaurant paper lists **seven** food-category colours, but reports no experimental advantage for seven. A local legend and induction for staff moving between sites are therefore essential. (force2009thenhsscotlandnationala pages 10-13, zaporozhan2022haccpcolorcoding pages 1-4)
* **More colours are not automatically safer.** The sources establish a working **four-colour NHS example** and a proposed **seven-colour food example**, **not an evidence-based optimal or maximum number**. Use the *fewest distinct categories that still maintain necessary hazard barriers*; reserve extra categories only when the risk assessment requires them, test whether staff can identify them under working conditions, and retain names or numbers as the authoritative identifiers. No defensible universal “use exactly four” or “no more than five” rule follows from the cited studies. (force2009thenhsscotlandnationala pages 10-13, zaporozhan2022haccpcolorcoding pages 1-4, thomas2019colorvisiondeficiency pages 5-5)

**Interpretation of measurements.** ATP and general microbial counts can assess aspects of cleaning but do not establish the absence of a particular allergen or pharmaceutical compound; use a validated **allergen/protein assay** or **compound-specific residue method** for that question. Monitor wrong-zone tool use and near misses alongside swabs and cleaning audits, and do not count improvements from an entire hygiene programme as proof that its colours independently prevented an incident. ((who2023generalprinciplesof pages 23-25, scheme2009guidetogooda pages 35-37, jackson2008cleaningandother pages 10-11, browne2023multimodalenvironmentalcleaning pages 1-2)
