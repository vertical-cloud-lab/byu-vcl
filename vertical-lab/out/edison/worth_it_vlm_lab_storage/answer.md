Question: Vertical lift modules (VLMs), vertical carousels and automated storage and retrieval systems (AS/RS) used in laboratories, biobanks, hospital and central-fill pharmacies, clean rooms and research facilities: what is stored (samples, consumables, reagents, tools, instruments), measured tray or carrier access times and picks per hour, reported benefits with numbers (floor space saved, pick accuracy, inventory traceability, ergonomics, access control), and reported problems (downtime, maintenance, being a single point of failure, what happens when the machine faults with items inside). Which of the reported benefits come from storing many passive items densely, and would any apply to a small number of instruments that run in place?

## Automated Storage and Retrieval in Laboratories, Biobanks, Pharmacies, and Research Facilities: Benefits, Problems, and Applicability to Instruments

### 1. What Is Stored

Across the settings examined in the literature, the items stored in vertical lift modules (VLMs), vertical carousels, and automated storage and retrieval systems (AS/RS) are overwhelmingly **passive, small, and numerous**:

- **Clinical and research laboratories**: Biological samples, individually barcoded tubes, sample racks (typically 50 samples per rack), and aliquots held in temperature-controlled freezer drawers at −20 °C to −170 °C. One laboratory AS/RS was designed for 300,000 samples, with daily throughput of approximately 30,000 sample stores and 30,000 retrievals (farnsworth1997laboratoryautomaticstorage pages 1-2).
- **Biobanks**: Frozen blood and tissue biospecimens in SBS-format racks and trays at −20 °C and −80 °C. Automated biobank stores at the UZ KU Leuven Biobank held over 63,000 samples within 15 months of integration (linsen2020automatedsamplestorage pages 1-2, baber2019automationinbiobanking pages 5-7, linsen2020automatedsamplestorage pages 8-9).
- **Hospital and central-fill pharmacies**: Medications, unit-dose packages, and controlled substances. A Spanish hospital's semiautomatic carousel systems processed 1,235,662 medication lines in six months (martinherranz2013tch002analysisof pages 1-1). Robotic pharmacy systems dispensed up to 6,300 non-IV doses per day (wang2022designinganeffectivea pages 8-12, wang2022designinganeffective pages 8-12).
- **Research compound management**: Solid compounds in vials, DMSO-solubilized samples in 1.4-mL microtubes, and 96-, 384-, and 1,536-well microplates. Reported capacities include more than 2 million solid-sample vials and nearly 3 million liquid-sample microtubes per store (walsh2011automatedcompoundmanagement pages 5-6, hodder2005buildingauhts pages 6-7). One prototype processed 200–500 weekly medicinal-chemistry submissions (quintero2009designandimplementation pages 3-4).

No published reports were identified describing laboratory instruments, analytical equipment, or tools being stored inside VLMs, vertical carousels, or AS/RS for retrieval and in-place operation.

**Floor space savings.** VLMs can save up to 85% of floor space by storing trays vertically and placing them as close as one inch apart (munoz2025taxonomicclassificationanda pages 39-43, munoz2025taxonomicclassificationand pages 39-43). VSMs also report up to 85% floor-space savings (torchio2023surveyonautomated pages 67-72). An industrial AS/RS case study demonstrated a 91.9% reduction in warehouse footprint (laksono2021automatedstorageand pages 4-9).

**Pick accuracy.** VLM systems report 99.9% pick accuracy through position indicators and inventory software (munoz2025taxonomicclassificationanda pages 39-43, munoz2025taxonomicclassificationand pages 39-43).

**Ergonomics.** VLMs present items at a waist-high input/output area, reducing reaching, bending, and climbing, and can eliminate up to 65% of walking time compared to static shelving (munoz2025taxonomicclassificationanda pages 39-43, munoz2025taxonomicclassificationand pages 39-43, novara2020anewdecision pages 59-64). In adjacent pharmacy automation evidence, a preparation robot reduced musculoskeletal disorders by 89% (salime2025assessmentofautomation pages 5-6).

**Inventory traceability.** Barcode-based identification, database records of each sample's rack and position, transaction logging, and audit trails provide comprehensive traceability in laboratory AS/RS (farnsworth1997laboratoryautomaticstorage pages 2-6). A hospital pharmacy preparation robot increased traceability to 97% (salime2025assessmentofautomation pages 5-6).

**Access control.** Automated dispensing cabinets resolved storage-key difficulties previously reported by 66.1% of staff, and retrieval time for controlled medications fell from 107 to 48 seconds (salime2025assessmentofautomation pages 5-6).

### 4. Reported Problems

**Downtime and reliability.** An industry survey of multi-aisle AS/RS reported average uptime of 97.34%, with first-year uptime rising from 94.05% to 96.22%. When high-quality totes or pallets are used, component reliability can exceed 99.98–99.99%. Most downtime was unexpected rather than scheduled. Insertion/extraction equipment was the most problematic subsystem, identified by almost 40% of respondents, while control software was the second-most problematic area during the first three years (lewczuk2021thestudyon pages 2-2, lewczuk2021thestudyon pages 2-3).

**Single point of failure.** High-density automated systems such as AS/RS, OSR, and cube storage can become a single point of failure: when a critical component fails, the system cannot continue operating automatically and cannot be operated manually either, causing "the whole plant [to come] to a halt" (novara2020anewdecision pages 24-28, novara2020anewdecision pages 28-32). Industry experts report that in most high-density systems, operators "must stop completely" when something fails (garcia2025identifyingthekey pages 19-23). For shuttle-based AS/RS, maintenance can make entire product aisles inaccessible (torchio2023surveyonautomated pages 63-67).

**Items inside during faults.** When a failure occurs, items stored inside may become operationally inaccessible until the system is repaired. No manual retrieval procedure, emergency access method, or guaranteed ability to remove items by hand was described in the sources examined (novara2020anewdecision pages 28-32, garcia2025identifyingthekey pages 19-23). In biobank systems, specific fault modes include minicrashes, tube-detection failures, software crashes, picker-calibration problems, and tube-on-tube errors (linsen2020automatedsamplestorage pages 2-3). A backward tray insertion (which the system did not detect) could cause the tray to be lifted and dropped, dislodging samples, causing sample loss, and obstructing mechanical parts (linsen2020automatedsamplestorage pages 4-5). Some system designs provide redundancy: single-deep racks allow other aisle cranes to access material after a device failure, and duplicated or bypass conveyor connections can reduce disruption (lewczuk2021thestudyon pages 3-4). Vapor-phase liquid-nitrogen storage provides a safety margin of up to three days after power failure (baber2019automationinbiobanking pages 7-8).

**Implementation burden.** At the UZ KU Leuven Biobank, two automated frozen-sample stores took approximately four years from decision to production because of labware changes, initial installation quality, and mismatched expectations between manufacturer and biobank (linsen2020automatedsamplestorage pages 1-2). Qualification and validation required approximately 0.5 FTE for one year from a dedicated internal super-user (linsen2020automatedsamplestorage pages 4-5, linsen2020automatedsamplestorage pages 7-8). For automated compound management systems, planned maintenance itself could create extended outages disrupting downstream research; preventive maintenance was scheduled twice yearly and later divided into smaller subsystem-focused packages to reduce disruption (walsh2011automatedcompoundmanagement pages 1-2).

**Maintenance.** Automated systems require recurring maintenance expenses for software updates, hardware repairs, and staff training in addition to substantial initial investment (salime2025assessmentofautomation pages 8-9). Enclosures that protect automation from airborne contamination also restrict access to robotic platforms, making routine maintenance and troubleshooting more difficult (hodder2005buildingauhts pages 4-6).

### 5. Which Benefits Derive from Storing Many Passive Items Densely, and Would Any Apply to a Small Number of Instruments That Run in Place?

| Benefit Category | Depends on Storing Many Passive Items Densely? | Would Apply to a Small Number of Active Instruments? | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Floor-space savings (**up to 85–92%**) | **Yes** | **No, generally** | The saving comes from consolidating many trays, bins, or loads vertically, eliminating aisles and unused shelf height. A few operating instruments still require benches, service clearances, ventilation, power, data, gases, fluids, and stable utility connections, so they cannot normally be packed away densely while running. The cited **85%** and **91.9%** figures are generic/industrial rather than laboratory-instrument demonstrations. Instruments that run in place are not repeatedly picked, so this metric is largely irrelevant. (torchio2023surveyonautomated pages 63-67, torchio2023surveyonautomated pages 67-72, rosi2016simulationbasedperformanceanalysis pages 6-10) |
| Pick accuracy (**99.9% reported**) | **Yes** | **Partially** | Position indicators and software reduce selection errors among many similar bins, packages, tubes, or SKUs. A few large, distinctive instruments are harder to confuse, although software could still verify that the correct instrument, accessory, or calibration standard was selected. (salime2025assessmentofautomation pages 5-6, munoz2025taxonomicclassificationanda pages 39-43, munoz2025taxonomicclassificationand pages 39-43) |
| Traceability and audit trail (**97% reported in adjacent pharmacy evidence**) | **Partially** | **Partially** | Barcode/database integration is especially valuable for large collections because it records each item's identity, location, movement, and custody. Active instruments may also require usage, calibration, maintenance, and user audit trails, but these can usually be provided more simply through instrument software, RFID/asset tags, access logs, or a LIMS. The **97%** figure came from a pharmacy preparation robot rather than an instrument-storage deployment. (farnsworth1997laboratoryautomaticstorage pages 2-6, salime2025assessmentofautomation pages 5-6, alkhatib2024datamanagementin pages 1-1) |
| Access control and security | **Partially** | **Partially** | Enclosed automation is useful for controlled drugs, hazardous compounds, scarce samples, and high-value inventory because release can be tied to user authorization. Expensive instruments may also merit access control, but badge-controlled rooms, locked enclosures, software logins, or cages usually provide it without a retrieval machine. (almahjari2024lowcostautomatedstorage pages 8-11, salime2025assessmentofautomation pages 5-6, alkhatib2024datamanagementin pages 1-1) |

This is a collection-scale benefit for biosamples or compounds, not for ordinary room-temperature instruments operating continuously. Specialized temperature-controlled instruments would require environmental control while operating, not dense passive storage. A handful of fixed instruments is readily visible and can normally be tracked with an asset register or LIMS; automation may still help track their removable tools, consumables, and accessories. The analysis above reveals that **virtually all of the quantified benefits reported for VLMs, vertical carousels, and AS/RS are density-dependent**—they arise from consolidating large numbers of small, passive items (samples, medications, compounds, consumables) into a compact footprint and automating their retrieval:

- **Floor space savings** (up to 85–92%) require many items to consolidate; a few instruments that need bench space, utility connections, ventilation, and service clearances cannot be packed densely while operating (munoz2025taxonomicclassificationanda pages 39-43, laksono2021automatedstorageand pages 4-9).
- **Pick throughput** (100–400+ lines/hour) is only meaningful when there are many frequent retrievals; instruments that run in place are accessed infrequently (torchio2023surveyonautomated pages 63-67, torchio2023surveyonautomated pages 67-72).
- **Pick accuracy** (99.9%) matters when many visually similar items could be confused; a few distinctive instruments present minimal selection-error risk (munoz2025taxonomicclassificationanda pages 39-43).
- **Ergonomic benefits** (65% less walking, 89% fewer musculoskeletal disorders) arise from eliminating repetitive reaching, bending, and walking during high-volume picking; infrequent access to a few stationary instruments creates negligible ergonomic burden from picking (salime2025assessmentofautomation pages 5-6, munoz2025taxonomicclassificationand pages 39-43).
- **Temperature control benefits** apply to large collections of temperature-sensitive biospecimens; instruments generally operate at room temperature (baber2019automationinbiobanking pages 7-8, linsen2020automatedsamplestorage pages 7-8).
- **Inventory visibility** scales with collection size; a few instruments are easily tracked with an asset register or laboratory information management system (farnsworth1997laboratoryautomaticstorage pages 2-6, jourdan1997automatedcompoundinventory pages 1-2).

**Traceability and access control** are the only benefit categories that partially transfer to instruments, since expensive or regulated equipment may require usage logs, calibration tracking, and authorized-user restrictions. However, these functions can typically be achieved with simpler means—RFID tags, software logins, badge-controlled rooms, or locked enclosures—without enclosing instruments inside a retrieval machine (farnsworth1997laboratoryautomaticstorage pages 2-6, salime2025assessmentofautomation pages 5-6, alkhatib2024datamanagementin pages 1-1).

In summary, the entire value proposition of VLMs, vertical carousels, and AS/RS is built around the dense, organized storage and rapid automated retrieval of **many passive items**. The problems these systems introduce—single point of failure risk, inaccessibility of contents during faults, multi-year implementation, specialist staffing, and maintenance outages—would be borne equally by any application, but the density-dependent benefits would not materialize for a small number of instruments that must remain connected to utilities and running in place. No published study was identified that uses a VLM, vertical carousel, or AS/RS to store and retrieve active laboratory instruments.
