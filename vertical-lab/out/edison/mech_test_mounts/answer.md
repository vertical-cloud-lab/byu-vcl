Question: For nanoindentation and profilometry-based indentation plastometry (PIP, e.g. Plastometrex PLX-Benchtop) on metal samples, what are the sample requirements regarding mounting? Can samples be tested while still in polymer (Bakelite or epoxy) metallographic mounts, or does the compliance of the mount distort results? What do standards (ISO 14577) and literature recommend: minimum thickness, parallelism of faces, rigid metal mounts, removing samples from mounts, or polishing the reverse face? What alternatives (e.g. mounting in metal holders, low-melting alloys, conductive mounts) are used in automated workflows?

## Bottom line

**A Bakelite or epoxy metallographic mount is not automatically disqualifying, but it is not automatically a rigid support.** The decisive distinction is whether the force beneath the indent passes through a sufficiently thick metal specimen into a stable platen, or through a substantial thickness of polymer. For quantitative nanoindentation—especially indentation modulus—avoid an uncharacterized polymer layer beneath the metal. For PIP, an adequately thick metal specimen can remain surrounded by resin, but its thickness, edge clearance and backing must be appropriate to the ball and indentation depth. Published PLX-Benchtop work has tested epoxy-mounted metals; that precedent does **not** establish that every mount geometry is free of bias. (jennett2007anintroductionto pages 25-30, fischercripps2006criticalreviewof pages 7-8, clyne2021profilometry‐basedinversefinite pages 3-4, ma2026inversemethodfor pages 3-5)

### What ISO 14577-related guidance actually says

ISO 14577 concerns **instrumented indentation**, in which force and penetration are measured during the test and used to calculate properties including indentation hardness and modulus. It should not be presented as a specimen-mounting specification for PIP, which fits a measured *residual indent profile* using an inverse model. The National Physical Laboratory’s ISO 14577-oriented Good Practice Guide 92 says that the tested region should be perpendicular to the indentation direction and that the entire specimen must be secured **without significant added compliance or flexure**. It advises pressing a specimen against its stub when bonding with cyanoacrylate, rather than simply setting it on a drop; with hot-melt wax, it advises removing bubbles, minimizing wax thickness and maximizing bonded area. It gives **no universal numerical minimum specimen thickness, reverse-face polish, parallelism tolerance, or requirement for a metal mount** in that mounting guidance. (jennett2007anintroductionto pages 14-22, jennett2007anintroductionto pages 25-30, clyne2021profilometry‐basedinversefinite pages 2-3)

The concern is measurable, not merely cosmetic: Fischer-Cripps identifies a thick layer of mounting resin as a common source of compliance that may **not be included in the instrument’s calibrated frame compliance**. Extra motion then enters the force–displacement response and can bias properties inferred from unloading stiffness. A polished *front* face does not correct an unstable *back* contact. Conversely, resin solely **around the sides** of a thick, directly supported metal coupon is a different mechanical arrangement from resin **between its reverse face and the platen**. Neither guide imposes a blanket ban on lateral resin. (fischercripps2006criticalreviewof pages 7-8, jennett2007anintroductionto pages 25-30)

### Thickness and geometry: distinguish the two tests

For PIP, Clyne and colleagues’ review says that the specimen behaves approximately as an effectively semi-infinite body once metal thickness exceeds **about two indenter diameters**, with the precise sensitivity depending on penetration ratio. Its more specific example is a **1 mm-*diameter* ball**, at penetration depth/radius of approximately 20%: a sheet thickness of roughly **1–1.5 mm**, and an indent axis **at least about 2 mm from a free edge**, are suggested. The authors explicitly discuss free edges for **mounted as well as unmounted** specimens. Thus, for a commonly used **2 mm-diameter ball**, approximately **4 mm of metal** is a useful *two-diameter, backing-insensitivity rule of thumb*—not a universal PLX-Benchtop minimum or an ISO requirement. Do not transfer the cited 2 mm edge distance unchanged to a larger ball. (clyne2021profilometry‐basedinversefinite pages 2-3, clyne2021profilometry‐basedinversefinite pages 3-4)

The frequently quoted **indentation depth below 10% of specimen thickness** is a *coating/substrate hardness* heuristic, not a general rule establishing that a thin bulk-metal PIP coupon on epoxy is acceptable. Elastic modulus can remain sensitive to a substrate even at shallow indentations. Nor does “a few micrometres of penetration” alone establish safe backing for a thin, flexible specimen: the relevant issue is deformation of the whole supported geometry. (jennett2007anintroductionto pages 12-14, jennett2007anintroductionto pages 14-22, fischercripps2006criticalreviewof pages 9-10)

There is direct evidence for **both** published workflows. Ma and colleagues report PLX-Benchtop spherical indentation on metals **cold-mounted in epoxy**, at a sphere radius of 1.006 mm and depth/radius near 0.18; they do not report sufficient reverse-face details or a mounted-versus-unmounted comparison to quantify epoxy’s effect. In contrast, Tallman and colleagues used a separate, **unmounted 65 × 50 × 5 mm** Al plate for PIP; their **epoxy-mounted** 7 × 8 × 5 mm sample was for *metallography*, not PIP. Neither example proves that 5 mm is a compulsory minimum or that all epoxy mounts are equivalent. Conventional nanoindentation has also been reported on graphite-filled phenolic-mounted metal samples, without thereby establishing that phenolic support has negligible compliance in every configuration. (ma2026inversemethodfor pages 3-5, tallman2022uncertaintyquantificationof pages 3-4, wolf2026cr–vbinaryalloys pages 2-3)

### Back-face preparation and automated workflows

**Parallelism and the reverse face are support-quality controls, not a requirement that both faces receive a mirror polish.** A reasonably flat, stable reverse metal face in full contact with the platen prevents rocking and concentrated contact; grind or machine that face if necessary, and arrange the test face approximately normal to the loading axis. Polish the *tested* face to the finish demanded by the measurement. For nanoindentation, NPL offers a surface-roughness guide of **Ra below 5% of contact depth** while cautioning that preparation can change the measured surface. PIP’s larger deformation volume makes a carefully prepared thin damaged layer less consequential, but does not remedy poor backing. A fixture with leveling capability can address modest face nonparallelism; it cannot make a thick resin layer rigid. (jennett2007anintroductionto pages 25-30, jennett2007anintroductionto pages 30-33, clyne2021profilometry‐basedinversefinite pages 14-15)

For a repeatable automated route, the **preferred engineering arrangement** is a metal coupon with its reverse metal exposed and seated on a rigid, flat metal platen, held laterally in an indexed metal pocket or clamp; alternatively, bond it to a rigid metal stub with the **thinnest practicable, pressed, void-free** adhesive/wax layer over a large area. If specimens must be co-mounted for automated grinding and imaging, a polymer puck can serve as the *handling and polishing carrier* while the reverse metal is subsequently exposed for indentation, or specimens can be removed and reseated in a rigid holder. The metallography manual describes mechanical removal of mounting resin, but removal methods must be chosen so as not to alter the polished specimen or its properties. These fixture designs are **recommendations derived from the load-path and compliance evidence**, not an ISO-mandated holder design. (jennett2007anintroductionto pages 25-30, fischercripps2006criticalreviewof pages 7-8, geels2007metallographicandmaterialographic pages 94-97)

**Conductive is not synonymous with stiff.** Metallographic guidance documents carbon- or metal-filled phenolic mounting materials and other conductive mounts principally for SEM or electropolishing; their electrical purpose does not establish negligible mechanical compliance beneath an indent. A low-melting metallic alloy could in principle serve as a removable carrier, but the retrieved indentation evidence does **not** validate it as a standard nanoindentation or kN-load PIP backing. If considered, check specimen heating, bonding/voids, contamination and time-dependent deformation, then compare its results against a directly supported coupon. (geels2007metallographicandmaterialographic pages 84-87, geels2007metallographicandmaterialographic pages 94-97, jennett2007anintroductionto pages 25-30)

**Practical acceptance test:** retain a polymer mount only when the metal thickness and edge clearance suit the actual PIP ball (or the nanoindentation depth), the assembly cannot rock or flex, and a representative **mounted-versus-directly-supported comparison** gives equivalent results within the required uncertainty. For nanoindentation compare load–depth curves and modulus as well as hardness; for PIP compare residual profiles and inferred stress–strain curves. If the mount introduces a substantial polymer layer directly under a thin metal specimen, use direct metal backing, reprepare the reverse face, or remove and remount the specimen before reporting quantitative properties. (jennett2007anintroductionto pages 25-30, fischercripps2006criticalreviewof pages 7-8, clyne2021profilometry‐basedinversefinite pages 3-4, clyne2021profilometry‐basedinversefinite pages 2-3, ma2026inversemethodfor pages 3-5)
