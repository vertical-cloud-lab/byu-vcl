# Facility-scale automated laboratories: layouts and operating limits

**The main distinction is how samples reach instruments.** Emerald Cloud Lab (ECL) largely retains instrument benches tended by staff; Strateos uses enclosed robotic workcells connected by magnetic transport; Liverpool and Argonne’s Polybot use mobile robots to connect distributed stations; A-Lab and Samsung ASTRAL use robot-served stations, including linear rails; and Ginkgo’s Reconfigurable Automation Carts (RACs) connect movable instrument modules with a sample track. These are not interchangeable definitions of a “cloud lab”: remote access, physical automation, and autonomous experimental decision-making are separate capabilities. Published campaign counts are substantially more common than measurements of facility-wide instrument occupancy. (vescovi2023towardsamodular pages 2-3, smith2026usingagpt5driven pages 3-6, ireland2022cloudlabsand pages 1-2, szymanski2023anautonomouslaboratory pages 7-7, chen2024navigatingphasediagrama pages 4-5, vriza2023selfdrivinglaboratoryfor pages 8-9)

## What the operating evidence says about layout choice

**1. Size the slow scientific step before optimizing transport.** Liverpool’s measured **232-minute GC step**, longer than its **183-minute preparation/photolysis step**, explains the overlapping batches and much of its 32% charged-but-idle time. ASTRAL identifies *serial powder preparation* as its limit even though later stages can overlap; A-Lab’s heating includes hours of dwell, ramps and cooling. These examples do not support an assumption that a faster conveyor, shorter robot journey or denser floor plan would by itself raise output. The appropriate response depends on the bottleneck: batch or replicate analytical capacity, improve sample preparation, or overlap independent work. (szymanski2023anautonomouslaboratory pages 7-7, chen2024navigatingphasediagrama pages 4-5, burger2020amobilerobotic pages 6-12, burger2020amobilerobotic pages 4-6)

**2. Choose mobility for access and sharing, not automatically for speed.** Liverpool’s free-roaming robot reaches established, human-oriented benches, and the 2024 implementation shares costly LC–MS and NMR; Polybot connects distinct synthesis and fabrication cells the same way. Fixed arm-centred cells and rails instead simplify repeatable positioning and high-frequency local transfers, but constrain what can be reached. Argonne’s authors describe a continuum: fixed integration generally trades flexibility for speed and reliability, while mobile routing admits more arbitrary equipment placement. Their proposed automatically reconfigured cart factory is explicitly distinct from the manually assembled eight-cart demonstration. (vescovi2023towardsamodular pages 2-3, dai2024autonomousmobilerobots pages 2-3, vescovi2023towardsamodular pages 7-9, vriza2023selfdrivinglaboratoryfor pages 8-9, vriza2023selfdrivinglaboratoryfor pages 7-8)

**3. Reconfigurable hardware still needs validated interfaces and human support.** Ginkgo’s carts and Argonne’s dockable modules make instrument combinations changeable, but physical docking, power, plate hand-offs, grippers and software definitions must all agree. At Argonne, carts were still positioned, pinned and powered manually; at Ginkgo, people prepared and loaded reagents. MINERVA’s authors describe the difficulty of controlling proprietary analytical instruments. A-Lab’s operating software explicitly provides human notifications, recoverable-error handling and maintenance pauses rather than treating unattended operation as maintenance-free operation. (smith2026usingagpt5driven pages 3-6, fei2024alabosapythonbased pages 16-20, vescovi2023towardsamodular pages 7-9, zaki2025aselfdrivinglaba pages 3-4, zaki2025aselfdrivinglaba pages 4-5)

**4. Several users require a resource scheduler, not merely a web portal.** Ginkgo’s Catalyst can run multiple workflows on a cart assembly; A-Lab reserves both devices *and physical sample positions* before starting tasks; biofoundry scheduling prevents contention and tracks plate identity and provenance. Cloud-lab users nevertheless report limited parallelism when a specialized instrument has no duplicate, and Ginkgo reports incubation extending under heavy utilization. These are stronger grounds for designing queues, batch sizes, priority rules, maintenance states and buffers than advertised round-the-clock access alone. They are **not** published measurements of percentage utilization across the named facilities. (fei2024alabosapythonbased pages 5-9, smith2026usingagpt5driven pages 3-6, singh2025biofoundriesprinciplestools pages 9-11, arias2024scientificdiscoveryat pages 6-6, smith2026usingagpt5driven pages 16-18)

**Interpretation of “utilization.”** The clearest measured robot-idleness figure among these reports is Liverpool’s approximately **32% charged-but-idle** during its 2020 campaign. A separate 2026 Liverpool process-chemist *preprint* reports a robot active for about **20% of three consecutive syntheses’ runtime**, largely because it waits for other operations; its proposed output with five reactors is a **projection**, not an observed five-reactor result. Neither measure is a fraction of time that every instrument, bench or square metre in a commercial facility was occupied. Campaign volumes, successful compounds, stated 24/7 availability and instrument-type counts must not be presented as such a fraction. (brass2026amobilerobotic pages 15-19, burger2020amobilerobotic pages 6-12)
