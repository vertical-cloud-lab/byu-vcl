Question: How do automated or robotic workflows load metallographic samples into scanning electron microscopes (tabletop SEMs such as Hitachi TM4000 or Phenom, and floor SEMs)? What sample holders, stubs and multi-sample holders are used, and how is an electrically conductive path made from the polished surface of a non-conductive mount (Bakelite/epoxy) to the stage: conductive (carbon/copper-filled) mounting resins, copper tape wrapped from top to bottom, silver paint, drilled holes with a screw or spring contact, or carbon coating? Which approaches are used or recommended for robotic/autonomous labs, and with what reported success?

# Robotic loading and grounding of metallographic SEM samples

**The most convincing demonstrated workflow carries the polished mount in a rigid, repeatably positioned fixture, rather than asking a robot to grip the resin cylinder or place a loose stub.** In AFRL’s LEROY serial-sectioning system, a 1.5-inch (38.1-mm) conductive PolyFast mount was permanently bonded to a stainless-steel RoboMet.3D fixture with silver conductive epoxy. The system exchanged the assembly through the automated load lock of a floor-standing Tescan Vega 3 XMH SEM. It completed **1,000 polished sections**, collecting optical images, BSE images and an EBSD map at each section; a vacuum-pump failure interrupted the run after section 900 before the final 100 were collected. A section cycle took approximately 63 minutes, including robotic transfers, polishing and imaging. EBSD patterns generally showed clear Kikuchi bands, although a few percent of pixels were initially unindexed. These are results for *one repeatedly sectioned specimen*, not a 1,000-specimen loading success rate. (chapman2021afrladditivemanufacturing pages 3-5, chapman2021afrladditivemanufacturing pages 5-7, chapman2021afrladditivemanufacturing pages 7-8, chapman2021afrladditivemanufacturing media 2c3faf61)

## How holders are loaded

| Workflow | Documented carrier and degree of automation |
|---|---|
| **Floor SEM, robotic exchange: LEROY** | A conductive, 1.5-inch metallographic mount remains attached to a stainless-steel fixture; the specimen is transported between preparation and microscopy stations and exchanged through the SEM load lock. The publication does not specify the gripper fingers or the exact electrical contact at the load-lock stage. (chapman2021afrladditivemanufacturing pages 3-5, chapman2021afrladditivemanufacturing pages 7-8, chapman2021afrladditivemanufacturing media 2c3faf61) |
| **Floor SEM, batch loading: Hitachi SU3900/AMICS** | An operator loads a **28-hole holder for 25- or 30-mm polished mounts**. Alternative carriers are a **15-mm SEM stub** for powder and a **100-mm disk** for two glass slides. AMICS then positions and analyzes selected samples automatically within the chamber. This is automated *measurement of a preloaded batch*, not documented robotic loading through the door. The authors analyzed polished epoxy-mounted rock and metal specimens **uncoated in variable-pressure mode** and demonstrated automated mineral/phase maps. Figure 1a depicts the three carrier types. (kostryzhev2025phasecharacterisationin pages 1-2, kostryzhev2025phasecharacterisationin pages 4-7, kostryzhev2025phasecharacterisationin pages 2-4, kostryzhev2024phasecharacterisationin media af839e52) |
| **Robotic metallography, SEM not yet installed: BAM RASI** | A UR5e robot uses a two-finger gripper to move a **30-mm cold- or hot-mounted specimen in a reusable holder**. Three side grooves register the holder at the polishing, etching and microscope stations. RASI demonstrated automated **optical** serial sectioning at roughly 1–2-µm section spacing and 4–10 images/hour; its proposed LEO 1530 SEM integration was **planned, not demonstrated** in the cited report. (lemiasheuski2023developmentofan pages 12-14, lemiasheuski2023developmentofan pages 7-10) |
| **Tabletop Phenom XL G2: EMSBot** | A modified 3D-printer robot picks up **carbon-tape-covered SEM stubs** with a vacuum needle from a removable tray, verifies pickup with a laser sensor, deposits dispersed powder and returns the prepared stub. The authors subsequently examined the stubs in a Phenom XL G2; they reported better powder dispersion and less agglomeration than manual preparation across multiple powders. **Robotic insertion into the Phenom, or handling of a polished resin mount, was not demonstrated.** (milsted2025automatedelectronmicroscopy pages 3-5, milsted2025automatedelectronmicroscopy pages 5-6, milsted2025automatedelectronmicroscopy pages 1-2, milsted2025automatedelectronmicroscopy pages 2-3) |

The literature also describes ELAVO 3D transferring a movable specimen holder between polishing equipment and an SEM with a six-axis robot, and LEROY/RS3D extending automated serial sectioning to SEM analysis. The cited account does not specify their stub geometry or quantitative loading reliability. **No verified robotic-loading recipe or proprietary holder dimensions for a Hitachi TM4000—or a polished-mount robotic loader for a Phenom—were established by these sources.** A tabletop implementation therefore needs a carrier fitted to the *particular instrument’s* specimen receptacle, height and chamber clearance; a floor-SEM multi-mount plate should not be assumed to fit it. (lemiasheuski2023developmentofan pages 5-7, kostryzhev2025phasecharacterisationin pages 2-4, milsted2025automatedelectronmicroscopy pages 2-3)

## Making the electrical path

The required circuit is **exposed metal or conductive coating → deliberate conductive bridge or conductive mount → metal holder/fixture → grounded SEM stage**. Merely placing insulating Bakelite or epoxy on a metal stage does not establish that circuit. The following table separates published results from engineering choices proposed for an unattended robot. (mackenzie2005analyticalcharacterizationof pages 34-39, cvetanovic2009acontributiontoa pages 60-62)

| Approach | Electrical path and practical caveat | Reported validation vs. robotic recommendation |
|---|---|---|
| **Conductive mount: carbon-filled phenolic/PolyFast; copper-filled Probemet; carbon-filled cold epoxy/CarboCond** | Conductive filler makes the mount itself part of the route from exposed specimen to a metal fixture. Copper-filled Probemet was reported to give effectively zero specimen-to-mount resistance and excellent edge retention; carbon-filled phenolic had much higher resistance. CarboCond cold epoxies charged little in SEM but remained orders of magnitude less conductive than hot-mounted ConduFast. Verify continuity because filler dispersion, specimen–resin gaps, and surface polishing can interrupt the path. (mackenzie2005analyticalcharacterizationof pages 34-39, besserer2016qualifyingelectricallyconductive pages 6-9) | **Strongest demonstrated robotic choice.** LEROY used a 1.5-inch conductive PolyFast mount permanently bonded with silver conductive epoxy to a stainless-steel fixture; automated load-lock exchange supported 1,000 serial sections, each with BSE imaging and EBSD, with only a few percent of EBSD pixels initially unindexed. Prefer a keyed, permanently bonded carrier plus measured resistance before loading. (chapman2021afrladditivemanufacturing pages 3-5, chapman2021afrladditivemanufacturing pages 5-7, chapman2021afrladditivemanufacturing pages 7-8) |
| **Copper-tape bridge** | Tape can bridge a conductive surface or specimen edge to the holder, but its adhesive is part of the circuit. Copper tape is relatively stiff, may contact unevenly, can outgas, and can interfere with robot-clearance or datum surfaces. Jaques *et al.* placed tape beneath and around drilled locations in a PolyFast block; charging fell where specimens tightly contacted the conductive component but persisted elsewhere. (jaques2022conductivecrosssectionpreparation pages 8-10, jaques2022conductivecrosssectionpreparation pages 3-5, westin2025enhancingmicrostructuralanalysis pages 5-7) | **Usable but weaker for unattended operation.** Suitable as a controlled, inspected secondary bridge, preferably captured mechanically rather than relying only on adhesive. The evidence does **not** validate a tape strip wrapped from polished top to mount bottom through repeated robot handling; qualify adhesion, vacuum compatibility, resistance, and collision envelope first. |
| **Silver conductive paint or silver-epoxy bond** | Silver paint can run continuously from the polished top down one side to the bottom/holder contact. One documented arrangement pressed the painted side against a copper holder with a screw. Silver paint reportedly had lower resistance and more reliable grounding than copper tape and produced positive EBSD results on otherwise nonconductive cold mounts; it must be applied evenly, dried fully, and kept away from the analysis region. Silver epoxy provides a more permanent fixture bond. (westin2025enhancingmicrostructuralanalysis pages 5-7, yang2001thestructureand pages 62-66) | **Recommended for robots when geometrically constrained or permanently bonded.** Use a repeatable masked stripe terminating on a defined metal contact pad, or silver epoxy between mount and reusable carrier. LEROY’s silver-epoxy-to-steel fixture is the strongest autonomous validation; freehand paint alone needs resistance inspection and cure-state control. (chapman2021afrladditivemanufacturing pages 3-5, chapman2021afrladditivemanufacturing pages 7-8) |
| **Rear drilled hole with screw/wire; proposed spring contact** | A hole drilled from the mount rear to the embedded metal can accept a screw; alternatively, a wire can be soldered to the specimen before cold mounting and left protruding. These provide direct specimen contact independent of resin conductivity. Risks include drilling into or stressing the specimen, variable screw torque, protrusions, contamination, and loss of a flat robot datum. A spring-loaded carrier contact could make the interface self-engaging, but contact force and oxidation must be controlled. (mackenzie2005analyticalcharacterizationof pages 34-39) | **Promising engineered interface, but not robot-validated in the located literature.** A captive screw or compliant spring pin in a keyed metal puck is preferable to a loose screw or wire, with continuity sensing before pump-down. The historical drilled-screw method supports the electrical principle, not successful autonomous SEM loading; no reported robotic spring-contact success was found. |
| **Carbon coating plus edge bridge** | Carbon coating makes the polished face conductive only if the film connects continuously to the grounded holder. Uneven coating at edges, vertical faces, or glue interfaces can break the route; one experiment achieved only slight, localized charging reduction because carbon layers were discontinuous. Coating can also alter composition measurements, especially carbon-sensitive EDS. (jaques2022conductivecrosssectionpreparation pages 8-10, jaques2022conductivecrosssectionpreparation pages 1-2) | **Secondary measure, not a stand-alone robotic contact.** Combine a controlled carbon film with a robust painted, taped, or mechanical edge connection and electrical test. Do not assume a coated face is grounded merely because it looks continuous; the reported carbon-layer preparation failed where edge and adhesive contacts were inconsistent. |
| **Variable-pressure SEM, uncoated** | Chamber gas neutralizes surface charge, so an insulating epoxy mount may be examined without a coating or explicit low-resistance surface bridge. This avoids coating time and added carbon/metal peaks, but variable pressure generally sacrifices resolution and does not create a true conductive path needed for high-current or tilt-sensitive methods such as demanding EBSD. | **Good autonomous fallback for BSE/EDS screening, not universal grounding.** Hitachi SU3900 AMICS analyzed uncoated epoxy-mounted specimens in variable pressure and automatically handled measurements across a 28-position holder for 25/30 mm mounts, with reported SEM–EDS spatial resolution down to about 1 µm. Use for robust screening; use a conductive mount/contact for high-vacuum, high-current, high-resolution, or EBSD workflows. (kostryzhev2025phasecharacterisationin pages 2-4, kostryzhev2024phasecharacterisationin media af839e52) |


*Table: Evidence-based comparison of six ways to control charging in polished metallographic mounts, separating demonstrated autonomous implementations from plausible but unvalidated robot interfaces.*

**Practical qualification for an autonomous laboratory.** For repeated polishing and high-vacuum SEM/EBSD, start with a conductive mount bonded to a reusable, keyed metal carrier: LEROY’s PolyFast–silver-epoxy–steel assembly is the directly demonstrated example. A copper-filled mounting compound is another documented conductive route with good edge retention, whereas carbon-filled cold-setting epoxies offer a lower-temperature option but should not be assumed to match the conductivity of hot mounts. Ensure the exposed specimen actually contacts the conductive resin and that the resin or bond contacts the carrier; inspect specimen–resin gaps and measure continuity *from the polished metal to the carrier/stage contact* after final polishing and before loading. This continuity check is an engineering recommendation, not a reported LEROY acceptance threshold. (chapman2021afrladditivemanufacturing pages 3-5, mackenzie2005analyticalcharacterizationof pages 34-39, besserer2016qualifyingelectricallyconductive pages 6-9)

For an existing insulating mount, a **continuous silver-paint trace from exposed polished metal over one side to a defined holder contact** is directly described: a side screw pressed the painted stripe against a copper SEM holder. Silver paint has also given positive EBSD results on otherwise nonconductive cold mounts and was reported to ground better than copper tape, but must dry fully. A back-drilled screw contacting the buried specimen is a published alternative; a captive spring contact would be a sensible *design adaptation*, **not a robot-validated result** in the located evidence. Copper tape can make contact but is stiff and adhesive-dependent; a published copper-tape/PolyFast mounting experiment reduced charging primarily where contact was tight. Neither that experiment nor the reviewed tape guidance demonstrates a top-to-bottom tape wrap surviving unattended robotic transfers. (jaques2022conductivecrosssectionpreparation pages 8-10, westin2025enhancingmicrostructuralanalysis pages 5-7, yang2001thestructureand pages 62-66, mackenzie2005analyticalcharacterizationof pages 34-39)

**Carbon coating is not a substitute for a verified stage connection.** One tested layered-carbon preparation charged where its coating was discontinuous at edges or separated from the sample by glue. Coating can also add a surface-composition contribution; if coating or contact would compromise the measurement, the SU3900 example shows a different, demonstrated choice: uncoated epoxy mounts analyzed by variable-pressure BSE/EDS. That avoids coating preparation but does **not** establish the low-resistance contact desirable for demanding high-vacuum EBSD. (jaques2022conductivecrosssectionpreparation pages 8-10, jaques2022conductivecrosssectionpreparation pages 1-2, kostryzhev2025phasecharacterisationin pages 2-4)
