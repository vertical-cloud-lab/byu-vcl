## Answer

# Assessment of a low-rate powder feeder for an argon-chamber melt stream

## Bottom line

**The limiting problem is not average screw capacity but the approximately 30-second gap between discharge events.** A smaller outlet alone cannot make a continuous stream: the final screw flight must supply material frequently enough, or a downstream device must hold and independently release powder between deliveries. For a first prototype, retain a reliable, relatively open screw-to-outlet transition and test a **separately actuated, short, smooth metal capillary or vibrating chute** as the final metering stage. A two- or three-start flight is a reasonable *experiment* for increasing event frequency, but its improvement in this rotating-tube geometry is not established. Avoid combining an aggressive taper, tighter pitch, and narrow unflighted nose in the first redesign: these changes can compress powder and create a new blockage. Published vibrating-capillary work demonstrates sub-milligram-per-second to milligram-per-second feeding, but does **not** establish that a capillary will smooth 30-second pulses while remaining stable from 1 to 50 mg/s. (engisch2014lossinweightfeedingin pages 134-139, matsusaka1996microfeedingofa pages 5-11, lu2008ultrasoundassistedmicrofeedingof pages 2-4, dai2008biomassscrewfeeding pages 4-6)

## 1. Screw pulsation and geometric changes

**Established mechanism.** At low rotational speed, discharge depends on which pocket reaches the last flight edge, its instantaneous fill, and whether material can escape past that edge. Engisch observed approximately sinusoidal low-speed feed-rate oscillations for commercial auger feeders; their one-second feed-rate distributions could become U-shaped rather than concentrated around the mean. Higher speeds made the distributions more nearly Gaussian. Hopper level, refill, packing density, cohesion, tilt, and outlet obstruction add slower or irregular components. A nominal single-start flight therefore suggests a fundamental at **one event per revolution**, with harmonics from a sharp or intermittent discharge event; this is a prediction for the stated device, not a universal measured spectrum. At one event per approximately 30 s, the fundamental is **0.033 Hz**. The Froude number, approximately \(\omega ^2R/g\), helps distinguish gravity-dominated from inertial motion, but does not by itself predict pulse amplitude: pocket filling, wall friction, outlet geometry, and cohesion remain essential. Engisch used Fourier spectra to characterize feeder noise but the accessible results do not supply transferable peak amplitudes for this device. DOI: **10.7282/t31z42vm**. (engisch2014lossinweightfeedingin pages 134-139, bostijn2019amultivariateapproach pages 10-14)

**Pitch, diameter, and starts.** A useful first-order *capacity*, not a fluctuation law, is \(V_{\rm rev}\sim\eta(\pi/4)(D^2-d_c^2)p\), where \(\eta\) represents filling and slip, \(D\) is flight diameter, \(d_c\) core diameter, and \(p\) axial lead. Reducing pitch or annular area reduces the volume available per revolution, and can permit a higher rpm at the same *mean* rate; higher rpm also moves a once-per-turn disturbance to higher frequency. Neither operation guarantees a smaller *fractional* pulse. Multiple starts can stagger flight-end events—ideally from \(f\) to \(Nf\) for \(N\) equally loaded starts—but they can instead increase total displacement per turn or produce unequal events. Specify **lead, pocket volume, phase, and fill of each start**, not simply “more starts.” The approximately 300 mm³ actually measured per turn is the appropriate calibration for the existing device, rather than an ideal annular-volume calculation. No validated amplitude-versus-start-count law for this printed, rotating-tube feeder was found. (engisch2014lossinweightfeedingin pages 134-139, bostijn2019amultivariateapproach pages 10-14, bostijn2019amultivariateapproach pages 14-18)

**Flight shape and downstream hardware.** Notches can reduce periodic outlet masking, but their benefit is strongly configuration-dependent. In one fertilizer discharger, an optimized, three-notch sector flight gave a **27.42% measured fluctuation coefficient at 60 rpm**, versus **92.28%** for its conventional spiral; simulated optimum-table value was **27.01%**. Its approximately **3.51-mm particles**, discharge geometry, and test windows make these *evidence that flight-end geometry matters*, not a prediction for 50–150-µm granules at approximately 0.033 Hz. DOI: **10.3389/fmech.2025.1535013**. Concave flights, cut flights, ribbon/shaftless flights, spring augers, discharge screens, and choke tubes alter clearance, filling, shear, and opportunities for bypass; the retrieved evidence does **not** establish numerical pulse attenuation or spectra for a matched comparison among these choices at the requested rates. Removing the 8-mm core might increase free space and reduce core-contact shear, but an open axial channel can also create an uncontrolled gravity-flow path when tilted outlet-down; it is not intrinsically a smoother meter. A screen can break lumps yet add an arching surface. These require direct phase-resolved tests, not a claimed percentage improvement. (dun2025parameteroptimizationof pages 11-12, dun2025parameteroptimizationof pages 6-8, dun2025parameteroptimizationof pages 9-11, hou2024developmentofa pages 156-161)

**Chokes and extensions serve another purpose.** Dai and Grace studied tapered, choked, and extended biomass screws primarily to create sealing plugs; longer chokes increased torque and blockage risk. Reported torque fluctuated roughly **10–30 N·m** in their much larger apparatus, and suggested biomass choke lengths were **4–10 screw pitches**—neither is a suitable design target for this microfeeder. DOI: **10.1016/j.powtec.2007.10.033**. An extended *unflighted* section is not automatically a pulse damper: if it packs, its wall resistance and eventual plug release may create larger, slower bursts. (dai2008biomassscrewfeeding pages 4-6, dai2008biomassscrewfeeding pages 8-9)

## 3. Converging nose, arching, and the proposed smooth tube

**Do not reduce downstream pocket capacity below what arrives unless there is a deliberate escape, increased downstream transport speed, or a demonstrated compressible plug process.** For the same core and filling assumptions, the local per-turn geometric capacity scales approximately as \((D^2-d_c^2)p\); both a smaller \(D\) and a tighter \(p\) drive it downward. Conserving throughput then requires less upstream filling, more downstream rpm—which a one-piece rotor cannot provide—or densification, slip/backflow, and rising axial stress. Dai and Grace explicitly calculated tapered-pocket volumes and corresponding compacted densities and torque; their work supports treating a taper as a **compression section**, not automatically a stable nozzle. A larger-diameter feed reservoir followed by a separately controlled smaller final meter avoids requiring equal volumetric capacity in a single rotating part. DOI: **10.1016/j.powtec.2007.10.033**. (dai2008biomassscrewfeeding pages 4-6)

For relatively dry, noncohesive spheres, roughly **five particle diameters** is a common empirical outlet scale above which clogging becomes infrequent; some particles require ratios approaching **ten**, and whether a truly clog-free critical diameter exists is debated. This is **not** a safe outlet design criterion for cohesive fines or an annular gap: powder strength, wall friction, consolidation history, and the *minimum local clearance* also matter. The proposed **3-mm annular clearance is 20–60 particle diameters** for 150–50 µm individual granules, respectively; this is reassuring only against simple few-grain geometric arches. An agglomerate, a progressively packed taper, a poorly defined local clearance around the conical tip, or fines can still stop flow. DOI: Zuriguel’s review **10.4279/PIP.060014**; archival version **10.48550/arXiv.1412.5806**. (ahmed2018outflowandclogging pages 6-10, zuriguel2014invitedreviewclogging pages 1-2)

Vibration can destabilize an arch and lower effective wall friction, but it does **not** guarantee continuous discharge. Matsusaka’s capillary reached a critical acceleration of approximately **1.1–3.9 m/s²**—fitted at **2.5 m/s²** in those experiments—and its maximum flow scaled roughly as **bore diameter^2–2.5**. Rate rose with frequency and then saturated; sufficiently free-flowing powder could continue discharging after vibration stopped in a larger bore. Their authors also identified tube slope, packing fraction, powder properties, and wall interaction as governing variables without providing a universal predictive flow law. In vibrating clogged hoppers, arch lifetimes can have long tails, so a seemingly effective shaker may still permit extended outages. DOIs: **10.1016/S0921-8831(08)60509-9** and **10.1103/PhysRevLett.120.198002**. (matsusaka1996microfeedingofa pages 5-11)

**A smooth tube is a low-pass filter only conditionally.** Let \(M\) be powder held between screw and outlet. Conservation gives \(dM/dt=q_{\rm screw}-q_{\rm exit}\). If the short tube retains a *persistent* bed and its vibration-controlled outlet rate responds mainly to its slowly changing inventory—not directly to incoming impacts—then its residence-time distribution can attenuate the screw-frequency component. If the tube empties between pulses, the output must also stop between pulses; if it fills until an arch collapses, output becomes intermittent instead. At **10 mg/s and a 30-s gap**, at least **300 mg of *usable* holdup** must remain deliverable between incoming pulses. At **1–50 mg/s**, the same 30-s interval represents **30–1,500 mg**. Those masses, not merely bore diameter, set the minimum buffer requirement. For any proposed narrow tube, measure both the *inventory at which discharge ceases* and the attainable vibration-limited discharge against bore, tube length, inclination, amplitude, frequency, wall finish, and powder condition. The literature supports tunable vibrating-capillary discharge, **not** a measured transfer function or demonstrated 30-s smoothing for the specified screw. (matsusaka1996microfeedingofa pages 5-11, lu2008ultrasoundassistedmicrofeedingof pages 2-4, lu2008ultrasoundassistedmicrofeedingof pages 4-6)

## 4. Dry argon, prior evacuation, and thermal integration

Drying can remove capillary liquid bridges yet also reduce charge leakage; these competing effects make “argon improves flow” and “vacuum improves flow” unreliable generalizations. In controlled glass-bead experiments, fluorinated surfaces acquired **5.89-fold** the tribocharge of hydroxylated surfaces against stainless steel; increasing humidity from 25% to 75% RH accelerated charge relaxation **1.6-fold** for one surface chemistry and **12.2-fold** for another. These are *surface-specific electrostatic measurements*, not a correction factor for this metal feeder. Fine metal-powder studies likewise link moisture uptake and fines to higher cohesion. Dry argon changes humidity and possibly charging relative to room air; evacuation and heating can desorb water and gas, while contact charging, compaction, and thermal drift may change the opposite way. DOIs: **10.1021/acsomega.7b00125**, **10.3390/ma11122386**. (hou2024developmentofa pages 64-67)

The defensible practice is to condition and test **the actual powder lot in its actual sequence**: room-air loading, evacuation, argon backfill, thermal soak, and refill. Log hopper mass, inlet/outlet pressure, outlet temperature, motor torque/current, interruptions, and charge or adhesion if measurable. Electrically bond metal parts; assess PEEK and exposed insulating prints separately for charging and temperature. Locate the sensitive actuator and hopper away from radiative melt heating, use a replaceable heat-shielded metal nose, and avoid implying that a nominally vacuum-compatible stepper makes seals, bearings, lubricants, piezo bonds, cables, and powder-safe service automatically vacuum compatible. Powder transfer by augers has been investigated in vacuum soil-testing apparatus, but the retrieved sources do not validate **1–50 mg/s steadiness near molten metal after pump-down**. Thus vacuum-metallurgy addition practices and space-regolith conveyance establish feasibility of *operating mechanisms*, not this performance specification. (hou2024developmentofa pages 64-67)

## 5. Carrier-gas feeding versus local mechanical delivery

Blown-powder DED demonstrates the transport principle and flexible placement, but **1–50 mg/s equals only 0.06–3 g/min**. A retrieved DED example describes operation at **6 g/min = 100 mg/s**, already above the requested maximum; DED-process stability does not imply a measured low-rate, one-second-stable solids feed. A *pneumatically assisted microfeeder* is a more relevant scale example: Hou reports **0.7–21.2 g/h = 0.19–5.89 mg/s**, with RSD ranging approximately **<5% to <20% by material**; cohesive MCC reached **1.6–11 g/h = 0.44–3.06 mg/s** at **<5% reported RSD**, whereas difficult APAP required more gas and performed less well. In that apparatus the powder was **first metered mechanically**, then entrained: gas was not a substitute for a stable solids source. The cited comparison used, for example, **25 kPa and 5 L/min** for MCC versus **50 kPa and 10 L/min** for APAP at similar rates; those gas settings should *not* be transplanted into a small argon chamber. DOI for the dissertation: **10.48730/4gp7-jb67**. (hou2024developmentofa pages 137-143)

A small argon micro-jet may be attractive if the chamber can tolerate the **gas load**, recirculation, entrainment of particles away from the molten stream, and added gas-line, filter, and pressure-control hardware. Gas also changes particle impact position and may disturb the molten-metal stream. In a sealed chamber, a positive carrier-gas flow requires a corresponding exhaust or recirculation strategy; in an operating vacuum, ordinary atmospheric-pressure blown-powder correlations are not directly applicable. No reliable published **like-for-like cost** or **one-second fluctuation** comparison between a low-power 1–50 mg/s DED-style feeder and the proposed local vibrating feeder was obtained. Calling gas “cheaper” or “steadier” here would be speculative. (hou2024developmentofa pages 137-143)

## Recommended test sequence

1. **Define steadiness numerically:** measure delivered mass or particle counts in **0.1-, 1-, 5-, and 30-s windows**, with RSD, zero-flow fraction, pulse spectrum, and startup/refill behavior; report averaging explicitly. One-minute gravimetric RSD alone can conceal the defect of interest. (engisch2014lossinweightfeedingin pages 134-139, hou2024developmentofa pages 156-161)
2. **Separate geometry experiments:** first test the present flight with an open, short, accessible transition; then test multi-start phase spacing or altered last-flight geometry *without* adding a choke simultaneously. Measure discharged mass per turn and onset of backflow or torque rise. (dun2025parameteroptimizationof pages 11-12, dai2008biomassscrewfeeding pages 8-9)
3. **Prototype an independently vibrated final stage** with adjustable holdup and changeable bore, preferably thermally isolated from the melt. Establish the vibration-on/off curve, steady rate and dry-run emptying threshold **after evacuation and argon backfill** before judging its pulse-attenuation factor. Reserve at least one inter-pulse demand plus an experimentally measured non-dischargeable inventory; **300 mg** is only the theoretical *usable* 10-mg/s, 30-s minimum. (matsusaka1996microfeedingofa pages 5-11, lu2008ultrasoundassistedmicrofeedingof pages 2-4, hou2024developmentofa pages 64-67)
4. **Evaluate a small argon-assist jet in parallel** only if close, contact-free placement is essential and the chamber gas budget permits it. Compare powder *arriving at the molten stream*, not merely leaving the feeder. (hou2024developmentofa pages 137-143)

**Evidence boundary:** Published results establish screw-speed-dependent oscillations, the strong influence of outlet/flight geometry, and viable vibrating-capillary microfeeding. They do **not** determine which proposed geometry will produce a continuously uniform 1–50 mg/s stream of these granules in this evacuated-then-argon, heated apparatus; that decision requires phase-resolved, environment-matched testing. (engisch2014lossinweightfeedingin pages 134-139, matsusaka1996microfeedingofa pages 5-11, dun2025parameteroptimizationof pages 11-12, hou2024developmentofa pages 156-161)

Concave flights, cut flights, ribbon/shaftless flights, spring augers, discharge screens, and choke tubes alter clearance, filling, shear, and opportunities for bypass; the retrieved evidence does **not** establish numerical pulse attenuation or spectra for a matched comparison among these choices at the requested rates. Removing the 8-mm core might increase free space and reduce core-contact shear, but an open axial channel can also create an uncontrolled gravity-flow path when tilted outlet-down; it is not intrinsically a smoother meter. A screen can break lumps yet add an arching surface. These require direct phase-resolved tests, not a claimed percentage improvement. (dun2025parameteroptimizationof pages 11-12, dun2025parameteroptimizationof pages 6-8, dun2025parameteroptimizationof pages 9-11, hou2024developmentofa pages 156-161)

**Chokes and extensions serve another purpose.** Dai and Grace studied tapered, choked, and extended biomass screws primarily to create sealing plugs; longer chokes increased torque and blockage risk. Reported torque fluctuated roughly **10–30 N·m** in their much larger apparatus, and suggested biomass choke lengths were **4–10 screw pitches**—neither is a suitable design target for this microfeeder. DOI: **10.1016/j.powtec.2007.10.033**. An extended *unflighted* section is not automatically a pulse damper: if it packs, its wall resistance and eventual plug release may create larger, slower bursts. (dai2008biomassscrewfeeding pages 4-6, dai2008biomassscrewfeeding pages 8-9)

## 3. Converging nose, arching, and the proposed smooth tube

**Do not reduce downstream pocket capacity below what arrives unless there is a deliberate escape, increased downstream transport speed, or a demonstrated compressible plug process.** For the same core and filling assumptions, the local per-turn geometric capacity scales approximately as \((D^2-d_c^2)p\); both a smaller \(D\) and a tighter \(p\) drive it downward. Conserving throughput then requires less upstream filling, more downstream rpm—which a one-piece rotor cannot provide—or densification, slip/backflow, and rising axial stress. Dai and Grace explicitly calculated tapered-pocket volumes and corresponding compacted densities and torque; their work supports treating a taper as a **compression section**, not automatically a stable nozzle. A larger-diameter feed reservoir followed by a separately controlled smaller final meter avoids requiring equal volumetric capacity in a single rotating part. DOI: **10.1016/j.powtec.2007.10.033**. (dai2008biomassscrewfeeding pages 4-6)

For relatively dry, noncohesive spheres, roughly **five particle diameters** is a common empirical outlet scale above which clogging becomes infrequent; some particles require ratios approaching **ten**, and whether a truly clog-free critical diameter exists is debated. This is **not** a safe outlet design criterion for cohesive fines or an annular gap: powder strength, wall friction, consolidation history, and the *minimum local clearance* also matter. The proposed **3-mm annular clearance is 20–60 particle diameters** for 150–50 µm individual granules, respectively; this is reassuring only against simple few-grain geometric arches. An agglomerate, a progressively packed taper, a poorly defined local clearance around the conical tip, or fines can still stop flow. DOI: Zuriguel’s review **10.4279/PIP.060014**; archival version **10.48550/arXiv.1412.5806**. (ahmed2018outflowandclogging pages 6-10, zuriguel2014invitedreviewclogging pages 1-2)

Vibration can destabilize an arch and lower effective wall friction, but it does **not** guarantee continuous discharge. Matsusaka’s capillary reached a critical acceleration of approximately **1.1–3.9 m/s²**—fitted at **2.5 m/s²** in those experiments—and its maximum flow scaled roughly as **bore diameter^2–2.5**. Rate rose with frequency and then saturated; sufficiently free-flowing powder could continue discharging after vibration stopped in a larger bore. Their authors also identified tube slope, packing fraction, powder properties, and wall interaction as governing variables without providing a universal predictive flow law. In vibrating clogged hoppers, arch lifetimes can have long tails, so a seemingly effective shaker may still permit extended outages. DOIs: **10.1016/S0921-8831(08)60509-9** and **10.1103/PhysRevLett.120.198002**. (matsusaka1996microfeedingofa pages 5-11)

**A smooth tube is a low-pass filter only conditionally.** Let \(M\) be powder held between screw and outlet. Conservation gives \(dM/dt=q_{\rm screw}-q_{\rm exit}\). If the short tube retains a *persistent* bed and its vibration-controlled outlet rate responds mainly to its slowly changing inventory—not directly to incoming impacts—then its residence-time distribution can attenuate the screw-frequency component. If the tube empties between pulses, the output must also stop between pulses; if it fills until an arch collapses, output becomes intermittent instead. At **10 mg/s and a 30-s gap**, at least **300 mg of *usable* holdup** must remain deliverable between incoming pulses. At **1–50 mg/s**, the same 30-s interval represents **30–1,500 mg**. Those masses, not merely bore diameter, set the minimum buffer requirement. For any proposed narrow tube, measure both the *inventory at which discharge ceases* and the attainable vibration-limited discharge against bore, tube length, inclination, amplitude, frequency, wall finish, and powder condition. The literature supports tunable vibrating-capillary discharge, **not** a measured transfer function or demonstrated 30-s smoothing for the specified screw. (matsusaka1996microfeedingofa pages 5-11, lu2008ultrasoundassistedmicrofeedingof pages 2-4, lu2008ultrasoundassistedmicrofeedingof pages 4-6)

## 4. Dry argon, prior evacuation, and thermal integration

Drying can remove capillary liquid bridges yet also reduce charge leakage; these competing effects make “argon improves flow” and “vacuum improves flow” unreliable generalizations. In controlled glass-bead experiments, fluorinated surfaces acquired **5.89-fold** the tribocharge of hydroxylated surfaces against stainless steel; increasing humidity from 25% to 75% RH accelerated charge relaxation **1.6-fold** for one surface chemistry and **12.2-fold** for another. These are *surface-specific electrostatic measurements*, not a correction factor for this metal feeder. Fine metal-powder studies likewise link moisture uptake and fines to higher cohesion. Dry argon changes humidity and possibly charging relative to room air; evacuation and heating can desorb water and gas, while contact charging, compaction, and thermal drift may change the opposite way. DOIs: **10.1021/acsomega.7b00125**, **10.3390/ma11122386**. (hou2024developmentofa pages 64-67)

The defensible practice is to condition and test **the actual powder lot in its actual sequence**: room-air loading, evacuation, argon backfill, thermal soak, and refill. Log hopper mass, inlet/outlet pressure, outlet temperature, motor torque/current, interruptions, and charge or adhesion if measurable. Electrically bond metal parts; assess PEEK and exposed insulating prints separately for charging and temperature. Locate the sensitive actuator and hopper away from radiative melt heating, use a replaceable heat-shielded metal nose, and avoid implying that a nominally vacuum-compatible stepper makes seals, bearings, lubricants, piezo bonds, cables, and powder-safe service automatically vacuum compatible. Powder transfer by augers has been investigated in vacuum soil-testing apparatus, but the retrieved sources do not validate **1–50 mg/s steadiness near molten metal after pump-down**. Thus vacuum-metallurgy addition practices and space-regolith conveyance establish feasibility of *operating mechanisms*, not this performance specification. (hou2024developmentofa pages 64-67)

## 5. Carrier-gas feeding versus local mechanical delivery

Blown-powder DED demonstrates the transport principle and flexible placement, but **1–50 mg/s equals only 0.06–3 g/min**. A retrieved DED example describes operation at **6 g/min = 100 mg/s**, already above the requested maximum; DED-process stability does not imply a measured low-rate, one-second-stable solids feed. A *pneumatically assisted microfeeder* is a more relevant scale example: Hou reports **0.7–21.2 g/h = 0.19–5.89 mg/s**, with RSD ranging approximately **<5% to <20% by material**; cohesive MCC reached **1.6–11 g/h = 0.44–3.06 mg/s** at **<5% reported RSD**, whereas difficult APAP required more gas and performed less well. In that apparatus the powder was **first metered mechanically**, then entrained: gas was not a substitute for a stable solids source. The cited comparison used, for example, **25 kPa and 5 L/min** for MCC versus **50 kPa and 10 L/min** for APAP at similar rates; those gas settings should *not* be transplanted into a small argon chamber. DOI for the dissertation: **10.48730/4gp7-jb67**. (hou2024developmentofa pages 137-143)

A small argon micro-jet may be attractive if the chamber can tolerate the **gas load**, recirculation, entrainment of particles away from the molten stream, and added gas-line, filter, and pressure-control hardware. Gas also changes particle impact position and may disturb the molten-metal stream. In a sealed chamber, a positive carrier-gas flow requires a corresponding exhaust or recirculation strategy; in an operating vacuum, ordinary atmospheric-pressure blown-powder correlations are not directly applicable. No reliable published **like-for-like cost** or **one-second fluctuation** comparison between a low-power 1–50 mg/s DED-style feeder and the proposed local vibrating feeder was obtained. Calling gas “cheaper” or “steadier” here would be speculative. (hou2024developmentofa pages 137-143)

## Recommended test sequence

1. **Define steadiness numerically:** measure delivered mass or particle counts in **0.1-, 1-, 5-, and 30-s windows**, with RSD, zero-flow fraction, pulse spectrum, and startup/refill behavior; report averaging explicitly. One-minute gravimetric RSD alone can conceal the defect of interest. (engisch2014lossinweightfeedingin pages 134-139, hou2024developmentofa pages 156-161)
2. **Separate geometry experiments:** first test the present flight with an open, short, accessible transition; then test multi-start phase spacing or altered last-flight geometry *without* adding a choke simultaneously. Measure discharged mass per turn and onset of backflow or torque rise. (dun2025parameteroptimizationof pages 11-12, dai2008biomassscrewfeeding pages 8-9)
3. **Prototype an independently vibrated final stage** with adjustable holdup and changeable bore, preferably thermally isolated from the melt. Establish the vibration-on/off curve, steady rate and dry-run emptying threshold **after evacuation and argon backfill** before judging its pulse-attenuation factor. Reserve at least one inter-pulse demand plus an experimentally measured non-dischargeable inventory; **300 mg** is only the theoretical *usable* 10-mg/s, 30-s minimum. (matsusaka1996microfeedingofa pages 5-11, lu2008ultrasoundassistedmicrofeedingof pages 2-4, hou2024developmentofa pages 64-67)
4. **Evaluate a small argon-assist jet in parallel** only if close, contact-free placement is essential and the chamber gas budget permits it. Compare powder *arriving at the molten stream*, not merely leaving the feeder. (hou2024developmentofa pages 137-143)

**Evidence boundary:** Published results establish screw-speed-dependent oscillations, the strong influence of outlet/flight geometry, and viable vibrating-capillary microfeeding. They do **not** determine which proposed geometry will produce a continuously uniform 1–50 mg/s stream of these granules in this evacuated-then-argon, heated apparatus; that decision requires phase-resolved, environment-matched testing. (engisch2014lossinweightfeedingin pages 134-139, matsusaka1996microfeedingofa pages 5-11, dun2025parameteroptimizationof pages 11-12, hou2024developmentofa pages 156-161)
