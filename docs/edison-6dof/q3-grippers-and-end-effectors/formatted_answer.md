**Custom gripper and end-effector design for a low-cost 6-DOF arm in a materials self-driving laboratory — state of the art, design methodology (including generative CAD / generative systems design), and the open publishable gaps.**

This is the third in a sequential series of high-effort literature scans for a university materials-science self-driving lab (SDL). Please treat the established findings below as given context and do **not** re-derive them; build on them.

## Established context from the two prior scans

Scan 1 (arms in SDLs) established: every deployed arm-based SDL uses an expensive industrial arm (KUKA KMR-iiwa at Liverpool; ABB YuMi IRB 14000 for PXRD; Franka Research 3 for vision-guided powder scooping, Radulov et al. *Digital Discovery* 2026; Denso-Wave COBOTTA at NIMS). No published SDL runs on a sub-$5k arm. In every one of these systems the arm is a **logistics/transfer layer**, not the chemistry, and the end-effector is **custom or heavily modified**: a custom Festo HGPLE-14 gripper with vial/rack fingers (KUKA), modified ABB SmartGrippers (YuMi), a Robotiq 85F holding a spatula/scoop (Franka), and a **custom 3D-printed gripper** (COBOTTA). Reliability is the binding constraint and is essentially unreported: Ada (UBC/AC) implies ~97.5% per-sample success (~1 run-ending failure per 40 samples); gripper slip is named in the failure taxonomy; no deployed SDL paper reports MTBI for the arm or the end-effector specifically.

Scan 2 (metrology and reliability methods) established how to characterize arm pose accuracy without a laser tracker (ISO 9283 / ASME B89.4.22 adapted to ballbar, dial-indicator, and vision-based methods), thermal-drift warm-up protocols, and the cycle counts needed to distinguish 97.5% from 99.8% per-operation reliability.

## Our situation

- **Arm:** an AgileX **PiPER** 6-DOF arm (~US$2.5k class, ~1.5 kg rated payload, manufacturer-claimed ~0.1 mm repeatability that we regard as unverified), with a **custom wrist camera** we attach ourselves — most likely a Raspberry Pi HQ camera, chosen specifically because its fixed C/CS-mount lens does not shift like an autofocus module.
- **Fabrication on hand:** Bambu Lab A1 mini FDM printers (possibly a second one dedicated to end-effector iteration), so a print-test-revise cycle on a gripper is hours, not weeks. FDM in PLA/PETG/ASA/PA-CF; no metal printing, no CNC in-loop.
- **Objects the end-effector must handle**, which is the crux of this question: SEM stubs (~12.7 mm pin stubs); scintillation and HPLC **vials, both empty and filled** (mass and CG change between the two); SBS-footprint **well plates**; **aluminum DSC-style crucibles and their lids, including a press-fit lid step**; an **atomizer transfer step** moving loose metal powder; 3D-printed **tensile/impact coupons** that must seat in a test fixture; and loose **cohesive metal powders** (AlSi10Mg, Si) handled with a scoop/trough.
- **Related in-house systems:** `powder-doser` (a pure-mechanical gantry-mounted tilting trough — no actuators on the bucket, a fixed cam ramp does the tilting, a design that an earlier Edison review materially corrected); `tensegrity-optimization` (a closed BO loop through 3D print → drop test with *manual* specimen handling); an OT-2; a CubXL gantry; and **CADSmith**, our own multi-agent text-to-CAD system that writes CadQuery code, validates geometry against exact OpenCASCADE kernel measurements plus a vision-language-model judge over three-view renders, and refines in a closed loop (100% execution rate, 38× reduction in mean Chamfer Distance vs. zero-shot on a 100-prompt benchmark).

A striking finding is that **no deployed SDL paper reports a per-operation grasp-success rate, cycle life, or mean time between interventions (MTBI) for the end-effector specifically.** The Liverpool mobile robotic chemist completed 688 experiments over eight days and A-Lab ran 355 consecutive experiments in 17 days, but neither reports the denominator of failed handling operations, retries, or human interventions required (moran2026theriseof pages 6-9, moran2026theriseof pages 19-24). The Ada system reports 10 µm arm repeatability and 5 µL liquid-transfer accuracy, but no end-effector reliability metric (moran2026theriseof pages 4-6). This systematic absence of reliability reporting is itself a publishable finding and a gap.

### 1.2 Labware-Specific End-Effectors

The most systematic treatment of laboratory-container geometry is Zwirnmann et al. (IROS 2023), who developed a taxonomy of biolaboratory liquid containers classified by weight (up to 1.5 kg), width, diameter, and lid type, then designed monolithic dual-extrusion fingers supporting pinch grasps (tested at 20 N) and power grasps (tested at 100 N) for containers ranging from 6 mm micro/cryotubes through Petri dishes, cell-culture flasks, and 96-well plates (zwirnmann2023towardsflexiblebiolaboratory pages 1-2, zwirnmann2023towardsflexiblebiolaboratory pages 7-8, zwirnmann2023towardsflexiblebiolaboratory pages 2-3). The Liverpool KUKA uses a Festo HGPLE gripper with custom fingers for 10 mL Agilent GC vials and racks, supporting both top-down and sideways grasps (burger2023amobilerobotic pages 52-56).

**For DSC crucibles and press-fit lids, no published robotic handling system was found.** Piro et al. (2021) explicitly chose a screw-on lid design over press-fitting for fluoride-salt DSC crucibles, citing fabrication reliability. This is a clear open gap. **SEM stub handling, atomizer-to-vessel powder transfer, and tensile/impact coupon seating in test fixtures are also absent from the published SDL literature.**

### 1.3 Compliant and Underactuated Grippers

Passive compliance is used in the Zwirnmann taxonomy fingers through dual-material (rigid/soft) construction and in the InstaGrasp design through TPU tendons that survived more than 86,000 stress cycles (doloi2025democratizingselfdrivinglabs pages 25-27). Fin Ray effect structures are referenced in the broader gripper literature as underactuated alternatives, but no SDL-specific quantitative data on their performance was found (zwirnmann2023towardsflexiblebiolaboratory pages 1-2).

**No published study quantifies the system-level substitution of finger or fixture compliance for arm pose precision in laboratory tasks** — that is, no paper demonstrates that a specific amount of compliant capture envelope compensates for a measured amount of arm repeatability error to achieve a target insertion or seating success rate. Remote-center-of-compliance (RCC) devices, widely used in industrial peg-in-hole assembly, have no published application in laboratory automation. This absence is significant for the PiPER arm, whose 0.1 mm repeatability claim is unverified.

### 1.4 Tool Changers and Kinematic Couplings

Precision kinematic couplings (three-groove Maxwell and Kelvin configurations) achieve exceptional repeatability: Slocum reports sub-micron repeatability for silicon-nitride/steel systems over initial cycles, with degradation to approximately 10 µm for steel-on-steel systems after several hundred cycles due to fretting (slocum2010kinematiccouplingsa pages 3-4). Three-Vee configurations achieved 0.3–1.4 µm (3σ) radial and axial repeatability, and split-groove couplings achieved ±5 µm positioning error versus ±50 µm for dowel pins (kruis2016designanalysistesting pages 29-30). However, **no published lightweight tool changer designed for a sub-1.5 kg payload arm was found.** Commercial robotic tool changers (ATI, Stäubli) typically weigh 0.5–1.5 kg and are designed for 5–50 kg payload arms; at 1.5 kg total payload, a 0.5 kg changer consumes one-third of the capacity. This is a design opportunity.

### 1.5 Force/Tactile Sensing

GelSight-class vision-based tactile sensors are the most accessible option for low-cost tactile feedback. The GelSight Mini provides high-resolution contact geometry and can estimate three-axis forces with mean absolute error of approximately 4% up to 15 N when calibrated (ka2016threedimensionalcomputer pages 1-2). The DIGIT sensor costs approximately US$15–50 and can be customized with 3D-printed sensing surfaces. For the PiPER arm specifically, **motor-current-based force estimation is theoretically possible but unpublished for this hardware class**, and its resolution is likely limited by gear friction and cogging. A dedicated F/T sensor (BOTA SensONE or ATI Nano17 class) would cost US$1,500–5,000 and add 50–100 g, making it impractical for routine use but valuable for characterization.

### 1.6 Eye-in-Hand Camera Placement

Published low-cost vision-guided grasping systems report 85–93% success rates. A monocular system on a 5-DOF arm achieved 90% success with ±3 mm residual error after linear distortion compensation, with failures concentrated at field-of-view edges where errors exceeded 5 mm (zhou2026designandkinematic pages 1-2, zhou2026designandkinematic pages 6-8). Eye-in-hand configurations typically operate at 15–20 cm working distance (sulaiman2026multimodalcontrolof pages 13-15). **No published work uses a Raspberry Pi HQ camera with a fixed C/CS-mount lens for robotic manipulation.** The fixed-focus approach avoids autofocus-induced intrinsics drift but constrains the depth of field; at f/1.6 with a 6 mm lens at 100 mm working distance, depth of field is approximately ±5 mm, which may be insufficient for the full approach trajectory without refocusing strategy or stopping down the aperture. This is an extrapolation, not a cited finding.

### 1.7 Powder-Specific End-Effectors

Powder handling in SDLs is dominated by commercial systems (Chemspeed Powder Dose) with proprietary designs (burger2023amobilerobotic pages 52-56). **No open-source, published powder scoop or spatula design includes quantified cross-contamination data, cleaning protocol efficacy, or triboelectric charging characterization.** MATTERIX demonstrates virtual powder weighing and scooping using Isaac Sim's position-based dynamics particle system, but cohesive-powder behavior (adhesion, caking, electrostatic clumping) is explicitly unvalidated (darvish2025matterixtowarda pages 15-16, darvish2025matterixtowarda pages 5-7, darvish2025matterixtowarda pages 7-9). The user's existing `powder-doser` with its cam-actuated tilting trough represents an unusual in-house data point that the published literature does not cover.

## 2. Generative CAD and Generative Systems Design — What Is Real

### 2.1 Topology Optimization and Generative Design for End-Effectors

Fit2Form (Ha et al., 2020) remains the clearest example of object-specific generative gripper design with physical validation. It maps target object geometry to parallel-jaw finger forms through a learned fitness network, generates fingers using TSDF representations, 3D-prints them, and tests them on a WSG50 gripper with a protocol involving 5 N grasp, 15 cm lift, shaking, ~30 N poking, and rotational perturbation. All four simulation-discovered grasp strategies (relaxed imprints, single- and double-sided cages, dynamic hooks) transferred to reality (ha2021fit2form3dgenerative pages 8-10). However, Fit2Form does not optimize material properties, gripper kinematics, or control policy, and was not demonstrated on laboratory objects.

Co-Design of Soft Gripper with Neural Physics (Yi et al., 2025) jointly optimizes block-wise finger stiffness and grasp pose using a differentiable neural surrogate, then fabricates optimized designs by 3D printing structural variants. Optimized grippers outperformed baselines in hardware tests (yi2025codesignofsoft pages 1-3). Robot-Aware Computational Design of Passive Grippers (Omaisan, 2026) goes further in engineering analysis — incorporating contact screening, retention geometry, robot-aware collision sweeps, FEA, and topology optimization — but physical validation (fit, load, shake, 100-cycle tests) was explicitly pending at publication (omaisan2609robotawarecomputational pages 9-11).

### 2.2 Design for Additive Manufacturing: FDM Gripper Constraints

FDM-printed Tough PLA achieved up to 42,000 cycles at 45% of UTS with optimized grid infill, while mesh modifiers improved fatigue life by more than 40% (alagheband2025enhancingstructuralperformance pages 39-43, alagheband2025enhancingstructuralperformance pages 9-12). Layer-by-layer fabrication produces weaker interlayer bonds, and filament orientation can reduce mechanical strength by up to 90% relative to injection-molded material (alagheband2025enhancingstructuralperformance pages 12-16). **Fatigue data for PETG, ASA, and PA-CF in compliant-mechanism or flexure-gripper geometries is sparse to absent in the retrieved literature.** PETG creep data exists (Stepped Iso-Stress Method with <5% deviation from conventional tests), but cycle-life characterization for repeated flexion is not published (alagheband2025enhancingstructuralperformance pages 12-16). These data gaps directly affect design decisions for FDM grippers on the PiPER arm.

### 2.3 LLM/VLM-Driven CAD Generation

The text-to-CAD field has matured rapidly around CadQuery as a target representation. The key benchmark is Text2CAD-Bench (Wang et al., 2026): 600 examples across four complexity levels, with average code growing from 7.9 lines at L1 to 70.7 lines at L3, and performance dropping sharply on advanced topology (wang2026text2cadbenchabenchmark pages 8-9, wang2026text2cadbenchabenchmark pages 12-14). IterCAD (Hu et al., 2026) achieves state-of-the-art results through iterative visual/geometric feedback and geometry-aware RL: 0.64% invalid ratio and 10.92 mean Chamfer Distance on Text2CAD, versus substantially higher invalidity for single-shot approaches (hu2026itercadaniterative pages 8-10). CME-CAD uses multi-expert RL for CadQuery generation. CADIR provides a cross-backend editable intermediate representation for assemblies and constraints (liu2026cadiracrossbackend pages 8-8).

**Where does CADSmith sit?** Its reported 100% execution rate and 38× mean-CD reduction on a 100-prompt internal benchmark are competitive with IterCAD's numbers, but a rigorous head-to-head requires: (1) evaluation on Text2CAD-Bench's public 600-example set under its prescribed zero-/few-shot conditions; (2) reporting not only execution rate and Chamfer Distance but also IoU, invalidity rate, and the feature-level intent metrics Q1–Q5; (3) matched model budgets, token costs, and wall-clock time; (4) statistical confidence intervals. Most importantly, all current text-to-CAD benchmarks measure geometric similarity to a reference shape, not functional fitness for a task — a limitation that CADSmith's OpenCASCADE measurements and VLM judging partially address but do not resolve without physical validation.

Current text-to-CAD agents optimize digital similarity without physical grounding. MATTERIX simulates laboratory workflows but does not generate gripper designs. This gap is the highest-value publication opportunity.

### 2.5 MATTERIX and Digital Twins

MATTERIX (Darvish et al., *Nature Computational Science* 2026) provides a GPU-accelerated, Isaac Sim-based digital-twin framework with USD assets, physics and semantics engines, perception via FoundationPose, and hierarchical workflow planning (darvish2025matterixtowarda pages 1-2, darvish2025matterixtowarda pages 2-5). It supports reusable manipulation skills, multi-robot coordination, and sim-to-real deployment. Real-world demonstrations achieved 75% success for pick-and-place and 90% for pouring (darvish2025matterixtowarda pages 11-14). Powder and granular media are represented as particle systems with position-based dynamics, and virtual powder weighing/scooping is demonstrated (darvish2025matterixtowarda pages 15-16, darvish2025matterixtowarda pages 7-9). However, **cohesive-powder adhesion, triboelectric charging, and interparticle cohesion are not validated** — the particle models are position-based dynamics without demonstrated calibration against real AlSi10Mg or Si powder behavior (darvish2025matterixtowarda pages 5-7, darvish2025matterixtowarda pages 14-15). For powder-handling end-effector design iteration, MATTERIX would serve as a plausible environment for rigid-body collision and reachability screening but not as a reliable predictor of powder transfer, adhesion, or cross-contamination.

### 2.6 Generative Systems Design for Laboratory Layout

**No published system performs automated generative synthesis of laboratory workstation layouts** with reachability optimization, cycle-time minimization, and collision-free placement. The laboratory automation literature contains conceptual architectural frameworks (Axiomatic Design for lab architectures, tower-based configurations, modular track systems) but no computational layout-generation tools analogous to factory-floor layout optimization or architecture search (najmabadi2006newlaboratoryautomation pages 32-36, najmabadi2006newlaboratoryautomation pages 168-171, baquero2014designconsiderationsin pages 39-43). MATTERIX supports manual environment assembly and feasibility analysis but does not automate layout generation. This is an open gap, though it is a larger-scope project than end-effector design.

## 3. Gaps, Crowded Areas, and Ranked Project Recommendations

### 3.1 What Is Crowded vs. What Is Open

**Crowded areas** (a small lab should not enter as a primary contribution):
- General-purpose text-to-CAD benchmarking (Text2CAD-Bench, RealCADBench, DeepCAD, Fusion360 Gallery — many well-resourced groups)
- Dexterous multi-finger hand design for general robotics (large labs at CMU, MIT, Stanford, Columbia)
- GelSight/DIGIT tactile sensor hardware development (Adelson group, Lepora group)
- General grasp synthesis (Dex-Net, Contact-GraspNet, GraspNet-1Billion — saturated with incremental improvements)

**Open areas** with publishable opportunity for a small materials-SDL lab:
- End-effector reliability measurement and failure taxonomy for SDLs (essentially unreported)
- Physically closed generative end-effector design loop for laboratory objects
- Compliance-vs-precision quantification for low-cost arms on lab tasks
- Lightweight 3D-printed tool changers for sub-2 kg payload arms
- Robotic DSC crucible press-fit lid handling
- Open-source powder scoop with quantified cross-contamination
- Fixed-focus wrist camera for last-centimeter laboratory servoing

### 3.3 Notes on CADSmith De-Risking

Projects 1, 4, and 5 are **strongly de-risked** by CADSmith's existing CadQuery generation, OpenCASCADE kernel validation, and VLM render judging. The closed-loop generative design project (Rank 1) is the highest-leverage use of CADSmith because it extends the system from geometric similarity toward functional fitness — a transition no competing system has demonstrated. Projects 2, 3, 6, and 7 benefit from CADSmith for rapid fixture and tool generation but require substantial new metrology, force-sensing, and materials-characterization infrastructure.

### 3.4 Explicit Absences and Extrapolations

The following statements are **not in the literature** and represent extrapolations or confirmed absences:
- No published SDL reports MTBI for the arm or end-effector.
- No published study quantifies how much compliant capture envelope a finger or fixture needs to compensate for a specific arm repeatability error on a laboratory peg-in-hole task.
- No lightweight 3D-printed kinematic tool changer designed for a sub-1.5 kg arm exists in the literature.
- No robotic DSC crucible press-fit lid system has been published with insertion force, compliance, or success rate data.
- No published powder scoop for an SDL includes cross-contamination carryover measurements.
- No published system has used a Raspberry Pi HQ fixed-focus camera for robotic manipulation visual servoing.
- The claim that PLA/PETG flexure grippers can survive >10,000 grip cycles at laboratory loads is plausible from the 42,000-cycle Tough PLA fatigue data (alagheband2025enhancingstructuralperformance pages 39-43) but has not been demonstrated in a gripper geometry at operational strain amplitudes.
- MATTERIX's particle-based powder representation is stated to support granular media (darvish2025matterixtowarda pages 15-16) but its fidelity for cohesive AlSi10Mg or Si has not been validated — this is an extrapolation boundary, not a confirmed capability.
