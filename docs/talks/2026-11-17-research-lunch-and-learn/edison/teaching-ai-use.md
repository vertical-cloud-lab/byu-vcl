**Question.** What approaches are most effective for teaching university researchers (faculty, staff and graduate students) to use generative AI and AI agents productively in their research and teaching? Summarize the empirical evidence (studies, surveys, controlled trials) on training formats such as short talks or demonstrations, hands-on workshops, one-on-one onboarding or office hours, peer mentoring and communities of practice, and worked examples. What barriers to adoption are reported, and which interventions led to sustained use rather than one-time experimentation?

# Teaching university researchers to use generative AI and AI agents

## Bottom line

The best-supported approach is **not a stand-alone AI lecture**. It is a short, task-specific introduction followed by guided practice on participants’ own research or teaching work, examples they can adapt, explicit checking of outputs, and continuing access to peers or specialist support. This is a synthesis of convergent—but mostly noncomparative—studies, **not** a proven ranking of training formats. The clearest randomized evidence shows that even brief instruction can improve *initial* adoption and task performance; university staff studies suggest that spaced practice can increase reported use. Evidence that any format produces **durable, independently measured use** remains scarce. (chen2603trainingfortechnology pages 1-7, møgelvang2025generativeaiin pages 13-16, cho2025integratingtasktechnologyfit pages 4-5, bulbuk2026apioneeringfaculty pages 5-7)

## What each training format can—and cannot—claim

**Short talks and demonstrations: useful entry points, not established retention interventions.** In a randomized experiment involving 164 undergraduate and graduate law students, optional LLM access accompanied by a brief instructional video and quiz increased use on a legal-analysis examination from **26.3% to 41.4%** relative to unguided access (*p*=0.044). Mean examination scores were **0.27 grade points** higher (*p*=0.027), and statements of applicable rules were more accurate (*p*=0.014). This establishes that targeted instruction can improve adoption and performance *on the immediate task*; it does not establish sustained use, apply directly to faculty development, or isolate the effects of a demonstration from those of the quiz. Unguided access alone did not improve performance in this experiment. (chen2603trainingfortechnology pages 1-7, chen2603trainingfortechnology pages 17-24)

**Hands-on, spaced workshops: the clearest faculty-and-staff evidence for increased use.** At a Norwegian university college, approximately 200 educators and other staff attended an introductory course with three three-hour online seminars spread across about two months. Participants tried AI in their own practice and presented their experience to peers. Among **81 people with matched surveys**, mean *self-reported* use frequency increased from **3.39** (“more than once monthly”) before the course to **4.23** (“once a week”) at its final seminar (*p*<0.001; reported effect-size *r*=0.635). The change is more informative than satisfaction alone, but there was no untrained comparison group, usage log, or follow-up *after* the course. (møgelvang2025generativeaiin pages 5-8, møgelvang2025generativeaiin pages 8-10, møgelvang2025generativeaiin pages 13-16, møgelvang2025generativeaiin pages 20-23)

A five-session online workshop pilot with **10 medical faculty** used facilitator examples, experimentation, critique, and reflection to produce draft teaching materials. Participants reported confidence and intended applications, but only **five** answered its two-week follow-up; instructional artifacts were not formally assessed and teaching practice was not observed. Similarly, an Ecuadorian program involving **184 surveyed university teachers** documented practical workshops and collaborative activities, but did not establish which component changed practice or whether use persisted. These are encouraging feasibility findings, not controlled evidence that workshops outperform other formats. (anand2026designandevaluation pages 5-6, anand2026designandevaluation pages 12-13, cordero2024integrationofgenerative pages 5-7, cordero2024integrationofgenerative pages 7-9)

**One-to-one onboarding and office hours: promising for local problems, but no isolated effect estimate.** At a Ukrainian medical university, foundational **3–4-hour practical workshops** were supplemented with optional **60–90-minute individual consultations** on discipline-specific assignments and assessment problems; **127 of 211 workshop completers (60%)** took up consultation. That substantial uptake demonstrates demand for tailored help, not that consultation caused later adoption. The retrieved university studies do not provide a controlled comparison of office hours or one-to-one onboarding against workshops alone. (bulbuk2026apioneeringfaculty pages 2-4, bulbuk2026apioneeringfaculty pages 4-5)

**Peer mentoring and communities of practice: evidence for confidence and continuing engagement, less for individual long-term use.** A medical-educator community offered six one-hour sessions over 11 weeks, using a “see one, follow along/do one, share one” sequence on real educator micro-tasks. Of **30 attendees**, **13** answered a post-series survey. Their retrospectively assessed knowledge rose **1.31 points** (*p*<0.001); session attendance correlated with skill confidence (*r*=0.78) and intention to apply learning (*r*=0.72), **but not with likelihood of continued experimentation**. Participants identified regular meetups as helpful, yet actual post-series use was not measured. This is evidence for a workable peer-learning format, not proof of retention. (cho2025integratingtasktechnologyfit pages 1-2, cho2025integratingtasktechnologyfit pages 2-4, cho2025integratingtasktechnologyfit pages 4-5)

**Worked examples: strong practical rationale, limited component-specific testing.** The same community shared task-matched exemplars, prompt templates, and reusable workflows—including an exercise to build a Copilot agent. In the Ukrainian program, faculty developed **142 documented use cases**, 12 discipline-specific prompting guides, and 18 assessment rubrics. In a Swedish physiotherapy program, course champions first showed colleagues proposed assessment adaptations; faculty then revised their *own* courses in groups and received feedback. These examples made the work concrete and supported implementation, but none of the studies randomized participants to examples versus no examples. University language instructors interviewed in another study likewise requested scenarios connected to their existing teaching and assessment rather than ambiguous general guidance; that is a reported preference, not an effectiveness trial. (cho2025integratingtasktechnologyfit pages 2-4, bulbuk2026apioneeringfaculty pages 4-5, lindback2025structureddevelopmentof pages 2-4, kohnke2023exploringgenerativeartificial pages 6-8)

## Barriers that training must address

* **Accuracy and verification.** In a survey of **232 US academic STEM scientists**, **78%** identified misinformation as a leading concern. In a separate survey of **868 scientists who program**, perceived productivity was higher among some respondents with weaker software-development safeguards; the authors caution that accepting generated code without understanding, testing, or review can endanger scientific results. Training should therefore pair a useful research task with source checking, code tests, and explicit decisions about what must remain human-reviewed—not teach prompting as if fluent output were validated output. (arroyomachado2025generativeaiand pages 1-2, obrien2512morecodeless pages 1-3, obrien2512morecodeless pages 10-14)
* **Access, time, and fit to the job.** Half of respondents in the small medical-educator community cited lack of access to the necessary tool. University language instructors requested opportunities to try AI on their own materials and to discuss their concerns; the Ukrainian program’s pilot led organizers to increase hands-on time and add troubleshooting resources. These findings favor approved, accessible tools and discipline-specific practice over demonstrations that participants cannot reproduce at work. (cho2025integratingtasktechnologyfit pages 4-5, kohnke2023exploringgenerativeartificial pages 6-8, bulbuk2026apioneeringfaculty pages 2-4)
* **Privacy, integrity, and uncertain rules.** Academic scientists report concerns about sensitive data, fabricated citations, and integrity. The Ukrainian institution explicitly prohibited uploading sensitive medical data to public tools and required human verification of AI-assisted materials. A review of **116 US research-intensive universities** found sample syllabi at **56%** and sample AI-related curriculum activities at **50%**, but also warned that extensive guidance can impose substantial redesign work on instructors. Training needs a usable local policy and approved examples, not merely a list of tools. (arroyomachado2025generativeaiand pages 11-13, bulbuk2026apioneeringfaculty pages 4-5, bulbuk2026apioneeringfaculty pages 5-7, kohnke2023exploringgenerativeartificial pages 6-8)
* **Uneven starting points and confidence.** Among **760 academic-library employees**, **41.79%** reported never using generative AI at work and **28.01%** used it less than monthly; **69.76%** reported low confidence troubleshooting AI tools. This supports beginner-friendly, repeatable onboarding and access to help, while providing no causal evidence that a particular training format resolves the gap. (lo2024evaluatingailiteracy pages 1-4, lo2024evaluatingailiteracy pages 10-14)

## Which interventions show more than one-time experimentation?

The **strongest evidence of increasing individual use during a program** is the Norwegian matched pre/post result, but its endpoint was the last seminar—not a later retention test. In Spain, **180 university teachers** answered surveys across two academic years during a wider institutional AI initiative. Mean self-rated awareness increased from **4.82 to 7.06**, and **112 of 155 trained respondents (72.3%)** said they had applied what they learned. The reported decline in “no AI use” from **54.38% to 10.42%**, and rise in teaching use from **16.59% to 43.40%**, are **shares of multiple-choice responses, not percentages of individual teachers**. With no control group and a package including certification, workshops, and guidance, the changes cannot be assigned to a particular training activity. (møgelvang2025generativeaiin pages 13-16, vivasurias2026analysisofteachers’ pages 11-12, vivasurias2026analysisofteachers’ pages 12-14, vivasurias2026analysisofteachers’ pages 17-19)

The **strongest evidence of institutional implementation** comes from programs that made participants produce or change something after initial instruction. In Ukraine, **211 of 732 faculty** completed foundational training; **127** proceeded to individual consultations, **89** helped develop methodological materials, and the continuing community reached **156 members**. Faculty documented teaching and research-supervision applications, while two deployed institutional assistants recorded **more than 3,200 interactions in their first three months**. Those interactions concern deployed student-facing services; they **cannot be attributed uniquely to trained faculty or to workshops**. In Sweden, a lecture plus two-day collaborative workshop, course champions, worked course reviews, and later status meetings yielded implemented assessment and AI-use-guidance changes across a physiotherapy curriculum within **four months**. Neither case measured controlled, long-term individual retention. The Ukrainian program’s engagement and resource-development metrics are also displayed in its Figure 2. (bulbuk2026apioneeringfaculty pages 4-5, bulbuk2026apioneeringfaculty pages 5-7, bulbuk2026apioneeringfaculty media 37f3e137, lindback2025structureddevelopmentof pages 2-4, lindback2025structureddevelopmentof pages 4-5)

**Practical conclusion.** For faculty, staff, and graduate researchers, offer a brief demonstration of a *specific approved task*, then have each participant carry out and verify a real task; provide editable examples, a route to discipline-specific consultation, and recurring peer sessions where completed workflows and failures can be discussed. This design is consistent with the best available intervention and implementation evidence. It should be evaluated against a meaningful standard—verified research or teaching outputs and tool-use measures at **three, six, and twelve months**, compared where feasible with a talk-only or access-only group—because current studies rarely establish that short-term enthusiasm becomes sustained, productive use. Agent-specific training evidence is thinner still: a community pilot included an agent-building session, and an institutional program deployed assistants, but neither isolated agent-training effectiveness or demonstrated durable researcher adoption of agents. (chen2603trainingfortechnology pages 1-7, møgelvang2025generativeaiin pages 13-16, cho2025integratingtasktechnologyfit pages 2-4, bulbuk2026apioneeringfaculty pages 4-5, bulbuk2026apioneeringfaculty pages 5-7, anand2026designandevaluation pages 12-13)
