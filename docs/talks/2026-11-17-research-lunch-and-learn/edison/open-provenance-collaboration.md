**Question.** How do transparent, publicly visible research workflows (open notebooks, public issue trackers, and AI-assisted work with recorded prompts and outputs) affect reproducibility, accountability, trust and collaboration between research groups? Include evidence on AI-assisted or literature-based discovery of experts and collaborators, and on how such discovery leads to new collaborations.

# Transparent research workflows and collaboration

**Overall assessment.** Public notebooks, visible issue discussions, and preserved AI interactions make research easier to *inspect, challenge, continue, and attribute*. They can expose experimental details and decisions that a finished paper omits, while giving researchers outside the originating group a way to contribute before publication. The strongest evidence is for these mechanisms and for particular collaborations they enabled; there is much less controlled evidence that visibility itself increases replication rates or measured trust. Public access is also a separate choice from using a digital notebook or GitHub: a restricted repository does not provide the same opportunity for outside scrutiny. (schapira2019openlaboratorynotebooks pages 3-5, chen2025githubisan pages 2-6)

## Reproducibility and accountability

**Open notebooks expose the experimental record, including failures.** The Structural Genomics Consortium’s open-notebook initiative posts protocols, underlying data, and positive *and negative* results alongside explanations of the experiment’s rationale. Its Zenodo records are dated, citable by DOI, and preserved through appended versions rather than silently replacing published records; the project also archives its website to GitHub and the Internet Archive. The inspected example record visibly contains a date, DOI, protocol and data preview. These features let another group examine what was done and when, identify unsuccessful approaches, and attempt or critique the experiment without waiting for a conventional paper. They establish traceability, **not proof that an independent replication succeeded**. (schapira2019openlaboratorynotebooks pages 3-5, schapira2019openlaboratorynotebooks media ddfd8a41)

Open Source Malaria illustrates the additional value of testing across groups. Its published synthesis details point to the electronic laboratory notebook; researchers reassessed original antimalarial hits at **three institutions**, using different assays and controls to examine activity, interassay variation, and possible bias from already-public results. A community suggestion improved a synthetic procedure, and an independent undergraduate laboratory cohort supplied compounds for testing. Those are observable instances of external input and cross-institutional checking, although the paper does not estimate how much openness, as opposed to the collaborators’ other resources, improved reproducibility. (williamson2016opensourcedrug pages 3-5)

**An issue tracker can connect the reasoning to the work product.** In a molecular-biology laboratory’s reported GitHub workflow, an experiment begins as a numbered issue recording its rationale and design. Comments retain troubleshooting, intermediate figures, interpretations and decisions; labels, assignees and milestones identify status and responsibility. Researchers even put issue numbers on sample tubes, linking physical material to discussions and files. Closed, paused and abandoned issues can preserve decisions that otherwise disappear from the published account. The laboratory reports using this system with external collaborators, but its account is a single-group practice report: it neither establishes that all its issues were public nor measures gains in replication, trust or collaboration. Public visibility must therefore be deliberately configured, with sensitive material handled separately. (chen2025githubisan pages 2-6)

**Trust is better understood as warranted scrutiny than as automatic confidence.** Dated records and visible corrections make it harder to conceal a changed rationale or an unsuccessful experiment and give outsiders opportunities to flag technical errors. They do not certify that entries are complete or correct. The notebook authors explicitly note risks from preliminary errors, scooping, workload and collaborators who cannot release data; they also state that the impact of open notebooks had not been systematically evaluated. A comparative study of two open drug-discovery projects likewise found that participation depended on clear entry points, transparency and funding for physical work. Where substantive decisions occurred offline and access or reuse was restricted, the nominally open project offered fewer opportunities for outsiders to follow or join the work. (ardal2012opensourcedrug pages 1-2, schapira2019openlaboratorynotebooks pages 3-5, ardal2012opensourcedrug pages 8-9)

## What recording AI-assisted work adds

A statement that “AI was used” cannot show which evidence the system saw, what it proposed, what the researcher rejected, or how a claim was checked. For consequential AI assistance, a useful research record identifies the **task; tool and model version; prompts and relevant input or retrieval sources; raw outputs; selection and edits; validation against primary sources; and the responsible human decisions**. Linking this record to dated notebooks, issues, code and data makes an AI-supported literature search or analysis inspectable by another group. The documentation burden should be proportionate: language polishing and AI-generated analysis or literature synthesis do not present identical methodological risks. (binkyte2026inspectableaifor pages 3-4, cwik2026disclosureisnot pages 4-5)

There is empirical reason not to equate disclosure with an audit trail. In a study of **188 systematic reviews that explicitly acknowledged AI or automation**, just **two (1%) reported the prompts used**; another two supplied only a generic description of the LLM’s function. Descriptions of validation and human oversight were often formulaic. This measures *what was reported*, not whether unreported checks actually occurred. In a separate 16-run audit of AI-assisted literature retrieval, outputs varied by tool and retrieval method, and some runs returned no retrievable items. For natural-language results, the proportion of records with a correct, matching DOI ranged from **9/40 (22.5%) for Gemini to 38/40 (95%) for paid ChatGPT**. Preserving prompts, unedited outputs, identifiers, DOI checks and screening decisions made such failures discoverable; a plausible-looking citation alone did not make the evidence reliable. (bastounis2026bibliometricmethodologicaland pages 4-6, leon2026auditinggenailiterature pages 14-16, leon2026auditinggenailiterature pages 1-2)

A prompt log improves **auditability**, not necessarily exact computational repeatability: model updates, undisclosed provider settings and stochastic generation can change an output. A stronger reproducibility test reruns consequential steps and reports stability and errors, while preserving the original output for inspection. Nor should every prompt be made public. Patient information, unpublished results, confidential peer review and proprietary inputs may require redaction, a public methods summary, or controlled access to the fuller audit record. Human researchers retain responsibility for checking sources and conclusions. (binkyte2026inspectableaifor pages 6-7, watkins2026towardreproducibleresearch pages 6-8, cwik2026disclosureisnot pages 8-9)

## Finding experts—and establishing new collaborations

Literature and AI methods can make previously unfamiliar expertise discoverable. Systems use publication text and keywords to infer topics, and citation, coauthorship or project networks to rank relevant or complementary researchers. One publication-topic system reported **88.4% accuracy** in its retrospective assessment; a 2026 interdisciplinary recommender reported **Hit@10 of 36.6%** and **Recall@10 of 22.1%** against completed projects. These are *offline recommendation results*, not rates at which approached scientists agreed to work together. A searchable alternative, ModelMatcher, lets users find scientists by gene, ortholog or research term and reach out through their profiles; its federated registries made **495 scientists** discoverable as of January 2022. That is coverage, not a count of new teams. (almuhanna2022aninteractivescholarly pages 2-4, almuhanna2022aninteractivescholarly pages 1-2, zhong2026interdisciplinaryscientificcollaborator pages 1-2, harnish2022modelmatcherascientist‐centric pages 8-9)

Evidence that discovery can proceed **from a match to joint work** is clearer in rare-disease matchmaking, though it is observational and involves substantial human follow-up. Over 2.5 years, one GeneMatcher group submitted **71 candidate genes**, of which **60 matched**; matches for **28 genes** were associated with international collaborations and prospective publications, yielding **eight published papers** and **19 manuscripts in progress**. A separate clinical laboratory reported publications from **10% of its 69 submissions**, with **14 additional submissions** involved in ongoing collaborations. GeneDx reported that GeneMatcher was the primary facilitator of initial connections for **at least 375 genes** subsequently pursued as active collaborative investigations among **908 genes** moving from candidate to validated status in the period studied. These are gene- or submission-level outcomes, **not numbers of unique partnerships**: one project can generate several matches, and a match becomes a collaboration only when people respond, assess complementary evidence, secure appropriate permissions and perform further work. (bruel20192.5years’experience pages 2-3, taylor2022aclinicallaboratorys pages 1-2, mcwalter2022discoveryofover pages 3-3, mcwalter2022discoveryofover pages 2-3)

The connection to transparent workflows is therefore practical rather than automatic. Search and recommendation can identify a possible partner; a public, well-documented question or unresolved problem gives that person a way to judge the evidence and make a specific contribution; a shared, attributable record helps the groups coordinate and check what follows. Open-notebook projects document such approaches by people outside the original team: by December 2018 one platform included **23 scientists at six institutions**, and contributors reported contact from companies interested in posted results. Neither those observations nor the matchmaking studies isolate the *causal* contribution of public visibility. The defensible conclusion is that transparency lowers barriers to verification and participation and can support new cross-group collaborations, provided records are maintained, contributions receive credit, recommendations are verified, and legitimate confidentiality is respected. (schapira2019openlaboratorynotebooks pages 5-6, ardal2012opensourcedrug pages 8-9, bruel20192.5years’experience pages 2-3)
