## The template

From the design template, verbatim, with the margin notes from page 2 of the summary:

| Component | Doumont's one-line definition | Margin note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Attention getter** | "A way to lead the audience to the need efficiently" | "First, focus on your audience" |
| **Need** | "A difference between actual and desired situations" | |
| **Task** | "What I decided/was asked to do to address the need" | "Introduce yourself in the task" |
| **Main message** | "The one sentence I want my audience to remember" | |
| **Preview** | "A map of the body (ideally three points, max. five)" | |
| Point 1, transition, point 2, transition, … | | |
| **Review** | "A recap of the body, leading into the conclusion" | "Focus again on your audience" |
| **Conclusion** | "What the above means to the audience in the end" | |
| **Close** | "A way to end the presentation clearly and elegantly" | |

Before designing, answer the five planning questions: **Why** (purpose), **Who** (audience),
**What** (content), **When** (time constraints), **Where** (space constraints). Underneath
it all are his three laws: "Adapt to your audience", "Maximize the signal-to-noise ratio",
"Use effective redundancy".

## What the Scitable unit adds

- **The opening is a paper's introduction, changed in three ways.** "The context as such is
  best replaced by an attention getter", the object of the document becomes the preview,
  and the opening also states the main message, "just before the preview". So the opening
  has five items: "attention getter, need, task, main message, and preview."
- **The main message comes early, theorem-proof style.** "The main message is the one
  sentence you want your audience to remember, if they remember only one." Say it in the
  opening, then spend the body proving it. Choosing it early "is the key to being selective".
- **An attention getter has three qualities:** it is short, audience-oriented, and relevant
  and appropriate. It can be "a question, a statement, an anecdote (humorous or not), an
  analogy, a quotation, an object, a picture projected on the screen". Don't open with "My
  name is … and I would like to talk to you today about": "Wait until the task to talk about
  yourself." No filler and no thanking the organizers before it; you "might want to learn
  your first few and last few sentences by heart."
- **The body is a tree, not a chain.** Two to five main points, each with two to five
  subpoints. As a rule, "place your strongest arguments first and last".
- **Show the structure with a preview, transitions and a review.** The preview covers the
  main points of the body only. A transition wraps up one point and announces the next "by
  creating a need for it". Avoid the mechanical "First, I am going to talk about …"; use a
  collective *we* and show the logic.
- **Close in three steps:** review, conclusion, close. A good close "make[s] the link back to
  your attention getter". Avoid "So, that's all I had for you today", "Thank you for your
  attention" and "I will be happy to answer any questions you have" ("save this prompt for
  after the applause").

## The worked example: Doumont's own six-minute talk

The *Openings* and *Closings* handouts label each part of Doumont's six-minute talk
*What you should know about TeX*. Its format matches a lightning talk: six minutes, a
technical tool, an audience that mostly doesn't use it yet, and a call to try it. The
labels below are verbatim; the speech is paraphrased.

| Part | Doumont's label | What he says (paraphrased) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Attention getter | "Focuses on the audience" | You are scientists, and your most tangible output is papers and presentations. |
| Need | "Is audience-oriented like the attention-getter" | Few scientists know TeX, yet every scientist needs to know what it can do for them. |
| Task | "Shifts the focus to the speaker" | He has used it for 20 years, since his PhD at Stanford. |
| Main message | | Three short sentences: TeX is powerful, flexible, reliable. |
| Preview | "Includes the audience with a collective we" | We'll see what TeX is, the psychological barriers to using it, and what you gain once past them. |
| Review | "Recaps the body's three main points" | "So what to remember?", then the three points. |
| Conclusion | "Place the body's discussion into a broader perspective" | TeX is free, so you can install it as soon as you're back at your desk. |
| Close | "Links to the attention getter" | Back to the scientists of his first line: if you call yourself one, try it. |

## Slides, if any

From the summary (p. 3) and the slide checklist:

- One slide per subpoint, one message per slide. "State the message verbally, then develop it
  visually."
- The title states the *so what*, not the *what*, as a full sentence of about 12 words or
  fewer.
- Each slide must make sense on its own and be readable at a glance. Leave out whatever adds
  little: backgrounds, logos, decoration.
- Show the structure (the preview) at a glance, one level deep, three main points if
  possible. Don't title it "Outline" or "Preview"; use the talk's title instead.
- "Do your slides right… or don't do slides at all."

On delivery (p. 4): "Memorize your outline but not your wording. Eradicate filler words;
learn to value silences."
