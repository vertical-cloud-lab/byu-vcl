## Preservation of Vertical Layering During Sampling of Shallow Powder Beds: Evidence from Experimental Studies, DEM Frameworks, and Statistical Considerations

### 1. Overview and Literature Landscape

The question of how well sampling tools preserve the chronological (vertical) layering of a powder bed has been addressed most thoroughly in the pharmaceutical blending literature, where blend uniformity testing by thief probes is critical for regulatory compliance. The key experimental studies are by Muzzio et al. (1997), who quantitatively compared side-sampling, end-sampling, and streamlined probes using layered glass-bead beds solidified with gelatin (muzzio1997samplingpracticesin pages 7-8, muzzio1997samplingpracticesin pages 8-11, muzzio1997samplingpracticesin pages 11-14), and Susana (2012), who developed and tested a sliding-cover sampler (SCS) using stratified lactose–cocoa powder beds and measured both sampling efficiency and drag contamination (susana2012lowshearwet pages 114-119, susana2012lowshearwet pages 119-123, susana2012lowshearwet pages 123-128). Funnel-flow and mass-flow discharge order have been studied by Engblom (2012) using tracer objects and composition monitoring during silo emptying (engblom2012segregationofpowder pages 29-33, engblom2012segregationofpowder pages 69-72, engblom2012segregationofpowder pages 56-62). Cohesive powder behavior is reviewed by Hogg (2009) and Hickey et al. (2003) (hogg2009mixingandsegregation pages 2-4, hickey2003aguideto pages 74-78, hogg2009mixingandsegregation pages 4-5).

A notable finding of this literature search is that **no DEM simulation has been published that directly models the insertion of a thief or core sampler into a layered granular bed** and quantifies boundary smearing or drag-down in particle diameters. DEM studies of granular intruders (ploughs, indentation tools, probes) exist, using Hertz–Mindlin contact mechanics with JKR or linear cohesion models (behjani2018numericalsimulationof pages 79-83, pasha2013modellingofflowability pages 157-163, knight2019fluidflowand pages 77-82, knight2019fluidflowand pages 74-77), but they have not been applied to the sampling geometry. The quantitative data below therefore come entirely from physical experiments with tracer- or contrast-layered beds.

#### Method 1: Open Glass Tube, Thumb-Sealed

No published study uses this exact configuration. However, a glass core sampler (15 mm ID) pushed through a 150 mm lactose bed recovered only 60% of the insertion depth due to wall-friction plugging (susana2012lowshearwet pages 114-119, susana2012lowshearwet pages 119-123). The axial stress required to continue filling the tube grows exponentially with depth and is determined by the wall friction coefficient μ_w and the Janssen K-ratio (susana2012lowshearwet pages 114-119). When the tube is open during insertion, powder enters continuously from the bottom; wall friction drags upper material downward, compressing the core. Dividing the shortened core into equal thirds does not correspond to equal original bed depths. For cohesive 15–63 µm powder, the high surface-to-volume ratio increases wall adhesion, making plugging more severe.

#### Method 2: Piston Corer

The piston corer addresses the continuous-entry problem by holding a plunger at the powder surface while the tube is pushed down. This is mechanically similar to the core sampler tested by Susana (2012), which showed <10% contamination of upper-layer material 10 mm below a sharp interface—substantially better than side-sampling thieves (90%) or slug end-samplers (45%) (susana2012lowshearwet pages 119-123). However, core shortening remains a concern: the 60% sampling efficiency measured for a 15 mm core tube means that a 40% compression of the core occurs. During extrusion, the stress required to push out the core can be double that developed during insertion (the exponent effectively doubles), further compacting the sample and potentially inducing reverse drag (susana2012lowshearwet pages 114-119, susana2012lowshearwet pages 123-128). For cohesive powder, wall adhesion a_w contributes an additional exponential term to the stress profile (susana2012lowshearwet pages 114-119). Using a larger-diameter, polished, thin-wall tube reduces stress buildup substantially.

#### Method 3: Multi-Compartment Sampling Thief

The extensive experiments of Muzzio et al. (1997) demonstrate that all thief designs introduce significant errors in layered beds. A commercial side-sampling thief (Globe Pharma) produced samples that were "composed almost entirely of particles from the top layer regardless of whether the top layer was composed of smaller particles or larger particles," with maximum composition errors approaching 100% (muzzio1997samplingpracticesin pages 8-11). Even particles entered the cavity before it was opened. The streamlined Rutgers end-sampling thief still showed ~10% composition error and contamination from particles originally 5–10 cm from the target location (muzzio1997samplingpracticesin pages 11-14). The conventional side-sampling probe produced mixed-color sand within two aperture diameters (3.25 mm) of the layer boundary (muzzio1997samplingpracticesin pages 8-11). For cohesive powders, an additional problem is that material may not flow readily into thief cavities, producing undersized or biased samples (muzzio1997samplingpracticesin pages 7-8).

#### Method 4: Layer-by-Layer Removal with Spatula

This method avoids insertion drag entirely because no probe penetrates the bed. The principal errors are (i) cutting too deep, mixing the current layer with the one below, and (ii) surface segregation during scooping, where fines can percolate through coarser particles on a sloped surface (hickey2003aguideto pages 67-71). For the user's application (a tube a few cm across, total depth a few cm), removing full cross-sectional layers by careful horizontal scraping should give the best depth fidelity. Cohesion is beneficial here: it suppresses particle mobility, reduces slumping of exposed surfaces, and makes the exposed layer more stable (hogg2009mixingandsegregation pages 2-4, hickey2003aguideto pages 74-78).

#### Method 5: Pour-and-Sample Along a Line

Pouring powder from a container involves complex flow patterns. In narrow containers, discharge commonly follows a funnel-flow pattern, which is only approximately first-in-last-out: material near the center exits first while wall-adjacent material exits last (engblom2012segregationofpowder pages 29-33, engblom2012segregationofpowder pages 56-62, engblom2012segregationofpowder pages 41-46). Engblom (2012) found that discharge produced nearly constant fines content for most of the outflow, with strong composition changes only in the final material (engblom2012segregationofpowder pages 69-72, engblom2012segregationofpowder pages 72-76). Critically, pouring onto a surface creates a heap, and heap formation causes size segregation: fines percolate centrally while coarse particles roll outward (hickey2003aguideto pages 74-78). Surface avalanche flow mixes adjacent layers during deposition. The model for discharge segregation showed only moderate agreement with experiments (R² = 0.70) (engblom2012segregationofpowder pages 88-91). For cohesive 15–63 µm powder, discharge may be plug-like (more order-preserving) but could also produce rat-holing or intermittent flow (hogg2009mixingandsegregation pages 4-5).

### 3. DEM Contact Models and Simulation Framework

While no DEM simulation has been published for sampling tool insertion into layered beds, the relevant framework is well established. The standard contact model is the **Hertz–Mindlin no-slip formulation**: normal forces depend nonlinearly on elastic overlap through the effective Young's modulus and effective radius (Hertzian contact), while tangential forces use Mindlin's incremental stiffness with Coulomb friction limiting (knight2019fluidflowand pages 77-82, knight2019fluidflowand pages 74-77). For cohesive powders, the **Johnson–Kendall–Roberts (JKR) model** is typically added to introduce adhesion, with pull-off forces proportional to surface energy and particle radius; this is implemented in common DEM packages such as EDEM 2.7.1 (behjani2018numericalsimulationof pages 79-83). The Rayleigh timestep criterion (typically 20–40% of the critical value) governs simulation stability (behjani2018numericalsimulationof pages 79-83). Practical DEM simulations can handle several hundred thousand to a few million particles (modenaUnknownyearpreliminarydesignof pages 62-65, modenaUnknownyearpreliminarydesignofa pages 62-65); for the user's application (a tube a few cm across containing ~5–100 g of 15–63 µm powder), the particle count would be on the order of 10⁶–10⁸, which is at the limit of current capability and would require coarse-graining (e.g., particle upscaling by a factor of 3–10, with adjusted contact parameters to preserve bulk behavior).

### 4. How Conclusions Change for Cohesive 15–63 µm Aluminium Powder

The user's powder spans a size range where interparticle cohesion becomes significant. Particles below ~20 µm are strongly cohesive, with dry-powder cohesion reducing individual-particle mobility and promoting agglomerate formation (hogg2009mixingandsegregation pages 4-5). For the full 15–63 µm distribution, the key changes relative to the literature experiments (which used free-flowing glass beads or lactose) are:

- **Reduced segregation:** Cohesion suppresses percolation and trajectory segregation mechanisms, meaning the as-deposited layering should be better preserved during storage (hogg2009mixingandsegregation pages 2-4, hickey2003aguideto pages 74-78). This is favorable for the user's objective.
- **Increased plugging risk in core samplers:** Wall adhesion a_w adds an exponential term to the stress buildup inside the probe, reducing sampling efficiency below the already-low 60% measured for lactose (susana2012lowshearwet pages 114-119). A polished, large-bore stainless or PTFE-lined tube is advisable.
- **Thief cavity underfilling:** Cohesive powders may not flow into thief cavities, producing undersized or unrepresentative samples (muzzio1997samplingpracticesin pages 7-8).
- **Layer-by-layer removal is more stable:** Cohesion prevents the exposed surface from slumping or self-segregating, making spatula removal the most reliable method (hogg2009mixingandsegregation pages 2-4).
- **Pouring behavior is less predictable:** Discharge may be plug-like or may produce rat-holing and intermittent flow, making the depth-to-line-position mapping unreliable without calibration (hogg2009mixingandsegregation pages 4-5).

### 5. SEM-EDS Statistical Requirements

For detecting a composition difference Δ (in wt% Si) between layer samples using per-particle SEM-EDS:

**Recommended particle counts:** BS ISO 13322-1:2014 and NIST recommend measuring approximately **10,000 particles** for statistical robustness of morphological descriptors (balart2022automatedsemedsanalysis pages 6-10). Automated SEM-EDS systems such as AutoEMX typically collect ~100 EDS spectra per sample (giunto2026accuratesemedsquantification pages 3-5). For the user's current protocol of ~30 particles per sample, a standard two-sample t-test power analysis applies:

n = 2(z_(α/2) + z_β)² σ² / Δ²

where σ is the per-particle standard deviation of Si content, α is the significance level, β = 1 − power, and Δ is the minimum detectable difference. For AlSi10Mg (nominal ~10 wt% Si), if the per-particle standard deviation σ ≈ 1–2 wt% Si (a reasonable estimate for single-particle EDS on well-alloyed spherical particles), then:

- To detect Δ = 2 wt% Si with 80% power at α = 0.05: n ≈ 2 × (1.96 + 0.84)² × (1.5)² / (2)² ≈ **18 particles per sample** (for σ = 1.5 wt%).
- To detect Δ = 1 wt% Si: n ≈ **70 particles per sample**.
- To detect Δ = 0.5 wt% Si: n ≈ **280 particles per sample**.

The user's 30 particles per sample is therefore adequate to detect ~1.5–2 wt% Si differences but insufficient for sub-1 wt% resolution. Increasing to ≥50 particles per sample, or ideally ≥100, would substantially improve sensitivity. For the number of samples (depth locations), Muzzio et al. (1997) showed that N = 30 random samples gave a poor estimate of the true composition distribution F(c), while N ≥ 100 samples gave close approximations (muzzio1997samplingpracticesin pages 14-16). For the user's three-point depth profile (top, middle, bottom), three samples is the minimum for detecting a monotonic drift; five or more depth points would allow assessment of nonlinear trends. Replicate samples at each depth (≥3 replicates) are essential to distinguish real drift from sampling noise.

### 6. Recommendation

For the user's specific scenario—small batches (5–100 g) of 15–63 µm cohesive aluminium alloy powder in a narrow stainless tube—**Method 4 (layer-by-layer removal with a spatula or spoonula)** is the best-supported approach. It avoids all probe-insertion artifacts, and cohesion helps preserve the exposed surface. Divide the powder into at least three depth fractions by removing full cross-sectional layers, weighing each. For each fraction, analyze ≥50 particles by SEM-EDS to detect Si differences of ~1 wt%. If physical access to the tube interior is limited, **Method 2 (piston corer)** with a large-bore, polished, thin-wall tube is the second-best option, but the core must be corrected for ~40% shortening and boundary slices discarded. Methods 1 and 3 introduce unacceptable drag-down for this application, and Method 5 requires tracer calibration before trust can be placed in the depth-to-position mapping.
