**A-Lab (UC Berkeley / LBNL, ambient-air comparator).** The original A-Lab is a bespoke open-air robotic platform—not enclosed in a glovebox—that uses a central Mitsubishi robot arm in a custom Labman Automation precursor-preparation station, plus two Universal Robots UR5e collaborative arms for sample and labware transfer between stations. One UR5e is mounted on an Olympus Controls linear rail. Powder dosing uses a Mettler Toledo Quantos automatic dispenser-balance, mixing is performed in a Hauschild Smart DAC250 Dual Asymmetric Centrifuge, and liquid transfer is handled by a Sartorius rLine automated pipettor. Heating uses four custom-actuated Thermo Scientific F48055-60 furnaces, and characterization relies on a Malvern Panalytical Aeris Minerals XRD (szymanski2024automatingthesynthesis pages 78-80). Targets were screened for air-stability so that a glovebox was not required (szymanski2023anautonomouslaboratory pages 1-2).

**A-Lab GPSS (UC Berkeley / LBNL, nitrogen glovebox).** The A-Lab for Glovebox Powder Solid-state Synthesis extends the platform to air-sensitive materials (lithium halide spinel conductors). It is housed in a "customized double-glovebox" filled with nitrogen. Two robot arms are mounted on the glovebox floor, with a linear rail for inter-station sample transfer. Five integrated workstations perform automated powder dispensing, mixing, heating (two parallel furnaces, up to six samples per heating cycle), post-heating grinding, and XRD sample preparation. The glovebox manufacturer and specific robot arm models are not identified in the preprint. A key design strategy is vertical "stacking"—placing equipment in subfloor wells and secondary platforms—to maximize space utilization. Lower-maintenance components like furnaces and robot arms are placed along the glovebox centerline, while frequently serviced modules are positioned along sidewalls near glove ports (fei2026agenticllmreasoning pages 1-3, fei2026agenticllmreasoning pages 3-6).

**Chemspeed in MBraun glovebox (FAU Erlangen-Nürnberg).** Salaheldin et al. (2017) implemented a Chemspeed Swing XL Autoplant II hybrid workstation inside an MBraun glovebox for automated hot-injection synthesis of CdSe quantum dots, with six parallel stainless-steel reactors, automated liquid handling, controlled injection, and temperature monitoring (salaheldin2017automatedsynthesisof pages 1-6).

**STORMS (Novartis / EPFL).** The STORMS stochastic robotic microsampling system uses a Universal Robots UR3e arm (6-DOF, 3 kg payload, 500 mm reach, ±0.03 mm repeatability) fitted with a Robotiq Hand-E gripper, integrated in a glovebox-enclosed environment. The arm operates a vacuum-assisted solid coring module for sub-milligram to milligram solid sampling (lisowski2026highthroughputsolidmicrosampling pages 15-18).

**Perovskite high-throughput platform (FAU Erlangen-Nürnberg).** Reinhardt et al. (2020) describe an automated robotic platform enclosed within an MBraun glovebox for high-throughput synthesis and characterization of perovskite thin-film layers (reinhardt2020rapidcharacterizationand pages 1-3).

**GIS for 2D materials (SUSTech, China).** Yang et al. (2025) describe a custom interconnected multi-glovebox system with transition chambers, nitrogen circulation and filtration, gate-valve-connected CVD furnaces, and three-axis electrically controlled robotic arms for automated wet and dry transfer of air-sensitive 2D materials (yang2025unifiedtransmissionelectron pages 2-4, yang2025unifiedtransmissionelectron pages 4-5).

Additional automated workstations operating inside nitrogen-filled gloveboxes are described generically, including Symyx Technologies core-module platforms with liquid-handling robots and high-temperature reactor arrays for CdSe nanocrystal synthesis (han2025intelligentsystemsfor pages 4-6).

**Vendor documentation note:** Angstrom Engineering manufactures glovebox-integrated thermal evaporators for thin-film deposition, and their products have been referenced in perovskite solar cell studies. LC Technologies, Inert Technology, Vigor, and Terra Universal gloveboxes are commercially available with automation-ready options (extra penetrations, larger antechambers), but no peer-reviewed publication identified in this survey explicitly describes robot-arm integration with these specific brands. Any such precedent would need to be confirmed through vendor case studies rather than peer-reviewed literature.

## 2. Design Requirements and Failure Modes for Operating a Robot Arm Inside an Argon Glovebox

### 2.1 Spatial Constraints and Layout

The A-Lab GPSS provides the most detailed published guidance on this topic. The constrained glovebox workspace requires equipment to be "packed densely while maintaining sufficient clearance for robotic motion and occasional manual intervention." Vertical stacking—using subfloor wells, secondary platforms, and multi-tier consumable racks—is essential to exploit the glovebox's vertical volume. Frequently serviced components should be placed along sidewalls for glove-port access, while robust equipment (arms, furnaces) can occupy the less-accessible centerline (fei2026agenticllmreasoning pages 3-6). A review on SDL safety emphasizes that constrained glovebox spaces make robotic-arm collisions more likely and that complex workflows require "substantially better motion planning and spatial optimization" (leong2025steeringtowardssafe pages 30-33).

### 2.2 Outgassing and Lubricant Compatibility

Published peer-reviewed literature on the specific outgassing rates of commercial robot arms (UR3/UR5e, Franka Emika, etc.) in sub-ppm O₂/H₂O environments is essentially absent. However, the analogous field of astromaterials curation provides relevant design guidance: JAXA's Hayabusa sample-handling system uses no bearing lubrication inside its nitrogen sample cabinet, constructed from aluminum, stainless steel, Teflon, and quartz, specifically to minimize contamination (mccubbin2019advancedcurationof pages 29-31). For vacuum and cleanroom applications, perfluoropolyether (PFPE) greases (e.g., Fomblin, Krytox) are widely used due to their chemical inertness, thermal stability, and low vapor pressure, which minimizes outgassing of organic volatiles that would poison purifier catalyst beds. Conventional hydrocarbon-based greases should be considered incompatible with sub-ppm operations.

### 2.3 Heat Rejection

Robot arms rated for 100–200 W continuous power dissipation rely on convective air cooling in open environments. Argon has approximately the same thermal conductivity as air (~18 mW/m·K at 25°C), so a sealed glovebox's primary concern is not the gas itself but the lack of forced ventilation. Most glovebox recirculation systems push gas through the purifier at 50–100+ LPM, providing some convection, but motors and drivers may still require derating. Placing motor drivers, power supplies, and controllers outside the glovebox via hermetic feedthroughs is the standard approach in vacuum/cleanroom robotics and was implicitly adopted in the A-Lab GPSS design (doloi2025democratizingselfdrivinglabs pages 25-27). Thermal monitoring of arm joints is advisable.

### 2.4 Cable and Power Feedthroughs

Hermetic electrical feedthroughs (KF-flanged multi-pin connectors, fiber-optic Ethernet, sealed AC/DC power entries) are standard glovebox accessories. For robot arms, the critical requirement is routing the arm's umbilical (power, encoder, I/O, potentially pneumatics) through the glovebox wall with sufficient pin count and current capacity while maintaining the pressure boundary. No published paper in this survey provides a detailed feedthrough specification for a specific robot arm inside a glovebox, indicating this is typically handled as custom engineering.

### 2.5 Particulate Generation

Gearbox wear, belt degradation, and cable flexing generate microscopic particles. Fine metal-oxide and metal particles from the robot's own wear, combined with the Al/Mg/Si process powders, can accumulate on purifier columns. Copper-catalyst oxygen-removal beds and molecular sieves are susceptible to poisoning by particulates, organic vapors, and sulfur compounds. The A-Lab GPSS and similar systems mitigate this by using sealed powder containers, capped crucibles, and localized handling, but no published system reports quantified particulate loading from the robot arm itself. Pre-filtration (HEPA or activated-carbon inline filters) upstream of the purifier is recommended.

### 2.6 Servicing Without Breaking Atmosphere

This is identified as a major practical challenge. The A-Lab GPSS maintains glove ports along sidewalls specifically for manual intervention (fei2026agenticllmreasoning pages 3-6). For consumable restocking, crucibles and caps are commercially available and can be loaded through the antechamber. However, major arm service (e.g., joint replacement, cable rerouting) would likely require opening the glovebox. The JAXA system requires periodic disassembly for servicing (mccubbin2019advancedcurationof pages 29-31). No published system describes an arm-isolation vestibule or modular arm-removal system for in-atmosphere service, though such a design is technically feasible.

### 2.7 Electrostatic Discharge / Ignition Risk with Combustible Metal Powders

NFPA 484 (Standard for Combustible Metals) and NFPA 652 (Standard on the Fundamentals of Combustible Dust) govern the handling of combustible metal powders including Al, Mg, and Si. Aluminum powder can have a minimum ignition energy (MIE) below 10 mJ and an explosibility class of St3 (K_St > 300 bar·m/s, P_max > 10 bar), making it extremely reactive (taveau2019metaldustsexplosion pages 1-2). Magnesium is similarly hazardous and can burn in nitrogen, requiring argon or helium for inerting rather than nitrogen alone (reding2018metaldustexplosion pages 3-4). Silicon is classified as "highly reactive" (taveau2019metaldustsexplosion pages 1-2).

Key safety provisions include: (1) maintaining the enclosed atmosphere below the limiting oxygen concentration (LOC) via gas blanketing, with argon being necessary for Mg-containing powders since Mg can react with N₂ (reding2018metaldustexplosion pages 3-4); (2) grounding all conductive components to prevent electrostatic discharge, which is particularly important in the very dry (<1 ppm H₂O) argon atmosphere where charge dissipation is extremely slow; (3) using intrinsically safe electrical equipment; (4) avoiding open flames, hot surfaces, and frictional spark sources; (5) controlling powder dispersion to avoid lofting concentrations above the MEC (reding2018metaldustexplosion pages 1-2, reding2018metaldustexplosion pages 3-4, reding2018metaldustexplosion pages 2-3). Water-based cleaning is prohibited for alkali and reactive metal dusts (reding2018metaldustexplosion pages 2-3). Even within an inert atmosphere, caution is warranted: "many fine and most ultrafine metal powders may burn in carbon dioxide, water vapor, and even nitrogen," so the inert gas choice and oxygen-exclusion level must be validated for each specific powder (taveau2019metaldustsexplosion pages 1-2).

## 3. Sample Transfer Architectures Without Breaking Atmosphere

### 3.1 Standard Antechambers

Conventional glovebox antechambers use manual door operation with vacuum-pump-down and backfill cycles. A typical vacuum/purge cycle (pump to ~0.1 Torr, backfill with argon, repeat 3–5 times) requires 5–15 minutes and consumes approximately 2–5 L of argon per cycle per liter of antechamber volume (no single peer-reviewed publication in this survey provides a standardized benchmark; these values derive from manufacturer specifications and laboratory practice).

### 3.2 Automated Load-Locks and Vacuum Transfer

The IP-VEST system demonstrates a differentially pumped load-lock with a detachable vacuum suitcase for transferring samples from an argon glovebox (800–900 Torr) to UHV instruments. A mobile vacuum carrier used automated docking with an average cycle under 60 seconds; after 72 hours in the carrier, oxygen exposure was 66 ppm and moisture 349 ppm (kurtUnknownyearmondaymorningoctober pages 4-4). These values represent a less demanding specification than sub-ppm glovebox operation, indicating that carrier materials and seals require careful selection for demanding reactive-powder work.

### 3.3 Multi-Chamber Glovebox Networks

JAXA's Hayabusa curation facility uses five sequentially connected chambers transitioning from vacuum (CC3-1, CC3-2) through vacuum-to-nitrogen conversion (CC3-3) to purified nitrogen processing (CC4-1, CC4-2), with no direct ambient exposure at any stage (mccubbin2019advancedcurationof pages 24-27). The GIS system for 2D materials uses multiple interconnected gloveboxes with transition chambers, gate-valve-connected CVD furnaces, and a large TEM-holder transfer chamber, all under nitrogen circulation (yang2025unifiedtransmissionelectron pages 2-4, yang2025unifiedtransmissionelectron pages 4-5). Neither system reports quantitative cycle times or gas consumption per transfer.

### 3.4 Positive-Pressure Purge Enclosures

Low-cost approaches such as DIY positive-pressure enclosures using nitrogen or argon flow at 15 L/min have been demonstrated for 2D-material transfer, achieving <0.1% O₂ within minutes and protecting air-sensitive black phosphorus for >24 hours after gas shutoff (buapan2021versatilelowcostand pages 5-7). However, these do not achieve sub-ppm performance and are inadequate for reactive metal powder work.

## 4. Automated Powder Dosing and Dispensing Inside Inert Atmospheres

### 4.1 Mettler Toledo Quantos

The A-Lab uses the Mettler Toledo Quantos automatic dispenser-balance for precursor powder dispensing into plastic vials. Prescribed powder masses are dispensed automatically, followed by mixing with ethanol and ZrO₂ balls in a Hauschild Smart DAC250 dual asymmetric centrifuge. The ethanol amount is automatically calculated based on powder mass and density (szymanski2024automatingthesynthesis pages 78-80). The original A-Lab operates in ambient air; the A-Lab GPSS extends this to nitrogen-atmosphere operation but does not report modified dosing specifications or accuracy figures for air-sensitive powders (fei2026agenticllmreasoning pages 1-3, fei2026agenticllmreasoning pages 3-6).

### 4.2 STORMS Stochastic Microsampling

The STORMS system achieves sub-milligram to milligram-scale solid sampling using vacuum-assisted coring tools. With the 1.5 mm inner-diameter tool, the average standard deviation across mass ranges is 0.24 mg; with the 0.6 mm tool for sub-milligram work, standard deviation is 0.06 mg. At low masses, the Mettler Toledo SPC215-111 analytical balance (0.01 mg readability, 0.01 mg repeatability) contributes approximately 0.2 mg effective repeatability under operating conditions, becoming a major relative error source. The system preferentially targets 3–5 mg quantities where compatible (lisowski2026highthroughputsolidmicrosampling pages 13-15). Electrostatic disturbances are identified as environmental factors affecting weighing-cell stability, though no quantitative data on triboelectric charging in dry argon is provided (lisowski2026highthroughputsolidmicrosampling pages 15-18).

### 4.3 Chemspeed Platforms

Chemspeed Technologies' Swing XL Autoplant II has been operated inside an MBraun glovebox for automated nanocrystal synthesis, demonstrating that integrated liquid/solid handling platforms can be accommodated within commercial gloveboxes (salaheldin2017automatedsynthesisof pages 1-6).

### 4.4 Triboelectric Charging in Dry Argon

No peer-reviewed publication identified in this survey provides quantitative measurements of triboelectric charging effects on powder dosing accuracy specifically in dry argon (<1 ppm H₂O) glovebox atmospheres. This is a recognized but under-characterized hazard. The extremely low humidity in argon gloveboxes eliminates the surface moisture films that normally dissipate static charge on powder particles, leading to strong adhesion to container walls, dosing heads, and balance pans. For reactive metal powders (Al, Mg, Si, AlSi10Mg), this is both a dosing-accuracy concern and a potential ignition source. Grounding of all conductive components, conductive (antistatic) containers, ionizing bars (if compatible with the atmosphere), and validation of dosing accuracy under actual operating conditions are essential.

### 4.5 Powder Flowability Under Argon

No systematic studies of flowability changes for Al, Mg, Si, or AlSi10Mg powders under argon versus ambient air were identified. Powder flowability is influenced by particle size, morphology, surface oxide thickness, and moisture content. Removing surface moisture in dry argon may increase particle-particle adhesion for fine powders (Carr index effects), potentially reducing flowability. The STORMS system found that sampling success dropped dramatically above a Carr index of approximately 30 (lisowski2026highthroughputsolidmicrosampling pages 15-18).

## 5. Published Self-Driving / Autonomous Laboratories Operating Inside Gloveboxes

### 5.1 A-Lab GPSS (Air-Sensitive Solid-State Synthesis)

The A-Lab GPSS is the most directly relevant published system. It operated under nitrogen to synthesize 352 samples of lithium halide spinel solid-state ionic conductors. Key practical lessons: (1) a "customized double-glovebox" was required, suggesting standard commercial boxes were insufficient; (2) a semi-automated approach was adopted for characterization (XRD samples were manually sealed with Kapton before transfer outside the box; EIS was performed inside), because "practical considerations, including cost, robustness, and experimental flexibility, lead to the implementation of a combination of automated synthesis and semi-automated post-synthetic handling" (fei2026agenticllmreasoning pages 3-6); (3) vertical stacking and sidewall placement strategies were necessary for space optimization; (4) the heating step (average 21 hours per cycle) was rate-limiting, mitigated by parallel furnaces; (5) crucible caps are discarded after each reaction and crucibles are manually cleaned outside the glovebox (fei2026agenticllmreasoning pages 24-29). Average processing time per sample was approximately 19 min for dispensing, 20 min for mixing, and 37 min for grinding and XRD sample prep (fei2026agenticllmreasoning pages 3-6).

### 5.2 A-Lab (Ambient-Air Solid-State Synthesis)

The original A-Lab synthesized 41 of 58 novel inorganic compounds over 17 days of continuous operation (71% success rate). Key lessons: (1) failure modes included sluggish reaction kinetics (11/17 failures), precursor volatility, product amorphization, and computational inaccuracies (szymanski2024automatingthesynthesis pages 76-78); (2) system modifications needed include multi-step heating, intermediate regrinding, and expanded precursor selection; (3) manual regrinding and higher-temperature heating rescued two additional targets, raising success to 74% (szymanski2024automatingthesynthesis pages 76-78); (4) the systematic pairwise-reaction database built during experiments reduced search space by up to 80% (szymanski2023anautonomouslaboratory pages 3-4).

### 5.3 Chemspeed + MBraun (Quantum Dot Synthesis)

Automated CdSe quantum dot synthesis inside an MBraun glovebox demonstrated that six parallel reactors with automated liquid handling could achieve reproducible size distributions, eliminating human-operator variability in sensitive hot-injection protocols (salaheldin2017automatedsynthesisof pages 1-6).

### 5.4 Perovskite Fabrication

Multiple groups perform automated perovskite thin-film fabrication inside nitrogen gloveboxes, including with spin-coating robots. However, these are typically semi-automated rather than fully autonomous (reinhardt2020rapidcharacterizationand pages 1-3).

### 5.5 General SDL Safety and Maintenance Lessons

Leong et al. (2025) emphasize that SDLs handling air- and moisture-sensitive materials must preserve inert conditions through global purging, inert-gas blankets, adapted apparatus, or robotic systems installed inside inert gloveboxes. Air or moisture ingress can quench sensitive reagents, clog tubing, corrode hardware, and for pyrophoric compounds cause fires or explosions. Many robotic systems lack cleanroom certification, and their long-term maintenance requirements in oxygen- and moisture-free environments require additional analysis (leong2025steeringtowardssafe pages 30-33). The field of air-sensitive autonomous synthesis is described as "nascent" (leong2025steeringtowardssafe pages 30-33).

### Recommended Architecture: Option (c) with a Staged Upgrade Path

Based on the evidence gathered, the recommended near-term architecture for BYU is **Option (c): external robot arm with automated antechamber transfer, with the powder dosing machine installed inside a purifier-equipped glovebox under argon.** The rationale is as follows:

**1. Atmosphere integrity and purifier protection.** Keeping the robot arm outside the glovebox eliminates its contributions to outgassing, particulate generation, and heat loading on the purifier system. This is particularly important given the reactive metal powders (Al, Mg, Si, AlSi10Mg) that pose both contamination and ignition risks. Aluminum powder MIE can be below 10 mJ, and magnesium can burn in nitrogen, requiring argon rather than nitrogen as the inert gas (reding2018metaldustexplosion pages 3-4, taveau2019metaldustsexplosion pages 1-2).

**2. Maintenance accessibility.** Robot maintenance (lubrication, cable replacement, joint servicing) does not require breaking the glovebox atmosphere. This addresses the single largest practical concern identified in the glovebox-automation literature (fei2026agenticllmreasoning pages 3-6, leong2025steeringtowardssafe pages 30-33).

**3. Data quality.** Powder dosing inside the glovebox (using a Mettler Toledo Quantos or similar system, the de facto standard in autonomous powder synthesis labs (szymanski2024automatingthesynthesis pages 78-80)) maintains the reactive powders under sub-ppm O₂/H₂O at all times during the most atmosphere-sensitive steps. Sealed sample carriers can be used for furnace loading and characterization steps.

**4. Capital cost.** A used purifier-equipped glovebox (MBraun MB200MOD ~$12K or Vigor SG1200-750TS ~$14K) provides the enclosure at lowest cost, provided it passes leak testing, purifier-capacity verification, and loaded atmosphere acceptance tests. The refurbished Labconco Protector + AtmosPure (~$28K) is another viable option. The $3K purge-only Labconco should not be used for quantitative reactive-powder work until a recirculating purifier, O₂/H₂O analyzers, and adequate sealing are added.

**5. Safety compliance.** NFPA 484 and 652 requirements for combustible metal powders (grounding, intrinsically safe equipment, validated inerting below LOC, powder-containment controls) must be addressed regardless of the glovebox choice. An argon atmosphere effectively eliminates the oxidant leg of the explosion pentagon for all four powders, including Mg (which reacts with N₂). However, equipment grounding, ESD prevention in the very dry argon atmosphere, and powder-containment practices remain critical.

**6. Staged upgrade path.** If unattended internal manipulation becomes necessary, a compact robot arm (UR3e or Meca500) can later be installed inside the glovebox through a service port, following the A-Lab GPSS design paradigm. The initial external-arm architecture provides a low-risk platform to develop workflows, software integration, and operational experience before committing to the higher complexity of internal robotics.

**Key caveats.** (1) The automated antechamber is the throughput bottleneck in this architecture and must be engineered with interlocked doors, vacuum/purge capability, and sealed sample carriers. (2) An internal mechanism (simple linear actuator, or manual glove-port operation) is still needed to move carriers between the antechamber and the dosing station. (3) Sub-ppm atmosphere recovery after each antechamber cycle must be validated with the actual argon purifier system. (4) All four metal powders require combustible-dust hazard analysis per NFPA 484 before operations begin (reding2018metaldustexplosion pages 3-4, reding2018metaldustexplosion pages 2-3, taveau2019metaldustsexplosion pages 1-2, reding2018metaldustexplosion pages 4-5).

If budget permits and the intent is fully unattended internal operation from the outset, **Option (a)—a purpose-built automation-ready glovebox** specified with extra volume, engineered feedthroughs, local filtration, reinforced floor, and combustible-powder controls—provides the strongest foundation. The A-Lab GPSS demonstrates that this approach is feasible but requires substantial custom engineering, and the authors opted for a semi-automated characterization workflow even with a purpose-built system (fei2026agenticllmreasoning pages 3-6). The LC Technologies LC-100/LC-180 ($34K–$50K) would need to be evaluated against a detailed user-requirements specification including loaded atmosphere performance, internal dimensions adequate for the arm reach envelope, feedthrough provisions, and NFPA compliance before purchase.
