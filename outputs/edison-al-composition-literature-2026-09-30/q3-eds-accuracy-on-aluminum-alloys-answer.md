## Published Quantitative Evidence on SEM-EDS Accuracy for Aluminum Alloys

### Overview of Available Evidence

Published quantitative evidence on the accuracy of SEM-EDS with silicon drift detectors (SDDs) for aluminum alloys is limited in terms of aluminum-specific benchmarks, but substantial general guidance exists from NIST researchers Newbury and Ritchie, interlaboratory comparisons on related alloys, and multi-technique validation studies.

### General Accuracy Targets from Newbury & Ritchie (NIST)

Newbury and Ritchie have established performance benchmarks for SDD-EDS through extensive validation studies on certified reference materials, stoichiometric compounds, and independently characterized materials (newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 23-25, newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 1-2). Their 2015 landmark study demonstrated that modern SDD-EDS can achieve accuracy equivalent to wavelength-dispersive spectrometry when following rigorous standards-based k-ratio protocols with proper matrix corrections (newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 23-25). The achievable accuracy targets are concentration-dependent: major constituents (>10 wt%) can routinely achieve ±5% relative error, minor constituents (1-10 wt%) approximately ±10% relative error, and trace constituents (0.1-1 wt%) approximately ±25% relative error (newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 23-25).

Their 2024 low-beam-energy study at 5 keV analyzed 263 concentration measurements across 39 elements in 113 materials using NIST DTSA-II software with standards-based k-ratio quantification (newbury2024testingtheaccuracy pages 18-22, newbury2024testingtheaccuracy pages 1-3). More than 98% of results fell within ±5% relative deviation from expected value (RDEV), and 82% were within −2% to +2% RDEV (newbury2024testingtheaccuracy pages 18-22). For conventional beam energies (≥10 kV), 122 elemental analyses showed all results within ±5% RDEV and 89% within −2% to +2% RDEV (newbury2024testingtheaccuracy pages 18-22). These figures represent aggregate performance across diverse materials rather than aluminum-alloy-specific results, but they establish achievable accuracy benchmarks for properly conducted SDD-EDS analysis.

### Interlaboratory Comparisons and Round Robin Studies

An interlaboratory comparison by Jonnard et al. (2015) on a nickel-based metallic glass provides insight into EDS versus WDS performance and interlaboratory variability, though not specific to aluminum matrices (jonnard2015interlaboratorycomparisonof pages 11-13, jonnard2015interlaboratorycomparisonof pages 4-8). At 15-20 kV, EDS analyses from 57 spectrometers across multiple laboratories showed relative standard deviations of 1.06% for Cr (10.5 wt%), 1.43% for Fe (5.4 wt%), 0.77% for Co (23.5 wt%), 0.40% for Ni (51.4 wt%), and 1.94% for Mo (7.1 wt%) (jonnard2015interlaboratorycomparisonof pages 11-13). Mean signed errors relative to reference values were +22% for B, +1% for Cr, −4% for Fe, +0.4% for Co, +2% for Ni, and +6% for Mo (jonnard2015interlaboratorycomparisonof pages 4-8). WDS showed generally lower dispersion: relative standard deviations of 0.48-1.04% versus 0.40-1.94% for EDS (jonnard2015interlaboratorycomparisonof pages 11-13).

At 5 kV, EDS precision degraded substantially, with larger dispersions and mean signed errors of −14% for B, +1% for Cr, −30% for Fe, −3% for Co, +7% for Ni, and −10% for Mo (jonnard2015interlaboratorycomparisonof pages 4-8). The study demonstrated that low-voltage analyses are more challenging and that WDS generally provides better precision than EDS, though careful EDS implementation can approach WDS performance (jonnard2015interlaboratorycomparisonof pages 11-13).

### EDS vs. Alternative Techniques on Aluminum Alloys

Seidel et al. (2021) conducted a comprehensive comparison of EDXRF, WDXRF, LIBS, and spark-OES on commercial aluminum alloys, using spark-OES as the benchmark technique (seidel2021comparisonofelemental pages 10-13, seidel2021comparisonofelemental pages 5-7, seidel2021comparisonofelemental pages 7-8, seidel2021comparisonofelemental pages 8-10, seidel2021comparisonofelemental pages 2-4). This study, while not focused on SEM-EDS specifically, provides valuable quantitative data on aluminum alloy analysis accuracy. Three certified aluminum alloy reference materials (EB 313, BAM-311, and EB 315a) were analyzed by spark-OES against certified values (seidel2021comparisonofelemental pages 5-7).

For EB 315a (an Al-Si-Cu alloy with approximately 9.88 wt% Si, closest to AlSi10Mg among the tested materials), spark-OES showed relative deviations of +1.4% for Si, −0.7% for Fe, +2.7% for Cu, +0.6% for Mn, and +7.5% for Mg compared to certified values (seidel2021comparisonofelemental pages 5-7). Across the three certified aluminum alloys, spark-OES relative errors were generally below 5% for elements at 1-10 wt% concentration and mostly below 10% for elements below 1 wt% (seidel2021comparisonofelemental pages 5-7).

For light elements critical to aluminum alloys (Mg, Al, Si), LIBS performed better than XRF techniques due to XRF's lower sensitivity for low-Z elements and absorption by air and detector windows (seidel2021comparisonofelemental pages 10-13, seidel2021comparisonofelemental pages 8-10). A notable exception was EDXRF measurement of Mg in AlMg4.5Mn0.7, which showed approximately 2 wt% (about 40% relative) deviation above spark-OES values (seidel2021comparisonofelemental pages 10-13, seidel2021comparisonofelemental pages 8-10). For most major and minor elements, absolute deviations were below 1 wt% for main components and below 0.25 wt% for minor elements below 2 wt% (seidel2021comparisonofelemental pages 8-10).

### Standards-Based vs. Standardless Quantification

Newbury and Ritchie strongly recommend standards-based k-ratio protocols over standardless quantification for accurate aluminum alloy analysis (newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 23-25, newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 1-2, goldstein2018quantitativeanalysisthe pages 1-4). Standardless normalization (where results are internally normalized to 100%) can conceal analytical problems, particularly specimen topography effects and unmeasured constituents (newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 23-25, newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 5-6). The standards-based k-ratio method requires measuring standards and unknowns under identical conditions (beam energy, dose, EDS parameters, target orientation, and take-off angle) and applying matrix corrections through software such as NIST DTSA-II (goldstein2018quantitativeanalysisthe pages 1-4).

For aluminum alloys, suitable standards include pure Al, Si, Mg, Cu, Fe, Mn, and other relevant elements, or stoichiometric compounds for reactive/problematic elements (newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 1-2, goldstein2018quantitativeanalysisthe pages 1-4). While probably more than 98% of reported EDS analyses use standardless methods, achieving WDS-equivalent accuracy requires the k-ratio/standards protocol with polished standards and unknowns plus rigorous matrix corrections (newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 23-25).

### Effect of Accelerating Voltage

Accelerating voltage significantly affects both spatial resolution and analytical accuracy. Newbury and Ritchie's 5 keV study demonstrated that low beam energy is particularly vulnerable to surface contamination and oxidation effects (newbury2024testingtheaccuracy pages 16-18). At 5 keV, surface contamination layers (carbon, oxygen) make the analyzed volume a composite of underlying alloy and surface film, potentially causing significant errors (newbury2024testingtheaccuracy pages 16-18). Proper materialographic preparation (mounting, grinding, polishing to <100 nm RMS roughness) is essential, especially for low-energy analysis (goldstein2018quantitativeanalysisthe pages 1-4).

The 5 kV results from Jonnard et al.'s round robin showed substantially poorer precision and larger systematic errors compared to 15-20 kV measurements (jonnard2015interlaboratorycomparisonof pages 4-8). For aluminum alloys, 10-20 kV generally provides greater robustness if the relevant X-ray lines (Mg Kα at 1.25 keV, Al Kα at 1.49 keV, Si Kα at 1.74 keV, Cu Kα at 8.05 keV, etc.) remain suitable (newbury2024testingtheaccuracy pages 16-18). The beam energy must exceed the critical ionization energy, with efficient excitation requiring E₀ > 2Ec (kallio2025accuracyandprecision pages 15-20, kallio2025accuracyandprecisiona pages 15-20).

### Effect of Count Statistics and Total Counts

Count statistics follow Poisson/Gaussian behavior: one-sigma precision is approximately 3.1% at 1,000 counts, 1.0% at 10,000 counts, 0.31% at 100,000 counts, and 0.10% at 1,000,000 counts (donovan2014electronprobemicroanalysis pages 20-23). These figures describe random uncertainty only; systematic errors from matrix corrections, surface geometry, or detector efficiency remain even with high count totals (donovan2014electronprobemicroanalysis pages 20-23). For trace elements and achieving sub-1000 ppm detection limits, Newbury and Ritchie recommend measurement times of 500 seconds or more to accumulate sufficient counts (newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 23-25).

Higher total counts improve precision but do not eliminate systematic errors from improper surface preparation or unsuitable quantification methods (donovan2014electronprobemicroanalysis pages 26-29, donovan2014electronprobemicroanalysis pages 15-20, donovan2014electronprobemicroanalysis pages 20-23). The tradeoff among accelerating voltage, beam current, and acquisition time affects both count statistics and specimen damage/heating (donovan2014electronprobemicroanalysis pages 26-29).

### Effect of Surface Preparation

Surface condition is critical for accurate aluminum alloy analysis. Rough, scratched, pitted, or oxidized surfaces can substantially alter electron scattering, X-ray generation, and X-ray absorption paths (newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 5-6). Newbury and Ritchie emphasize that a flat, highly polished surface below 100 nm RMS roughness is preferable, with <50 nm RMS recommended when analyzing low-energy photons below 1 keV (C, N, O, F, and low-energy L-lines) (goldstein2018quantitativeanalysisthe pages 1-4). Rough or irregular surfaces produce geometry-related errors that standardless normalization conceals but that remain problematic for standards-based quantification (newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 5-6).

Surface oxidation is especially problematic for aluminum alloys at low beam energy, where even thin oxide layers significantly affect the analyzed composition (newbury2024testingtheaccuracy pages 16-18). Chemical polishing should be avoided due to potential shallow compositional changes; physical grinding and polishing with fine abrasives (100 nm diamond, alumina, or ceria) are preferred (goldstein2018quantitativeanalysisthe pages 1-4). Ion beam milling can be used but may implant ions or cause differential sputtering (goldstein2018quantitativeanalysisthe pages 1-4). Conductive coatings must be applied uniformly to standards and unknowns since thickness differences alter measured intensities (donovan2014electronprobemicroanalysis pages 29-34).

### Specific Findings for Aluminum Alloy Elements

**Magnesium (Mg)**: Light-element analysis (Z=12) is challenging for X-ray fluorescence techniques due to absorption. LIBS performs better than EDXRF or WDXRF for Mg in aluminum matrices (seidel2021comparisonofelemental pages 10-13, seidel2021comparisonofelemental pages 8-10). One EDXRF measurement showed approximately +40% relative error for Mg in AlMg4.5Mn0.7 (seidel2021comparisonofelemental pages 10-13, seidel2021comparisonofelemental pages 8-10). For SEM-EDS at conventional beam energies, Mg Kα (1.25 keV) is readily excited and detected, but surface oxidation can affect results (newbury2024testingtheaccuracy pages 16-18).

**Silicon (Si)**: Silicon analysis shows element-specific challenges across techniques. LIBS agreed better with spark-OES for Si in aluminum alloys than either EDXRF or WDXRF (seidel2021comparisonofelemental pages 8-10). For EB 315a with 9.88 wt% Si, spark-OES showed +1.4% relative deviation from certified value (seidel2021comparisonofelemental pages 5-7). Si Kα (1.74 keV) is readily analyzed by SDD-EDS at 5-20 kV.

**Copper (Cu), Zinc (Zn), Iron (Fe), and Manganese (Mn)**: These transition metals showed generally good accuracy across multiple techniques. Spark-OES deviations from certified values for Cu, Fe, and Mn in certified aluminum alloys were typically below 5-10% relative for concentrations above 0.1 wt% (seidel2021comparisonofelemental pages 5-7, seidel2021comparisonofelemental pages 7-8). The Jonnard et al. round robin on a Ni-based alloy (not aluminum) showed EDS relative standard deviations of 1.4% for Fe and interlaboratory mean errors of −4% at 15-20 kV (jonnard2015interlaboratorycomparisonof pages 11-13, jonnard2015interlaboratorycomparisonof pages 4-8).

### Open Datasets and Public Data Availability

No dedicated open-access database of EDS spectra from aluminum alloys of certified composition was identified in the published literature. Newbury and Ritchie (2024) mentioned a publicly supported measurement database associated with their study, containing detector configurations, calibration spectra, analyzed material spectra, standard spectra, and Excel files with raw, normalized, and atomic concentrations plus uncertainty budgets (newbury2024testingtheaccuracy pages 16-18). However, the excerpt did not provide a public download location or confirm aluminum-alloy coverage.

The NIST DTSA-II software and source code are publicly available (newbury2024testingtheaccuracy pages 18-22, newbury2024testingtheaccuracy pages 1-3, goldstein2018quantitativeanalysisthe pages 1-4), providing the quantification engine used in the Newbury and Ritchie studies. This software implements standards-based k-ratio protocols with comprehensive matrix corrections and uncertainty budgets, making it valuable for reproducing and validating quantitative aluminum alloy measurements even in the absence of a dedicated spectral database (newbury2024testingtheaccuracy pages 16-18, goldstein2018quantitativeanalysisthe pages 1-4).

### Recommendations for AlSi10Mg Calibration

For calibrating quantitative SEM-EDS on LPBF AlSi10Mg powder and printed parts:

1. **Standards-based protocol**: Use pure Al, Si, and Mg metal standards, or suitable compounds for elements difficult to handle in pure form. Measure k-ratios following the protocol described by Goldstein et al. and Newbury & Ritchie, implementing NIST DTSA-II for matrix corrections (newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 23-25, newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 1-2, goldstein2018quantitativeanalysisthe pages 1-4).

2. **Certified reference materials**: EB 315a (9.88 wt% Si, 0.45 wt% Mg, 2.46 wt% Cu) is the closest available CRM to AlSi10Mg, though Cu content differs. NIS-CRM067, NIS-CRM068, NIS-CRM069, or NIST SRM 85B/87A may provide suitable matrix-matched materials for method validation (seidel2021comparisonofelemental pages 5-7, yamani2021preparationandcertification pages 7-9, yamani2021preparationandcertification pages 3-6).

3. **Surface preparation**: Polish samples to <100 nm RMS roughness using physical abrasives. Avoid chemical polishing. For LPBF powder, embedding and polishing cross-sections is preferable to analyzing irregular particle surfaces (newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 5-6, goldstein2018quantitativeanalysisthe pages 1-4).

4. **Beam energy**: Use 15 or 20 kV for robust analysis of Al, Si, Mg, and typical minor elements (Fe, Mn, Cu). Lower voltages (5-10 kV) improve spatial resolution but increase vulnerability to surface contamination (newbury2024testingtheaccuracy pages 18-22, jonnard2015interlaboratorycomparisonof pages 4-8, newbury2024testingtheaccuracy pages 16-18).

5. **Count statistics**: Acquire sufficient counts for desired precision: 10,000+ counts for 1% precision on major peaks, 100,000+ for 0.3% precision (donovan2014electronprobemicroanalysis pages 20-23). For minor elements (0.2-0.5 wt% Mg), 500-second or longer acquisition times may be needed (newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 23-25).

6. **Independent validation**: Compare SEM-EDS results against spark-OES, LIBS, or ICP-OES measurements on the same samples. Spark-OES typically achieves <10% relative error for elements >0.1 wt% in aluminum alloys (seidel2021comparisonofelemental pages 5-7, seidel2021comparisonofelemental pages 7-8). Alternatively, WDS/EPMA provides higher-precision independent measurements for validation (jonnard2015interlaboratorycomparisonof pages 11-13).

### Limitations and Gaps in Published Evidence

The published literature contains limited aluminum-alloy-specific quantitative SEM-EDS validation data, particularly for:
- LPBF AlSi10Mg and other additive manufacturing aluminum alloys
- 7xxx-series, Al-Cu-Li, Al-Ce-Mg, Al-Zr-Er-Sc, and other specialized alloy families mentioned
- Direct element-by-element EDS accuracy benchmarks for Mg, Si, Cu, Zn, Fe, and Mn in aluminum matrices versus certified compositions
- Open-access EDS spectral databases from certified aluminum alloys

Most quantitative evidence comes from general SDD-EDS validation studies (not aluminum-specific) or comparisons using alternative techniques (XRF, LIBS, spark-OES) rather than SEM-EDS directly. The interlaboratory round robins identified involved Ni-based metallic glass rather than aluminum alloys. These gaps suggest opportunities for future work to establish rigorous aluminum-alloy-specific EDS benchmarks, particularly for the alloy families relevant to additive manufacturing and advanced aerospace/automotive applications.
