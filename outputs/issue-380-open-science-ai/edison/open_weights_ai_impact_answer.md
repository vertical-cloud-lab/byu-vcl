Question: What do studies show about the economic and scientific impact of open-weight or open-source AI models compared with closed models? Include evidence on usage shares, cost and price differences, consumer surplus estimates (e.g., Nagle & Yue on the latent role of open models), innovation and diffusion effects, risk assessments (e.g., the 2024 NTIA report on dual-use foundation models with widely available model weights), and trends in model openness by sector (academia, industry, government) and by country over time (e.g., Epoch AI data, Stanford AI Index).

## The Economic and Scientific Impact of Open-Weight and Open-Source AI Models Compared with Closed Models

### 1. Usage Shares and Market Structure

Despite substantial cost advantages, open-source AI models account for a minority of inference market usage. Analysis of the LLM inference API market shows that open-source models represent less than 30% of market share on average, even though they are approximately 87% cheaper than comparable closed-source models conditional on model intelligence (demirer2025theemergingmarket pages 46-49, demirer2025theemergingmarket pages 20-23). This gap suggests that buyers value dimensions beyond price—including capability, reliability, ecosystem integration, and support. Nevertheless, the open-weight ecosystem has grown rapidly: by May 2025, more than 1.75 million open models were hosted on Hugging Face, and DeepSeek R1 alone exceeded 700,000 downloads from that platform in April 2025 (reineking2025debunkingtheartificial pages 14-16).

According to Epoch AI data reported in the Stanford AI Index 2025, the most common release mechanism for notable AI models in 2024 was API access (32.8%), followed by open weights with restricted use (18.03%), unreleased models (16.39%), unrestricted open weights (11.48%), noncommercial open weights (9.84%), and hosted access without an API (8.20%) (maslej2025artificialintelligenceindex pages 50-52). Thus, "open versus closed" is best understood as a spectrum rather than a binary.

### 2. Cost and Price Differences

The cost advantage of open models has been documented across multiple settings. In the inference API market, open-source models are estimated to be 87% cheaper than closed-source models at comparable intelligence levels (demirer2025theemergingmarket pages 20-23). Production case studies show even larger savings: replacing GPT-4 with self-hosted open-source small language models (SLMs) yielded cost reductions of 5× to 29× in a real product deployment (irugalbandara2024scalingdownto pages 2-2, irugalbandara2024scalingdownto pages 1-2). In a laboratory-scale evaluation, fine-tuned open-weight model inference cost $0.31 versus $2.65 for GPT-4-Turbo—an approximately 88% reduction—with competitive or superior quality after domain adaptation (wolfe2024laboratoryscaleaiopenweight pages 2-3, wolfe2024laboratoryscaleaiopenweight pages 1-2).

The broader trend in LLM inference pricing has been dramatic. Economy-tier models exhibit a price half-life of approximately 1.10 years—substantially faster than Moore's Law—while mid-tier models have a 1.55-year half-life (du2603tieredsupermooreslaw pages 3-8, du2603tieredsupermooreslaw pages 1-3). GPT-3-level input pricing fell from $60 per million tokens in June 2020 to approximately $0.10 per million tokens for economy models by early 2025, a roughly 600-fold nominal reduction (du2603tieredsupermooreslaw pages 3-8). Quality-adjusted frontier costs have fallen approximately 5–10× per year, with algorithmic efficiency gains (after adjusting for hardware improvements) contributing roughly 3× per year for open-weight models (gundlach2025thepriceof pages 2-5, gundlach2025thepriceof pages 1-2, gundlach2025thepriceof pages 8-9). Open-source model prices change more frequently (10.5% of usage-weighted provider-model-week observations) compared to closed-source models (0.9%), reflecting more intense provider-level competition (demirer2025theemergingmarket pages 16-20).

### 3. Consumer Surplus and the Latent Role of Open Models

Nagle and Yue (2025), in their paper "The Latent Role of Open Models in the AI Economy," examine the broader economic role of open models in the AI ecosystem. While this paper was identified by multiple sources and cited in the literature (demirer2025theemergingmarket pages 46-49), its full text was not obtainable for direct analysis. The broader evidence base suggests that open models generate substantial but partially hidden economic value. Analogous to the open-source software ecosystem—which is estimated to generate over $8 trillion in value and appears in 96% of commercial software (garcia2023commentsofpublic pages 9-11)—open AI models likely create significant consumer surplus through lower prices, expanded access, greater customization, and competitive pressure on closed providers. The finding that open models are ~87% cheaper yet hold less than 30% of market share implies that the competitive discipline they impose benefits even users of closed models (demirer2025theemergingmarket pages 46-49, demirer2025theemergingmarket pages 20-23).

### 4. Stock Market Evidence on Value Redistribution

Event-study evidence from 39 major LLM releases (November 2022–July 2026) reveals that open-weight and closed model releases redistribute value across the AI value chain in opposite directions. Open-weight releases generated a software-minus-compute spread of +2.20% (software firms: +1.28%, compute firms: −0.91%), while closed releases produced a spread of −2.30% (software firms: −2.11%, compute firms: +0.18%). The 4.5-percentage-point difference between open and closed releases was statistically significant (Welch t = 2.98; permutation p = 0.004) (huang2026openweightversusclosed pages 3-7, huang2026openweightversusclosed pages 7-9, huang2026openweightversusclosed pages 1-3). This suggests that open weights reduce expected rents associated with scarce upstream compute infrastructure while expanding opportunities for downstream software and application providers.

### 5. Innovation and Diffusion Effects

Open-weight models function as important engines of innovation and technology diffusion. Structurally, openness reduces downstream fine-tuning costs, enables self-hosting and local deployment, and broadens participation in adaptation and experimentation (xu2510theeconomicsof pages 1-3, jamison2026competingforthe pages 6-9). A structural model of foundation-model openness estimated that short-term benefits of openness account for less than half of its contemporaneous costs, implying that longer-term considerations—particularly knowledge spillovers from downstream developers that improve future model generations—are the primary rationale for open strategies (jamison2026competingforthe pages 6-9, jamison2026competingforthe pages 4-6). Empirically, for-profit organizations are 14–18% less likely to open-source models, but Big Tech firms are approximately 20% more likely than other for-profit organizations to do so, reflecting strategic ecosystem considerations (habibi2501opensourcinggpts pages 1-4).

In science, the diffusion of open models has been particularly pronounced. Since 2019, 92% of adopted foundation models in scientific papers were open-weight, and open models were customized 42% of the time versus only 23% for closed models (trisovic2511therapidgrowth pages 9-13). Foundation-model use in scientific publications reached 1.4% of all publications by 2024, with especially rapid growth in chemistry and biology (trisovic2026openscienceclosed pages 2-3). Open models enable researchers to download, adapt, and deploy models locally, supporting reproducibility and domain-specific innovation (kapoor2024onthesocietal pages 3-4, kapoor2024onthesocietal pages 1-2).

### 6. Performance Convergence

The performance gap between open-weight and closed models has narrowed dramatically. On the LMSYS Chatbot Arena, the leading closed-weight model's advantage over the leading open-weight model declined from 8.0% in January 2024 to 1.7% by February 2025. On MMLU, the gap fell from 15.9 points in late 2023 to just 0.1 point at the end of 2024, driven largely by Meta's Llama 3.1 and DeepSeek V3 (maslej2025artificialintelligenceindex pages 96-98). This convergence strengthens the competitive pressure that open models exert across the market.

### 7. Risk Assessments: The 2024 NTIA Report

The 2024 NTIA report on dual-use foundation models with widely available model weights adopted a conditional, risk-based approach rather than recommending a blanket prohibition on open weights (iii2024aifoundationmodels pages 13-16). NTIA identified substantial benefits of open availability, including strengthening cyber deterrence and defense, supporting safety research and vulnerability discovery, and enabling transparency and accountability through independent third-party audits (iii2024aifoundationmodels pages 13-16). At the same time, the report identified corresponding risks: widely available models could reduce barriers for nonexperts seeking to develop chemical, biological, radiological, and nuclear (CBRN) weapons; facilitate offensive cyber operations; and enable disinformation at scale (iii2024aifoundationmodels pages 13-16).

Academic assessments have emphasized the concept of *marginal risk*—the additional risk attributable to open models beyond what is already possible with closed models or pre-existing technologies. Kapoor et al. (2024) concluded that existing research is "insufficient to effectively characterize the marginal risk of open foundation models relative to pre-existing technologies" (kapoor2024onthesocietal pages 4-5, kapoor2024onthesocietal pages 1-2). Bommasani et al. (2024), writing in *Science*, found that for disinformation, dissemination and platform control are more important bottlenecks than content generation; for biosecurity, comparable information is already publicly available; and for cybersecurity, open code models could strengthen defensive capabilities alongside any offensive potential (bommasani2024considerationsforgoverning pages 4-6). Open-weight models present a larger attack surface because safeguards can be removed and distribution is irreversible, but the evidence base for quantifying incremental harm remains limited (casper2026opentechnicalproblems pages 1-3).

### 8. Trends in Model Openness by Sector and Country

**By sector:** Industry has become the dominant producer of notable AI models. According to Epoch AI data reported in the Stanford AI Index 2025, industry produced 90.2% of notable models in 2024, with industry–academia collaborations at 8.2% and industry–government collaborations at 1.64%; standalone academia or government produced no notable models in that year's accounting (maslej2025artificialintelligenceindex pages 47-48, maslej2025artificialintelligenceindex pages 48-50). Google led overall with 187 notable models from 2014–2024, followed by Meta (82), Microsoft (39), and OpenAI (36). Academic institutions such as Carnegie Mellon and Stanford (25 each), Tsinghua and UC Berkeley (22 each) remained contributors but at much smaller scale (maslej2025artificialintelligenceindex pages 48-50).

**By country:** The United States led in 2024 with 40 notable models, followed by China with 15 and France with 3 (maslej2025artificialintelligenceindex pages 47-48). However, China has emerged as the dominant source of open-weight models adopted in science: by 2026, Chinese-developed model families represented 61% of open-weight selections in single-family scientific papers, with Qwen alone accounting for 83% of that share (dunivin2608whousesopenweight pages 7-8, dunivin2608whousesopenweight pages 1-2). China's research community shows the highest public-funding share and lowest API-only engagement, while the Global South exhibits a contrasting pattern—API-only engagement rose from 0.4% to 10.0% of engaged papers, indicating growing dependence on externally hosted, often closed, models (trisovic2026openscienceclosed pages 1-2, trisovic2026openscienceclosed pages 2-3).

**Open science patterns:** Public funding is associated with greater open-weight model use, while private funding and industry cloud credits are associated with closed-model and API-only reliance (trisovic2026openscienceclosed pages 1-2, trisovic2026openscienceclosed pages 2-3). The gap across research systems is "not one of AI adoption but of control"—off-the-shelf use is rising across fields and country groups, but dependence on proprietary infrastructure is diverging (trisovic2026openscienceclosed pages 1-2).

### Summary

The literature reveals that open-weight AI models exert substantial competitive pressure on closed alternatives through dramatically lower costs (87% cheaper conditional on capability), rapidly narrowing performance gaps, and broad diffusion in science and industry. Open models drive innovation through knowledge spillovers, downstream adaptation, and ecosystem expansion, while creating intertemporal incentives for firms to trade short-run monetization for long-run platform growth. The inference market has experienced "super-Moore's Law" pricing declines, with economy-tier half-lives of approximately 1.1 years, driven partly by competition among open-model hosts. However, open models remain a minority of commercial usage, their production is overwhelmingly concentrated in industry rather than academia, and their geographic diffusion reflects national technology ecosystems—particularly China's growing dominance in open-weight scientific use. Risk assessments, including the 2024 NTIA report, have adopted conditional, evidence-based frameworks rather than recommending blanket restrictions, recognizing that the marginal risks of open weights over existing technologies remain empirically uncertain while the innovation, competition, and transparency benefits are substantial.
