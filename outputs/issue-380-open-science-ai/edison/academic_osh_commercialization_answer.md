# Commercialization of academic open-source laboratory hardware

**The documented pathway is to publish a reproducible design while selling the things that publication does not supply:** manufactured and tested instruments, convenient kits, specialist components, integration, training, and dependable support. Openness permits competing manufacturers; it does not eliminate customers’ willingness to pay for an instrument that works on arrival. Evidence of commercial *activity* is considerably stronger than evidence of company revenue or profit for individual academic projects. (bowman2023improvinginstrumentreproducibility pages 1-4, wenzel2023openhardwarefrom pages 17-20, pearce2017emergingbusinessmodels pages 4-6)

## Pathways and observed outcomes

**Product companies and local manufacture.** OpenFlexure Industries is listed as a manufacturer of the OpenFlexure microscope and block stage. Separately, Tanzanian partner Bongo Tech and Research Labs manufactures an OpenFlexure-derived **sayansiScope** and was working toward medical-device certification in the 2024 account. OpenFlexure had been built or used in **more than 45 countries** and had reached **technology-readiness level 7**, including operational testing with patient samples; neither figure is a sales total. The cited material does not establish that either manufacturer is a *formal university spinout*, nor disclose its revenue. Other documented product businesses include Opentrons, openUC2 and Sci-Bots, which is listed for the DropBot digital-microfluidics controller. These are examples of commercialization around open designs, not evidence that all have the same institutional ownership or licensing arrangement. (wenzel2023openhardwarefrom pages 17-20, meistring2024helmholtzopenscience pages 113-151, knapper2024developingtheopenflexure pages 1-3, knapper2024developingtheopenflexure pages 3-4, knapper2024developingtheopenflexure pages 9-11, knapper2024developingtheopenflexure pages 4-6)

**Assembled robots and application-specific integration.** An early Opentrons **OT.One was listed at US$3,000**; that is a product price, *not* Opentrons revenue. During the COVID-19 response, an account reported an Opentrons-supported workflow developed with diagnostic partners for **up to 2,400 tests per day**, associated with US FDA emergency-use authorization, and reported automation at Barcelona’s Hospital Clínic diagnostic centre. The regulatory claim concerns that testing workflow; it should not be read as approval of every use of an open pipetting robot. An academic lab can also publish a liquid handler without establishing a seller: PHIL, FINDUS and OpenWorkstation document buildable laboratory-automation designs, but their reported prototype or component costs are not evidence of commercial sales. (pearce2017emergingbusinessmodels pages 4-6, chagas2020leveragingopenhardware pages 8-9, eggert2020openworkstationamodular pages 12-13)

**Kit sellers, component suppliers and distributors.** Imperial College London’s **openFrame** team worked with Cairn Research Ltd; its paper states that Cairn **would sell** openFrame components to users who want to assemble instruments but not fabricate the components. This documents a stated supplier route, not a measured volume of completed sales. The paper estimates **under £10,000** in components for some self-assembled bright-field or fluorescence configurations and approximately **£35,000** for its motorized easySTORM configuration, excluding a computer; both are *assembly-cost estimates*. Jubilee illustrates a transition from sourcing parts from numerous vendors to a complete kit offered by a US vendor in February 2022. Its **170 independently built machines** by then establish uptake before the kit launch, not 170 kit sales. More generally, LabMaker and Adafruit are identified as vendors of designs developed by others; marketplaces and resellers—including Tindie and, in broader open-hardware examples, MatterHackers and Adafruit—can provide distribution without acquiring exclusive design rights. (bowman2023improvinginstrumentreproducibility pages 1-4, wenzel2023openhardwarefrom pages 17-20, pearce2017emergingbusinessmodels pages 4-6, dunn2023jubileeacase pages 6-8, lightley2023openframeamodular pages 11-12, dunn2023jubileeacase pages 3-4, lightley2023openframeamodular pages 1-2)

**Crowdfunded small batches and educational kits.** The University of Utah’s open **CLSLab:Light** self-driving-laboratory teaching demonstrator went beyond a downloadable design: the authors report **two successful GroupGets rounds totaling 39 kits**, some used in university teaching. Their April 2023 disclosure gives a **US$80 list price plus shipping**, **US$51.19 hardware**, **US$15.57 platform fees**, about **US$6 labor/testing**, and approximately **US$2.24 profit per kit** before the effects of bulk discounts. These are unusually transparent *per-kit figures*, not annual revenue. The wider literature identifies crowdfunding as a way to finance pre-sales and test demand, and group purchasing as a means of reducing small-batch production risk; neither guarantees a scalable business. (parker2021thepotentialfor pages 13-16, lo2024reviewoflowcost pages 8-10, baird2023buildinga“hello pages 18-21)

**High-volume manufacture is possible, but projections must stay projections.** Stanford-origin Foldscope provides a different scale of example. Manu Prakash reported in 2017 that **50,000 microscopes had been made and sold**; his statement that the team *would ship one million that year*, and a separately reported million-device distribution goal, are **targets or forecasts**, not independently verified million-unit sales. The reported **less-than-US$1 production cost** likewise is not a sale price or revenue. Federal funding supported its early pilot. (parker2021thepotentialfor pages 1-6, parker2021thepotentialfor pages 13-16, pandika2017aconversationwith pages 1-2)

**Institutional licensing and industry co-development.** The CERN Open Hardware Repository shows a transfer route in which companies help develop or manufacture openly documented designs and the research institution serves as an initial customer and quality signal. CERN reported that, by the end of **2014**, **eight CERN designs were commercially available** and **more than 1,200 units** had been produced for almost **100 users**; these are totals across multiple designs, not OpenFlexure sales or a monetary revenue figure. The account identified **17 companies** involved with the repository and acknowledged that downstream diffusion was difficult to track. It also describes an INVENIO cloud-service spinout arising from demand for support of CERN open-source *software*: a useful example of service-based transfer, but not a hardware-sales outcome. (nilsen2016knowledgetransferat pages 3-4, nilsen2016knowledgetransferat pages 4-5)

## Licensing and how revenue is captured

Open licensing allows manufacture and commercial sale rather than reserving sales to the academic inventors. CERN’s original **Open Hardware Licence** was described as covering use, modification and distribution of design documentation and products made from it, with covered developments shared on the same terms. For a **specific microscope**, Imperial’s openFrame paper expressly identifies its core CAD-file license as **CERN-OHL-P version 2**, the permissive variant, and contemplates incorporation into companies’ commercial instruments. The paper’s own Creative Commons publication license is a different matter. Likewise, an OpenFlexure clinical paper says its **Figure 1** was reproduced under **CC BY-SA**; that caption alone does **not** establish that every OpenFlexure hardware file, software component and trademark has that license. A buyer or manufacturer must check the applicable files and versions rather than infer a project-wide license from an article or figure. (nilsen2016knowledgetransferat pages 3-4, lightley2023openframeamodular pages 1-2, knapper2024developingtheopenflexure media bad5fe8d, knapper2024developingtheopenflexure pages 3-4)

The corresponding business models are **manufacture and quality assurance; kits and hard-to-source components; customization and system integration; and installation, calibration, validation, maintenance and training**. Some are demonstrated above, whereas others are identified in the business-model literature as opportunities, not proven income streams for each named project. Complementary-product sales are another documented strategy: NECi released an open photometer design while selling the enzymes used with it, so additional manufacturers could enlarge demand for its principal product. Open branding and support can also distinguish an original vendor from a cheaper copy. No license, by itself, provides clinical certification or assigns manufacturer responsibility for product quality. (pearce2017emergingbusinessmodels pages 4-6, parker2021thepotentialfor pages 13-16, hsing2024ecologyandconservation pages 2-3, pearce2017emergingbusinessmodels pages 6-7)

## Barriers to turning a published prototype into a supported product

* **Certification, safety and liability.** Medical certification attaches to a **manufactured, assembled device**, not automatically to an open design. The sayansiScope team described certification as work in progress, requiring documented quality management under **ISO 13485** and risk management under **ISO 14971**. Different builders, components and modifications create calibration and traceability problems; the OpenFlexure team proposed testing each completed microscope, periodically rechecking essential characteristics, recording results and constraining clinical software to approved configurations. Open-source diagnostic assays and equipment still face jurisdictional regulation: an unapproved DIY device cannot simply substitute for a validated clinical system. Responsibility for calibration and conformity can be unclear when designers, builders and users are different parties—precisely the issue a commercial manufacturer or certified laboratory must resolve, rather than a liability that an open license removes. (parker2021thepotentialfor pages 10-13, meistring2024helmholtzopenscience pages 113-151, chagas2020leveragingopenhardware pages 8-9, knapper2024developingtheopenflexure pages 3-4, knapper2024developingtheopenflexure pages 4-6, knapper2024developingtheopenflexure pages 9-11)
* **Support and manufacturability.** Early clinician feedback on OpenFlexure found a preference for a device assembled by an **external supplier** and returned to that supplier for maintenance, despite its self-build design. Open projects need sustained documentation, usable interfaces, spare parts and technical assistance after a grant ends. Jubilee’s motion platform comprised **more than 800 components** sourced from over a dozen vendors, and interviewed builders commonly spent months assembling it; its eventual kit addresses a real logistical burden. Procurement rules can also make inexpensive overseas components difficult for universities to buy. (dunn2023jubileeacase pages 6-8, keller2023developmentandsharing pages 13-15, knapper2024developingtheopenflexure pages 6-7, knapper2024developingtheopenflexure pages 7-9, knapper2024developingtheopenflexure pages 9-11)
* **Cloning, incentives and finance.** Open designs permit competing copies. The literature documents Arduino clones and discusses branding, warranty, service, rapid iteration and complementary sales as responses; **it does not establish a quantified cloning loss for OpenFlexure or Opentrons**. Lack of exclusive IP may trouble investors, while university patent procedures, limited business-development capacity and the gap between prototype grants and production funding can delay transfer. Conversely, treating every copy as lost revenue would ignore legitimate independent manufacture and the potential for wider adoption. (wenzel2210globaltechnologyaccess pages 20-25, pearce2017emergingbusinessmodels pages 4-6, parker2021thepotentialfor pages 13-16, parker2021thepotentialfor pages 21-23, pearce2017emergingbusinessmodels pages 6-7)

**Bottom line.** The literature documents actual firms, supplied components, modest crowdfunded kit batches, clinical-development partnerships and some unit-level outcomes. It rarely reports audited revenue for the named academic-origin microscope and automation companies. The defensible conclusion is therefore that **several commercial routes have been demonstrated**, while reliable product-specific revenue, sustained profitability and—in many cases—formal spinout status remain unverified in the cited record. (wenzel2023openhardwarefrom pages 17-20, lightley2023openframeamodular pages 11-12, nilsen2016knowledgetransferat pages 4-5, lo2024reviewoflowcost pages 8-10)
