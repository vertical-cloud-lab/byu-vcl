Question: How has the openness of research outputs (open access publications, code release, data sharing, model weights) changed over time across academia, government and national laboratories, and industry, particularly in the United States? Include evidence on industry's growing share of AI research (e.g., Ahmed, Wahed & Thompson 2023 in Science), the decline of corporate basic research publication (e.g., Arora, Belenzon & Patacconi), national laboratory software release practices, and US federal open-science policy (2013 OSTP Holdren memo, 2022 OSTP Nelson memo, NIH 2023 data management and sharing policy).

# Openness of research outputs across U.S. sectors

**The trend is not a single movement from closed to open research.** Access to scholarly articles has broadened, and sharing code and data has become more common in prominent AI conferences. At the same time, established U.S. corporations have published less of their internal scientific research, while industry has gained a much larger role in producing frontier AI systems. A publicly readable paper, a reusable dataset, licensed source code, and downloadable model weights are distinct outputs; evidence of one does not establish the availability of the others. (arora2015killingthegolden pages 1-4, coakley2026theshifttoward pages 1-3, ahmed2023thegrowinginfluence pages 1-2, bommasani2024thefoundationmodel pages 1-3)

## Academia and scholarly publishing

Open access to publications has grown, but estimates depend on the papers and access categories counted. A large-scale study cited in subsequent bibliometric work estimated that **at least 28%** of the literature it examined was openly accessible, rising to **45% for papers published in 2015**; these are broad scholarly-publication estimates, not U.S.-academia-only rates. Moreover, free-to-read access need not confer an open reuse license. The retrieved full text labeled as that original study was mislinked to European Commission guidance, so these figures are supported here by a study quoting its results rather than by a verified copy of the original article. (wang2018thestateand pages 1-5, piwowar2020thestateof pages 8-11)

For a specifically U.S. baseline, Schares identified **1,326,472 publications acknowledging federal funding in 2017–2021**, approximately **265,294 annually**. About **26%** of the studied papers from each of 2017–2020 were classified as closed access, increasing to nearly **32%** for 2021 papers. Access was assessed after publication, so these figures do not measure whether an article was *immediately* available, nor do they measure the effect of the later 2022 federal guidance. (schares2023impactofthe pages 6-9, schares2023impactofthe pages 13-17)

Research artifacts show a different trajectory. In an analysis of **56,800 papers across five leading AI conferences**, the share reporting **both code and data sharing** rose from **11% in 2014 to 64% in 2024**. These conferences contain academic, industry, and joint work; the result is **not** an academia-only sharing rate. The authors’ estimates of improved reproducibility are inferred from documentation, not from rerunning every experiment. Research organizations also still have less-developed policies for recognizing and maintaining software than for managing research data. (coakley2026theshifttoward pages 1-3, barker2025institutionalpolicypathways pages 1-2)

## Industry: less corporate science publishing, greater AI-frontier influence

Arora, Belenzon, and Patacconi document declining scientific-journal publication by **publicly traded American companies during 1980–2007**, particularly large firms and across multiple industries, with biotechnology an exception. Firm patenting increased, and the value associated with patents did not show the corresponding decline. They interpret the pattern as reduced investment in *internal scientific capability* and greater emphasis on development and external inventions—not simply a decision to conceal an unchanged volume of basic research. Their publication measure nevertheless cannot count unpublished research directly, and the historical result should not be generalized to every company or to AI alone. (arora2015killingthegolden pages 1-4)

The AI-era result is complementary, not contradictory. Ahmed, Wahed, and Thompson report that papers with **at least one industry coauthor** grew from **22% of presentations at leading AI conferences in 2000 to 38% in 2020**. Industry’s share of the **largest models** grew from **11% in 2010 to 96% in 2021**; industry models averaged **29 times** the size of academic models in 2021. On their selected benchmarks, academia led **77% of the time before 2020**, whereas industry alone or with collaborators led **100% since 2020**. The conference statistic includes collaborations, and these measures of production and performance do **not** measure whether firms published all their research or released training data, code, or weights. The study’s talent evidence draws particularly on North American universities; its output series should not be read as U.S.-only. (ahmed2023thegrowinginfluence pages 1-2, ahmed2023thegrowinginfluence pages 2-3, ahmed2023thegrowinginfluence media 196a32de)

**Frontier-model openness is selective and variable.** The Foundation Model Transparency Index scored major developers an average **37/100 in 2023** and **58/100 in 2024**, but found persistent weaknesses in disclosure of upstream inputs, including training data and compute. Its **2025** assessment averaged about **40/100**, with training data and compute still particularly opaque. Because the 2025 indicators and company sample changed, the decline is **not a like-for-like longitudinal estimate**. Neither an aggregate transparency score nor a paper or API release establishes that model weights are available; conversely, released weights do not by themselves disclose the training dataset or make training reproducible. (bommasani2024thefoundationmodel pages 1-3, wan2026the2025foundation pages 1-3)

## Government and national laboratories

U.S. national laboratories demonstrate substantial **but conditional** software openness. A survey cited by Barker and colleagues identified **2,005 open-source repositories associated with Department of Energy laboratories**, just over half updated in the preceding **12 weeks**. A separate Code.gov analysis catalogued **3,760 DOE projects**, **76% hosted on GitHub**; about **15%**, mainly national-laboratory projects, were hosted on `.gov` sites, where distribution can differ from GitHub. These are inventories of identified projects or public repositories, **not denominators for all software created by laboratories**, and they do not show that every repository is usable or openly licensed. (barker2025institutionalpolicypathways pages 1-2, shrivastava2024measuringpublicopensource pages 1-3, shrivastava2024measuringpublicopensource pages 3-5)

Release can also occur in stages: NREL’s *System Advisor Model* first became publicly available in **2007**, but its **source code was released as open source in 2017**. Laboratory practices distinguish publicly available tools from source-code release and account for copyright, licensing, review, and restricted information. A federal-laboratory survey found different mixes of open-source, noncommercial, unrestricted, and restricted releases among contractor-operated and government-operated laboratories; security-related restrictions were particularly relevant to some DOE contractor-operated facilities. Thus, a public report, downloadable application, or code catalog entry does not establish that all associated code or data can be redistributed. (blair2018systemadvisormodel pages 14-20, blair2018systemadvisormodel pages 20-24, leech2021prospectiveeconomicimpacts pages 35-37, weck2021owlreleaseprocess. pages 7-9)

## The U.S. federal policy sequence

- **2013 OSTP “Holdren” memorandum:** Directed agencies with **more than $100 million in annual R&D expenditures** to develop public-access plans for federally funded publications and research data needed to validate findings. Publication access could follow an embargo of **up to 12 months**; the guidance also recognized privacy and proprietary interests. (bulock2023publicaccessto pages 1-3, spectorbagdady2024“adoubleedgedsword” pages 2-4)
- **2022 OSTP “Nelson” memorandum:** Extended the public-access direction to **all federal research-funding agencies**, regardless of that spending threshold, and removed the publication embargo: covered peer-reviewed publications should be publicly accessible **immediately upon publication**, alongside stronger expectations for access to supporting research data and agency data-sharing plans. Agencies were directed to develop implementation plans, with the subsequent implementation timetable reaching the **end of 2025**. This is a change in federal guidance and agency requirements, not proof that every covered output was already compliant or openly licensed. (schares2023impactofthe pages 1-3, bulock2023publicaccessto pages 1-3, harbeson2025thelegalbasis pages 1-3)
- **NIH Data Management and Sharing policy, effective January 25, 2023:** Broadens the expectation that NIH-funded or -conducted projects **generating scientific data** plan for management and sharing, rather than applying only above the former funding threshold. Data should be shared by the **first associated publication or the end of the award period, whichever comes first**. Ethical, legal, consent, privacy, and technical limits can justify restrictions, including controlled access for sensitive data. Sharing scientific data is not a blanket instruction to post identifiable health records publicly or to release every software package and AI model weight. (spectorbagdady2024“adoubleedgedsword” pages 4-5, evans2026thelawof pages 5-7, rivera2024publicperspectiveson pages 1-2)

**Overall:** Public funders have tightened expectations for access to articles and scientifically useful data, while AI venues have seen markedly more reported code-and-data sharing. But industry’s rising control of compute-intensive AI makes *which* research is conducted and *which artifacts* are disclosed increasingly consequential. Cross-sector claims that research has simply become “more open” or “less open” miss this divergence between readable publications, reproducible methods, reusable data, source code, and model weights. (coakley2026theshifttoward pages 1-3, ahmed2023thegrowinginfluence pages 1-2, bommasani2024thefoundationmodel pages 1-3, arora2015killingthegolden pages 1-4)
