# Funding and sustaining open scientific infrastructure

**Bottom line.** Open access is not cost-free operation. These services require continuing expenditure on expert curation, software maintenance, storage, security, user support, and governance. The literature finds a persistent mismatch: research grants are usually time-limited and reward new features, while trusted repositories and software need dependable funding for routine work over decades. The five named services illustrate different mixtures of public grants, institutional hosting, philanthropy, collective membership, and paid premium services; their published cost figures cover different years and activities and should not be compared as if they were equivalent annual budgets. (hooft2025financingmodelsfor pages 1-3, hooft2025financingmodelsfor pages 3-4, hooft2025financingmodelsfor pages 6-8, hooft2025financingmodelsfor pages 14-16)

## What failure and retrenchment look like

The risk is documented, not merely theoretical. The ProteomeXchange consortium reported that **Tranche and Peptidome closed after NIH support ceased, with partial loss of data**. Another case study records **NIAID’s nonrenewal of VEuPathDB support in September 2024**; that support had covered approximately **60% of operating costs**. VEuPathDB continued with stopgap philanthropic and host support while developing tiered subscriptions. Thus, loss of a grant can mean a changed access model as well as a threatened shutdown. The same study documents a contrasting response: the Biodiversity Heritage Library formed a consortium and subsequently relied on organizational memberships, while MorphoBank adopted voluntary memberships but was still seeking enough support to meet its needs. (imker2025sustainabilityduringinstability pages 1-4, imker2025sustainabilityduringinstability pages 8-11, imker2025sustainabilityduringinstability pages 11-13)

**Service reduction need not mean that the underlying platform disappears.** A 2024 examination of COS’s preprint ecosystem reported **29 listed preprint servers, 14 of which had ceased publishing or were no longer hosted by COS/OSF**, and discussed annual service charges as a possible contributor. That is a secondary account of *individual hosted services*, not evidence that the main OSF shut down or proof that every departure had the same cause. Repository dependencies can also fail more quietly when an API or data-release format changes, breaking downstream institutional workflows and creating new, often unbudgeted maintenance work. (domenighetti2026optimizingthemonitoring pages 78-81, hooft2025financingmodelsfor pages 3-4)

## Scientific software is part of the same infrastructure

Open-source scientific software often relies on grant-funded development and volunteer maintenance, even when many researchers or AI systems depend on it. A workshop report argues that paying indefinitely through sequential innovation grants is generally untenable; it proposes stable infrastructure-style funding, institutional membership, possible endowments, campus open-source coordination, research-software-engineer careers, and recognition of software in hiring and promotion. It also stresses an important limit: not *every* research script needs perpetual active maintenance; some should instead be documented and archived. A study of Astropy and research on grant-funded software transitions likewise treat community governance and maintainer succession—not merely preservation of code—as sustainability problems. (mccracken2026sustainingopensource pages 6-9, mccracken2026sustainingopensource pages 9-12, mccracken2026sustainingopensource pages 12-16, mccracken2026sustainingopensource pages 16-19)

Funder guidance recommends explicitly financing maintenance, documentation, governance, inclusive contributor communities, and scholarly credit, not only novel code. Fiscal sponsors and multi-project consortia can aggregate administrative work and fund paid maintainers; the Open Molecular Software Foundation’s collaboration across molecular-modeling projects is one proposed model. Such arrangements can combine public research grants, institutional resources, philanthropy, and industry contributions while keeping shared code open. (mccracken2026sustainingopensource pages 9-12, hooft2025financingmodelsfor pages 10-12)

## What changes as AI uses the commons

AI increases the *value* of curated open collections and can increase demand for machine-readable data, APIs, bulk transfer, and quality assurance. PDB’s role in protein-structure prediction is a concrete example of downstream AI value. Repository traffic also merits careful accounting: a PRIDE study of **159.3 million downloads in 2021–2025** classified **48.2% of traffic as automated bots** and found that bot traffic rose **sixteenfold**. **The study does not establish that those bots were generative-AI crawlers, or quantify the resulting bandwidth cost.** It does show why raw downloads are unreliable measures of human reuse and why capacity planning and bot-aware statistics matter. (berman2025proteindatabank pages 8-10, hewapathirana2026quantifyingdatareuse pages 7-7, hewapathirana2026quantifyingdatareuse pages 4-4)

The strongest financing proposals follow from that distinction between an open *commons* and costly *services on top of it*:

1. **Fund recurring work explicitly.** Price curation, preservation, security, basic access, maintenance, and the organization that provides them separately from new-feature development. Hooft and Roos propose matching each activity to an appropriate payer: long-horizon public or host support for core operations; project grants for bounded development; partnerships for shared governance; and charges for genuinely high-marginal-cost premium services. They warn that deposit fees can disadvantage poorer institutions and cannot reliably finance perpetual care of previously deposited material. (hooft2025financingmodelsfor pages 4-6, hooft2025financingmodelsfor pages 6-8, hooft2025financingmodelsfor pages 8-10, hooft2025financingmodelsfor pages 10-12, hooft2025financingmodelsfor pages 12-14, hooft2025financingmodelsfor pages 14-16)
2. **Pool beneficiary contributions without making ordinary access contingent on payment.** arXiv’s library memberships, international biodata funder coordination, and domain-specific consortia illustrate collective support. Public agencies, universities, and large commercial or AI beneficiaries could contribute to common operating funds; differentiated high-volume API or service charges are a *policy option*, not a proven universal solution or a claim that free reuse should end. The literature also notes that full government funding or an endowment could provide continuity, but both are difficult to secure in practice. (rieger2015sustainabilityscholarlyrepository pages 1-3, imker2025sustainabilityduringinstability pages 1-4, hooft2025financingmodelsfor pages 10-12, hooft2025financingmodelsfor pages 14-16, mccracken2026sustainingopensource pages 9-12)
3. **Reduce avoidable machine-use costs and protect openness.** Provide documented bulk snapshots, caching or mirrors, sensible API tiers, and bot-aware metrics so automated users need not repeatedly scrape interactive services. Retain openly reusable datasets, interoperable identifiers, preservation and exit plans, and community oversight; charging for high-capacity computation is different from enclosing the underlying scholarly record. These are recommendations inferred from documented usage patterns and the OpenAlex model, **not measured savings for all five platforms**. (borrego2512openalexfeaturesadvantages pages 1-2, domenighetti2026optimizingthemonitoringc pages 59-62, hewapathirana2026quantifyingdatareuse pages 7-7, hewapathirana2026quantifyingdatareuse pages 7-8, mabile2025recommendationsonopen pages 6-7)

**Assessment.** The most defensible conclusion is not that one revenue model will sustain every service. It is that grant-funded expansion, library or institutional stewardship, international consortia, philanthropy, potential endowment income, and paid high-cost services each finance different parts of the lifecycle. Long-term obligations need long-term commitments, transparent activity-level accounts, and governance that keeps the scientific commons accessible even when AI and other intensive users increase demand. (hooft2025financingmodelsfor pages 3-4, hooft2025financingmodelsfor pages 8-10, hooft2025financingmodelsfor pages 14-16, mccracken2026sustainingopensource pages 9-12)
