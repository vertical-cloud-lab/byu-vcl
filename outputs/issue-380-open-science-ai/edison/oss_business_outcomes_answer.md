# Commercial open source versus proprietary software: what the evidence shows

**Bottom line.** There is evidence that firms engaging with open-source software (OSS) can gain productivity, attract financing and become acquisition targets. There is much less evidence that **selling an open-core product, managed OSS service or dual-licensed product** produces higher valuations, faster revenue growth, more IPOs or better survival than selling proprietary software. Many studies measure *using or contributing to* OSS, not the licence of the firm’s principal revenue-generating product. These are different comparisons. (singh2026profitabilityofopensource pages 1-4, conti2021beefingitup pages 1-5, vetter2021acquisitionsandopen pages 111-114, comino2011duallicensingin pages 1-2)

## Firm outcomes and business models

| Outcome | Empirical finding | What it does **not** establish |
|---|---|---|
| **Revenue and employment growth** | A 2011 master’s thesis by Chowdhury linked an older European software-firm survey to accounts. In regressions with hybrid firms as the reference group, the coefficients on *mainly OSS* and *mainly proprietary* were respectively **0.971 (SE 0.496)** and **0.874 (SE 0.264)** for log sales (366 observations); for log headcount they were **0.228 (SE 0.134)** and **0.551 (SE 0.276)** (299 observations). The thesis interprets mainly-OSS firms as growing more in sales but less in employees than mainly-proprietary firms. Crucially, the two coefficients’ separate significance against *hybrids* does **not** establish a statistically significant OSS–proprietary difference; no direct test of that difference is reported in the cited tables. The historical, selected sample also does not isolate modern SaaS or open-core firms. (chowdhury2010theimpactof pages 47-52, chowdhury2010theimpactof pages 55-61, chowdhury2010theimpactof media 25e20b04) | A causal revenue-growth premium, or a reliable numerical percentage by which OSS outgrows proprietary companies. |
| **Profitability and productivity** | Singh *et al.* (2026) study **977 US high-tech firms, 2001–2025**, and estimate that adopting open product development through GitHub increased gross margin by approximately **4%–5% relative to nonadopters**, with specifications including staggered difference-in-differences and synthetic controls. Their mechanism analysis finds a positive indirect productivity effect when outside volunteers account for approximately **35%** of observed contributions; continued internal R&D is important. This covers high-tech firms adopting a development practice, **not** an identified cohort of commercial OSS vendors. Nagle’s *Open Source Software and Firm Productivity* (2019) is reported in the retrieved literature as finding productivity benefits conditional on complementary IT capabilities; its primary full text was unavailable here, so no precise Nagle coefficient is asserted. (singh2026profitabilityofopensource pages 1-4, singh2026profitabilityofopensource pages 33-35) | That every OSS firm has higher margins, or that open-core licensing itself causes the reported gains. |
| **Financing and market value** | In a working-paper analysis linking **160,065 US startups** to GitHub, Conti, Peukert and Roche find that engagement with external OSS communities precedes an **at least 36% increase relative to the mean** in likelihood of first funding, versus matched startups. Engagement also precedes more product launches; the association is stronger for novel technologies and weaker in highly competitive markets. This is **funding probability**, not a valuation comparison, and GitHub engagement does not mean the startup sells OSS. An event study by Aksoy-Yurdagul (2025) uses **149 uncontaminated code-contribution announcements by 65 listed firms**: average abnormal returns are positive, with announcements explicitly seeking user insights associated with approximately **1.42% greater market value** than other contribution announcements. It measures announcement reactions, not an enduring open-core valuation premium. (conti2021beefingitup pages 1-5, conti2021beefingitup pages 20-23, conti2021beefingitup pages 23-26, aksoy‐yurdagul2025generatingvalueby pages 1-2, aksoy‐yurdagul2025generatingvalueby pages 5-6, aksoy‐yurdagul2025generatingvalueby pages 8-9) | Higher private-company valuations or superior long-run shareholder returns for OSS vendors versus otherwise comparable proprietary vendors. |
| **Acquisitions, IPOs and survival** | Vetter’s Crunchbase–GitHub study follows **67,794 young software firms**, of which **2,639 were acquired**; **111 IPOs** and **3,172 cessations of operations** are recorded. Firms whose employees worked on public OSS had a **higher acquisition hazard** than firms without observed OSS activity; more activity correlated with greater hazard. Within the OSS group, neither licence type nor attracting outside pull requests showed a significant acquisition association after controls. The principal acquisition model excludes firms that went public or ceased operations, using those outcomes as competing risks in a robustness analysis. This is an association with **participation**, not an estimate of OSS effects on exit *prices* or a finding that OSS firms survive longer. (vetter2021acquisitionsandopen pages 103-106, vetter2021acquisitionsandopen pages 111-114, vetter2021acquisitionsandopen pages 114-117) | A demonstrated OSS advantage in IPO probability, acquisition proceeds or firm survival. |

**Why model distinctions matter.** An *open-core* firm offers an OSS core alongside proprietary paid features; *SaaS* sells hosting and operations and may or may not have an OSS core; *dual licensing* offers the same code under both OSS and commercial terms. Comino and Manenti’s account of dual licensing is **theoretical**, explaining when community feedback can make it commercially sustainable, not reporting a measured exit or growth advantage. A small, deliberately selected 2025 survey of **44 visible OSS developer tools** reports high funding among open-core/SaaS projects and warns explicitly about estimated private valuations, survivor bias and the absence of proprietary comparators. Its numbers should not be read as a population-wide valuation premium. Red Hat’s reported **$34 billion IBM acquisition** and HashiCorp’s reported **$6.4 billion IBM acquisition in 2025** demonstrate possible large exits, but two selected transactions cannot identify the effect of licensing; HashiCorp had already changed Terraform’s licence by the latter transaction. (comino2011duallicensingin pages 1-2, pandey2025theeconomicsof pages 1-8, pandey2025theeconomicsof pages 8-13, pandey2025theeconomicsof pages 23-27)

## Why firms participate—and what happens when licences change

Lerner and Tirole’s empirical study of nearly **40,000 SourceForge projects** associates licence choice with the intended audience: end-user projects tended toward more restrictive *OSS* licences, whereas developer-oriented and internet projects tended toward less restrictive ones; its corporate-origin analysis is more tentative. Their definition of “restrictive” concerns **copyleft within open source**—it must not be confused with SSPL or BSL’s restrictions on uses that make those licences non-OSS. Fosfuri, Giarratana and Luzzi’s study of **1995–2003 OSS product introductions** finds that greater software-patent stocks predict more OSS releases, software trademarks predict fewer, and hardware trademarks predict more in some categories. These are evidence about firms’ strategic selection and complementary assets, **not** proof of superior returns after release. Nagle’s separate analysis of a French OSS-favouring procurement policy reports increased contributions and IT startup formation compared with controls, but likewise does not compare the financial performance of open-core and proprietary vendors. (lerner2005thescopeof pages 1-5, fosfuri2008thepenguinhas pages 1-2, fosfuri2008thepenguinhas pages 10-12, nagle2019governmenttechnologypolicy pages 1-6)

The four prominent relicensing episodes are distinct from choosing a copyleft OSS licence:

| Vendor change | Documented community or commercial outcome |
|---|---|
| **MongoDB, October 2018:** Community Server changed from AGPL to **SSPL** for subsequent releases. | This is an early instance of a vendor retaining source access while restricting commercial cloud-service use. The evidence assembled here does not establish a comparable sustained community fork or a causal effect on MongoDB revenue or valuation. (fermigier2026valuecapturein pages 22-23, dmitrenko2026whymemorycomponents pages 6-8) |
| **Elastic, announced January 2021; effective for Elasticsearch in February:** Apache 2.0 gave way to **SSPL/Elastic License**. | Amazon launched the Apache-licensed **OpenSearch** fork in April 2021. Elastic added the OSS **AGPLv3** option in August 2024; OpenSearch entered Linux Foundation governance in September 2024. In Foster’s measured periods, Elastic employees already supplied **over 95% of Elasticsearch lines added/deleted** before the change and continued to dominate afterwards. At OpenSearch, Amazon employees supplied **80% of additions** in its first year and **63%** in the measured year before its foundation transfer: diversity improved, but the fork was not initially independent of its corporate sponsor. (fermigier2026valuecapturein pages 22-23, foster2411thenewdynamics pages 8-11, foster2411thenewdynamics pages 11-15) |
| **HashiCorp, August 2023:** future Terraform releases changed from **MPL 2.0 to BSL**. | The Linux Foundation’s **OpenTofu** retained MPL 2.0. The manifesto had support from over **100 companies and 1,000 individuals**, but in its first measured year the main repository had **31 contributors at 11 organizations** making at least five commits; none had previously contributed to Terraform. HashiCorp employees dominated Terraform contributions both before and after relicensing. IBM’s subsequent acquisition cannot, without a counterfactual, be attributed to the licence change. (foster2411thenewdynamics pages 11-15, foster2411thenewdynamics pages 15-18, foster2026freeridingandrebellion pages 14-14, pandey2025theeconomicsof pages 23-27) |
| **Redis, March 2024:** BSD-3 gave way to **RSALv2/SSPLv1** for new Redis releases. | The BSD-3 **Valkey** fork launched under the Linux Foundation that month. In Foster’s 2024 observation windows, **12 non-Redis employees** made at least five Redis commits in the year *before* relicensing, versus **zero** in the following six months; their pre-change contributions were **319 commits**, compared with **164** from the six Redis employees meeting the same threshold. Valkey’s first six months had **29 such contributors at 10 companies**, **18** of whom had previously contributed to Redis. The windows differ in length, and these are contributor counts, not customer migration or financial performance. Redis subsequently offered **AGPLv3** with Redis 8 in 2025. (foster2411thenewdynamics pages 15-18, foster2411thenewdynamics pages 18-21, foster2024thenewdynamics media 6119b756, foster2024thenewdynamics media b2337e5f, dmitrenko2026whymemorycomponents pages 6-8) |

The interpretation is **community reallocation, not demonstrated vendor financial harm or benefit**. Foster’s three-case GitHub study finds that foundation-backed OpenTofu and Valkey brought in more organizationally varied contributors than the original vendor-controlled projects, whereas a single-company fork remained more sponsor-dependent. Its samples cover only selected projects and main repositories; commit counts omit documentation, governance, users and actual deployments. A later peer-reviewed netnography by Foster and Germonprez finds a recurring sequence across the three cases: vendors **framed other firms’ use as free-riding**, made unilateral licence changes, and encountered organized fork responses. These cases were *selected because forks occurred*; they cannot estimate the probability that any licence change causes a fork. (foster2411thenewdynamics pages 18-21, foster2411thenewdynamics pages 21-23, foster2026freeridingandrebellion pages 1-2, foster2026freeridingandrebellion pages 24-25)

**Financial conclusion.** A contemporary analyst review summarized by Foster found **no clear evidence that relicensing improved the vendors’ financial results**; that is not a controlled finding that the effect is zero. Licence changes often coincide with cloud competition, business maturation and other strategic decisions. Neither the observed forks nor subsequent revenues or acquisitions isolate what would have happened under continued OSS licensing. Similarly, survival of a **project or fork** should not be substituted for survival of its **sponsoring firm**. The strongest supported claims are about financing access, productivity under specified conditions, acquisition *association*, and changes in contributor governance—not a general valuation, revenue-growth, IPO or survival premium over proprietary software companies. (foster2411thenewdynamics pages 3-6, foster2411thenewdynamics pages 6-8, foster2411thenewdynamics pages 21-23, dmitrenko2026whymemorycomponents pages 2-4)
