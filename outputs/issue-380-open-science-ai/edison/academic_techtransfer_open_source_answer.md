Include evidence on technology-transfer office policies and the Bayh-Dole Act tension, university Open Source Program Offices (OSPOs), licensing income versus open release (e.g., AUTM data), DOE national laboratory software release and copyright rules, and measured outcomes such as adoption, spinouts and revenue.

# Open release and intellectual-property licensing in U.S. research institutions

**The central distinction is between controlling an invention and distributing a research product.** A university may patent a federally funded invention and license it exclusively, but it can also distribute software under a free, nonexclusive copyright license. A laboratory may likewise release code openly while pursuing patent licenses for other inventions. These choices are made project by project, subject to ownership, funding, contributor, security, and publication constraints; “open” is not synonymous with “uncommercialized.” (campbell2019universityinventionsreconsidered pages 23-26, young2024adefinitionof pages 1-7, gamblin2021pickinganopen pages 1-4, li2021understandingthemotivations pages 12-14)

## Universities: TTO review, Bayh–Dole, and OSPO support

University technology-transfer offices (TTOs) administer invention disclosure and review, institutional ownership claims, licensing terms, inventor revenue sharing, equity, and conflicts of interest. For a *federally funded subject invention*, Bayh–Dole permits an eligible university to **elect to retain title** if it fulfills disclosure, election, and patent-filing obligations; it does not automatically vest ownership of every university discovery in the institution. The government retains a paid-up license and specified safeguards. Nor does the statute require an exclusive commercial license for every output: its subject-invention obligations are distinct from the copyright permission needed to distribute source code. Where patent protection matters, review *before* a public code or design release preserves the opportunity to assess patent filing and sponsor obligations. (campbell2019universityinventionsreconsidered pages 23-26, fasi2022anoverviewon pages 6-7, gamblin2021pickinganopen pages 1-4)

This creates the practical Bayh–Dole tension. A patent-and-royalty workflow offers a route to finance expensive product development, particularly for physical inventions, but delay, exclusivity, or licensing friction can impede software reuse and scientific collaboration. Conversely, an open-source license can authorize broad copying and modification without collecting royalties on each download; it does not, by itself, dispose of every relevant patent, contributor, or third-party right. LLNL’s license analysis explicitly distinguishes copyright in code from patents on inventions and recommends licenses with express patent grants where broad adoption is the aim. (fasi2022anoverviewon pages 7-10, campbell2019universityinventionsreconsidered pages 23-26, gamblin2021pickinganopen pages 1-4, gamblin2021pickinganopen pages 4-6)

**Academic Open Source Program Offices (OSPOs) add expertise rather than replace the TTO.** A 2024 CURIOSS definition describes an OSPO as a campus convener, community steward, and open-source competency center. Its activities include license and release guidance, training, project discovery, external partnerships, and coordination with researchers and TTOs; its remit can extend to open hardware. The same guidance cautions that an OSPO generally should *not* unilaterally choose a project’s license or authorize its release. Its proposed role is to make the open route workable and sustainable alongside the TTO’s IP and contractual review—not to assert that every technology should be open. (young2024adefinitionof pages 1-7, young2024adefinitionof pages 7-13, young2024adefinitionof pages 16-21)

## DOE laboratories: contractor copyright is not federal-employee copyright

Most importantly, a **government-owned, contractor-operated** laboratory is not interchangeable with a **government-owned, government-operated** federal laboratory. Under 17 U.S.C. §105, code written by a federal employee in official duties is generally a U.S. Government work without domestic copyright. Contractor-produced code can be copyrighted: Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (LLNL) says its operating contractor, Lawrence Livermore National Security LLC, holds copyright in employees’ on-the-job code, while outside contributors or their organizations retain rights in their contributions. Open publication therefore needs both permission to release and a legally suitable license; a subsequent change of license can require contributor consent. (leech2021prospectiveeconomicimpacts pages 13-15, leech2021prospectiveeconomicimpacts pages 7-9, gamblin2021pickinganopen pages 1-4)

DOE does not simply instruct every lab to post every program. LLNL says its open-source release entails public release and can be precluded by **export control or lack of sponsor approval**. Its Computing Directorate recommends a permissive license with an explicit patent grant—**Apache-2.0 WITH LLVM-exception** as its default guidance—to facilitate commercial as well as academic uptake; its Figure 1 distinguishes permissive and copyleft licenses by their patent-license provisions. Separately, a DOE *assistance-award provision* says the government normally receives unlimited rights to distribute technical data generated under the agreement, while privately funded limited-rights data are treated differently. In specified delivery or open-source circumstances, the contracting officer **may negotiate** a term requiring *written approval* before a recipient asserts software copyright or changes government-license terms. This is a conditional award provision, **not evidence that the same approval clause applies to all laboratory operating contracts**. The retrieved documents do not establish a universal DOE CODE submission or approval workflow. (gamblin2021pickinganopen pages 1-4, gamblin2021pickinganopen pages 6-8, ipUnknownyeardepartmentofenergy pages 88-91, gamblin2021pickinganopen media d71759c9)

For **hardware**, the analogous choice concerns a potentially patentable device *and* the files needed to reproduce it. An open-hardware release requires accessible, modifiable design documentation and permission to make and sell, not merely a photograph or an academic paper. A conventional patent license can grant a manufacturer or startup protected commercial rights; an openly licensed design can instead support sales of manufactured devices, kits, customization, training, and services. These are alternative or combined business strategies, not proof that releasing a design produces no firms or revenue. A study assembled a database of **949 self-identified open-hardware firms** founded in 2001–2017 and interviewed founders from **66 firms**; that is evidence of entrepreneurship, **not a count of university spinouts or a revenue estimate**. (ipUnknownyeardepartmentofenergy pages 88-91, li2021understandingthemotivations pages 12-14, li2021understandingthemotivations pages 6-7, wenzel2023openhardwarefrom pages 5-9)

## What the measured outcomes do—and do not—show

* **Licensing income:** A review citing AUTM reports that U.S. university licensing revenue **exceeded $2.94 billion in 2018**, while noting that most TTOs did not earn enough to cover their operating costs. This is aggregate university licensing income, **not software-specific income, patent-only income, or foregone revenue attributable to open release**. A Stanford analysis of **4,512 inventions marketed in 1970–2020** reports **$581 million cumulative net income** and positive net income for only about **20%**. (fasi2022anoverviewon pages 7-10, liang2022systematicanalysisof pages 3-4)
* **Spinouts:** At Stanford, about **20%** of marketed inventions were licensed by startups founded by their inventors. All inventions with more than **$10 million net income** in that study were in the inventor-startup-licensed group, versus a **16%** such-licensing rate among inventions with less than **$10,000** net income. This is a strong *observational association*, not evidence that startup licensing—or rejecting open source—caused the income. A separate, single-office study recorded **15 startups in FY2015 and 23 in FY2020**, alongside an increase from **105 to 179 commercial licenses**; it did not isolate open-source startups or measure their revenue. (liang2022systematicanalysisof pages 4-6, loryman2024aquantitativesinglesite pages 18-22)
* **Open availability versus adoption:** LLNL reported in **2021** that **more than half** its software was open source and identified broader adoption, collaboration, contributions, and sustained funding as goals, without reporting comparative royalties or verified user counts. An institutional repository analysis identified **more than 216,000 affiliated repositories** across **32 CURIOSS institutions**; **23.4%** had a detectable license. That measures the presence of a license, **not actual use**; moreover, download-based projects may not know who their users are. (gamblin2021pickinganopen pages 1-4, romero2026towardsustainableanda pages 16-21, mccracken2026sustainingopensource pages 22-25)

**Bottom line:** Institutions manage a portfolio rather than a binary ideology: disclose and assess protectable inventions, satisfy federal and sponsor obligations, then choose patent licensing, genuinely open release, or compatible combinations according to the technology and intended users. TTO income and patented-invention startup counts are observable; open-source diffusion, maintenance, downstream services, and firms formed without an institutional IP license are less consistently captured. The evidence here establishes working policies and examples, but does **not** establish that either open release or exclusive licensing yields higher causal overall returns for comparable research projects. (campbell2019universityinventionsreconsidered pages 23-26, young2024adefinitionof pages 7-13, mccracken2026sustainingopensource pages 22-25, gamblin2021pickinganopen pages 1-4, liang2022systematicanalysisof pages 3-4, li2021understandingthemotivations pages 12-14)
