**Query.** Has anyone quantified, in dollar terms or as a share of value created, how much open scientific data and open-source software contributed to AI-for-science breakthroughs such as AlphaFold (trained on the Protein Data Bank) or machine-learning materials discovery (trained on the Materials Project, OQMD or AFLOW)?

---

Question: Has anyone quantified, in dollar terms or as a share of value created, how much open scientific data and open-source software contributed to AI-for-science breakthroughs such as AlphaFold (trained on the Protein Data Bank) or machine-learning materials discovery (trained on the Materials Project, OQMD or AFLOW)?

No. ## Short answer

**I found dollar estimates for the value of the Protein Data Bank (PDB) and a prospective estimate for materials-innovation infrastructure, but no study that credibly assigns a dollar amount or percentage of value created by *AlphaFold* or a machine-learning materials breakthrough specifically to open scientific data or open-source software.** The distinction is between valuing a shared input or measuring a breakthrough’s effects and estimating what *additional* value openness produced relative to a plausible restricted-access alternative. The principal PDB valuation expressly cautions that its modeled R&D returns cannot be attributed entirely to the PDB. (sullivan2017economicimpactsof pages 12-15, tsipouri2025theeconomicimpacta pages 8-10, scott2018economicanalysisof pages 90-94)

The closest quantitative evidence is summarized below; the estimates measure different things and **should not be added together**. Sullivan and colleagues’ PDB figures describe 2016 activity and were published in 2017, before AlphaFold2’s 2021 publication. (sullivan2017economicimpactsof pages 1-6, sullivan2017economicimpactsof pages 6-9, jumper2021highlyaccurateprotein pages 8-9)

### What the figures do—and do not—establish

* **PDB: repository-level dollars, not an AlphaFold share.** In a report commissioned by RCSB-PDB, Sullivan, Brennan-Tonetta and Marxen estimated **$5.5 billion per year in “use value”**—the imputed cost of researchers’ time *spent* using PDB data and services—and **$2.5 billion per year in efficiency gains**, modeled using an assumed 45% productivity improvement. They estimated **$760 million** in users’ willingness to pay using a value transferred from an EMBL-EBI study, and **$6.9 million per year** in PDB operating costs. Their **$1.1 billion annual** conservative R&D-return estimate and **$8.0 billion 30-year net present value** concern research *using* PDB data; they write that this value **“cannot be attributed 100% to RCSB-PDB.”** These are alternative indicators with differing assumptions, not a division of AlphaFold’s value among its contributors. The report did not survey PDB users directly. (sullivan2017economicimpactsof pages 1-6, sullivan2017economicimpactsof pages 6-9, sullivan2017economicimpactsof pages 9-12, sullivan2017economicimpactsof pages 12-15, sullivan2017economicimpactsof media b050686d)

* **Materials: a broad forecast, not a database or AI valuation.** A NIST-commissioned Materials Genome Initiative analysis by Scott and colleagues estimated **$123.229–$270.047 billion per year** in *potential* US benefits from improving the **entire Materials Innovation Infrastructure** (point estimate **$207.869 billion**, in 2013 dollars). Its interview-based scenario combines R&D efficiency with prospective improvements in products and markets and encompasses data, software, design methods, validation, scale-up and other capabilities. It does not allocate a dollar amount or percentage to the Materials Project, OQMD, AFLOW, openness, or machine learning; it is not a measurement of benefits already realized. (scott2018economicanalysisof pages 11-13, scott2018economicanalysisof pages 16-18, scott2018economicanalysisof pages 90-94)

* **Breakthroughs and research effects: quantities, not economic attribution.** AlphaFold’s original paper documents PDB structures used for training and templates, additional public sequence resources, and released source code and weights. Materials databases and associated open-source tools are established research infrastructure; the Materials Project, OQMD and AFLOW should not, however, be treated as *all* direct training inputs to any particular model without checking that model’s provenance. The GNoME paper reports **2.2 million** predicted structures below its reference convex hull and approximately **381,000 newly predicted stable materials**, not their commercial value. A 2026 economics study estimates effects of AlphaFold availability on research about previously unsolved proteins, but reports publication and downstream-research outcomes rather than dollars or a separate effect of open PDB data. (jumper2021highlyaccurateprotein pages 8-9, himanen2019data‐drivenmaterialsscience pages 4-5, merchant2023scalingdeeplearning pages 10-11, merchant2023scalingdeeplearning pages 2-3, merchant2023scalingdeeplearning pages 5-6, hill2026howartificialintelligence pages 68-70, hill2026howartificialintelligencea pages 32-35)

**Why the missing estimate matters.** PDB data, protein-sequence resources, model development and compute, open code, experimental validation, and later commercialization are complementary inputs. A repository’s operating cost, time-use value, or association with research does not reveal what would have happened had its data been inaccessible or licensed differently. A useful precedent for studying *openness itself* is Williams’s human-genome comparison: temporary restrictions on Celera’s sequence data were associated with approximately **20–30% less subsequent research and product development** than the relevant public-data counterfactual. That is a percentage effect on *genomics outcomes*, **not** a transferable AlphaFold or materials-discovery value share, and it was not monetized as one. (sullivan2017economicimpactsof pages 12-15, tsipouri2025theeconomicimpacta pages 8-10, williams2013intellectualpropertyrights pages 4-6, williams2013intellectualpropertyrights pages 1-4)

**Bottom line:** It is defensible to say that open scientific resources were important enabling inputs, and that PDB use and broad materials-infrastructure opportunities have been valued in dollars. It is **not** defensible, on the identified evidence, to claim that open data or open-source software accounts for a specified number of dollars—or a specified fraction—of the value created by AlphaFold or machine-learning materials discovery. (jumper2021highlyaccurateprotein pages 8-9, sullivan2017economicimpactsof pages 12-15, scott2018economicanalysisof pages 16-18)
