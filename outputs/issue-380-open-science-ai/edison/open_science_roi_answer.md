# The Economic and Scientific Return of Open Science, Open Data, and Open Research Infrastructure

## Overview

A growing body of empirical work attempts to quantify the value of open science, open data, and open research infrastructure. A recent scoping review of 27 studies published between 2000 and 2023 found generally positive—though still limited—evidence that open science accelerates research, reduces costs, fosters innovation, and supports economic growth, with the strongest quantitative evidence concentrated in the life sciences and in open/FAIR data initiatives (tsipouri2025theeconomicimpact pages 5-7, tsipouri2025theeconomicimpact pages 1-2). The review identified efficiency gains, enhanced innovation, and economic growth as the three primary channels through which benefits flow, while also noting persistent methodological challenges including attribution problems, self-reported data, and difficulty separating the effects of openness from other drivers (tsipouri2025theeconomicimpact pages 14-16, tsipouri2025theeconomicimpact pages 4-5). ## 1. The Protein Data Bank (PDB) and Its Role in Enabling AlphaFold

The Protein Data Bank, established in 1971 as the first open-access data resource in biology, now houses more than 230,000 experimentally determined macromolecular structures (berman2025proteindatabank pages 6-7). A 2017 economic impact assessment by Rutgers University estimated the PDB's annual use value at $5.5 billion, its annual efficiency impact at $2.5 billion, and its contingent value (user willingness to pay) at approximately $760 million—about 110 times its direct annual operating cost of approximately $6.9 million (sullivan2017economicimpactsof pages 12-15, sullivan2017economicimpactsof pages 1-6). The estimated replacement cost of the archive was $12 billion in 2017, rising to approximately $21 billion by 2023 (berman2025proteindatabank pages 6-7, sullivan2017economicimpactsof pages 1-6). Over 30 years, the modeled net present value of publicly funded research using PDB data was estimated at $8.0 billion (sullivan2017economicimpactsof pages 15-18). These methods combined web analytics (approximately 295,465 unique monthly users, 7 million annual sessions), contingent valuation adapted from the EMBL-EBI methodology, and assumed public R&D return rates of 20–60% (sullivan2017economicimpactsof pages 6-9).

The PDB's impact on drug discovery is substantial. Structure-guided methods contributed to more than 70% of FDA-approved small-molecule drugs between 2010 and 2018, with the archive containing structures covering approximately 88% of drug targets. For oncology specifically, PDB data supported 100% of the 34 anti-neoplastic agents approved from 2019–2023 (berman2025proteindatabank pages 8-10). HIV reverse-transcriptase and protease inhibitors developed with PDB structural data generated over $18 billion in global sales in 2015 alone (sullivan2017economicimpactsof pages 15-18). As of 2022, approximately 10,000 issued U.S. patents and about 90,000 worldwide patents or applications mentioned PDB data (berman2025proteindatabank pages 8-10).

The PDB's most widely appreciated downstream contribution is its role in enabling AlphaFold2, the AI protein-structure prediction system awarded the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. The open, validated, curated corpus of experimental 3D structures was essential training data for AlphaFold2 and related systems including RoseTTAFold and OpenFold (berman2025proteindatabank pages 10-11). A difference-in-differences analysis found that AlphaFold caused a 16–25% relative increase in research on proteins that previously lacked experimental structures, indicating that open structural data, amplified by AI, expanded the scientific frontier rather than displacing experimental work (hill2026howartificialintelligence pages 4-7, hill2026howartificialintelligence pages 29-32). The AlphaFold Protein Structure Database grew from 300,000 to over 214 million predicted structures and was used by more than 2 million people across 190 countries (chakraborty2026thetransformativeimpact pages 5-6). Studies show AlphaFold democratized research: lower-ranked institutions increased their share of high-impact protein publications by up to five percentage points within two years of AlphaFold's public release, with gains concentrated in protein domains and absent in non-protein fields (phalippou2026aiqualizingscience pages 1-5, divakaruni2025aiqualizingscience pages 7-10). A separate study found structural biologists experienced a 10% increase in citations to new projects and a 4% rise in publications in high-impact journals, though citation polarization between highly cited and less-cited researchers intensified (hill2026howartificialintelligence pages 4-7).

## 2. The Human Genome Project: Battelle Estimates and Critiques

The Human Genome Project (HGP), completed in 2003 after $3.8 billion in federal investment over 13 years, is the most frequently cited case of open-data-driven economic returns. The Battelle Technology Partnership Practice estimated that between 1988 and 2010, the HGP and associated genomics-sector activities generated $796 billion in U.S. economic output, more than $244 billion in personal income, and 3.8 million job-years of employment (carr2012datashowthat pages 1-1, tsipouri2025theeconomicimpact pages 8-10). The genomics-enabled industry generated more than $3.7 billion in federal taxes in 2010 alone—nearly matching the entire 13-year federal investment in a single year. Battelle calculated a headline return of $141 for every $1 of government spending (carr2012datashowthat pages 1-1).

These estimates warrant caution. The underlying input-output modeling captures direct, indirect, and induced economic activity associated with the genomics sector, but attribution is a central problem: much of the measured economic activity would have occurred through parallel private-sector and international investments in genomics regardless of the HGP itself (tsipouri2025theeconomicimpact pages 8-10). Critics argue that the economic model assumptions may overestimate returns and that the figures represent economic activity associated with genomics rather than a causal return solely attributable to the federal project (tsipouri2025theeconomicimpact pages 8-10, tsipouri2025theeconomicimpact pages 14-16). The scoping review of open science impact noted that such estimates involve "substantial attribution and assumption sensitivity" and that the small, distinctive set of cases prevents broad generalization (tsipouri2025theeconomicimpact pages 10-11).

## 3. The 2008 Landsat Open Data Policy

The U.S. Geological Survey's decision to make all Landsat satellite imagery freely available online at the end of 2008 provides one of the clearest natural experiments in open data economics. Before the policy, no more than 3,000 Landsat images were sold in any single month during the 1972–2008 period. In 2009, the first full year of free access, nearly one million images were downloaded, and by 2017 annual downloads had increased approximately twenty-fold (zhu2019benefitsofthe pages 2-3). Scientific publications using Landsat data, which had been below 400 per year from 1986 to 1999 and had begun rising after price reductions in 1999, surged to nearly 1,600 per year by 2017—a four-fold increase (zhu2019benefitsofthe pages 2-3).

Economically, a contingent-valuation user survey estimated $1.8 billion in benefits from 2.38 million downloaded images. A broader assessment estimated total annual benefits at $2.19 billion in 2011 ($1.79 billion for U.S. users), rising to $3.45 billion in 2017 ($2.06 billion for U.S. users) (straub2019economicvaluationof pages 1-7, straub2019economicvaluationof pages 17-19). For established users, average annual scene acquisitions more than doubled from 30 to 68, while average annual spending fell from $4,213 to $932, yielding one-time cost savings of $52.8 million (miller2013usersusesand pages 26-28). The free data policy also catalyzed other Earth observation programs—most notably the EU Copernicus Programme—to adopt comparable open-access policies, and enabled cloud-based processing platforms such as Google Earth Engine (zhu2019benefitsofthe pages 3-4). Studies warn that introducing even small fees would cause more than half of users to leave, with cascading effects on downstream products and environmental monitoring (straub2019economicvaluationof pages 17-19).

## 4. EMBL-EBI Impact Studies

EMBL-EBI, one of the world's largest providers of freely accessible biological data, has been the subject of two major economic impact assessments conducted using similar survey-based and valuation methodologies. The 2015–2016 study, based on 4,509 survey responses and an estimated 198,000 direct active users accessing data 88 million times per year, estimated an annual use value of £2.3 billion (the value of users' time spent using the resources), against annual operational expenditure of approximately £47 million (beagrie2016thevalueand pages 29-31, beagrie2016thevalueand pages 5-7). Users' stated willingness to pay averaged £1,628 per person annually, extrapolating to £322 million—approximately 6.8 times operating cost (beagrie2016thevalueand pages 26-29). The study estimated worldwide efficiency gains of at least £1 billion per year, with a possible upper bound of £5 billion, representing more than 20 times the direct operating cost (beagrie2016thevalueand pages 29-31). Wider research impacts were modeled at £920 million annually (£6.9 billion net present value over 30 years), of which £335 million per year was attributed to research that could not have been conducted without EMBL-EBI resources, as 45% of respondents reported they could neither recreate nor obtain the data elsewhere (beagrie2016thevalueand pages 35-38, beagrie2016thevalueand pages 31-35). A Gene Ontology case study estimated a 3:1 benefit-to-cost ratio (beagrie2016thevalueand pages 35-38).

The 2020 updated study, based on nearly 5,000 survey responses, found substantially higher figures: a use value of £5.5 billion per year, research impacts of £1.3 billion annually that could not have been realized without EMBL-EBI resources, and estimated efficiency savings ranging from £2.6 billion to £11 billion per year (cantelli2022theeuropeanbioinformatics pages 1-2, tsipouri2025theeconomicimpact pages 7-8). By 2020, EMBL-EBI resources received over 81 million web and API requests per day and had reached 390 petabytes of raw data storage (cantelli2022theeuropeanbioinformatics pages 1-2).

The authors of both studies stress that these are indicative estimates, not audited financial returns. EMBL-EBI is a contributing rather than solely attributable factor, and survey-based approaches are subject to self-selection bias, hypothetical bias, and difficulty in constructing counterfactuals (beagrie2016thevalueand pages 29-31, tsipouri2025theeconomicimpact pages 14-16).

## 5. The Materials Project and Open Materials Databases

In the physical sciences, the Materials Project (launched in 2011 by MIT and Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory) exemplifies the growing role of open computational databases. It provides freely accessible, open-source infrastructure for data-driven materials science, with computed structural, electronic, thermodynamic, and mechanical properties for 178,627 materials across 51,298 chemical systems and 228 space groups (horton2025accelerateddatadrivenmaterials pages 4-6). The platform has surpassed 600,000 registered users, with millions of data records downloaded daily. Since 2015, more than 145,000 DOIs have been minted for inorganic-materials data (horton2025accelerateddatadrivenmaterials pages 1-4, horton2025accelerateddatadrivenmaterials pages 9-11). The companion open-source toolkit pymatgen and the Matminer data-mining library enable computational screening that has yielded experimentally confirmed discoveries in catalysis, hydrogen storage, and Li-ion batteries (jain2013commentarythematerials pages 1-3, jain2013commentarythematerials pages 3-5).

The Materials Project ecosystem is complemented by other open databases including OQMD (approximately 400,000 DFT calculations), AFLOW, NOMAD, JARVIS, and Materials Cloud (horton2025accelerateddatadrivenmaterials pages 1-4, hill2016materialssciencewith pages 6-7). Together, these resources reduce duplication of costly quantum-mechanical calculations, enable broad comparative analyses, and serve as training data for universal machine-learning interatomic potentials such as M3GNet and CHGNet, which provide atomistic simulations at scales impractical for first-principles methods (horton2025accelerateddatadrivenmaterials pages 14-16). No formal monetized economic impact study has been published for the Materials Project, but its documented reach and reuse indicate substantial scientific returns in accelerating the conventional 10–20 year materials-development cycle (himanen2019data‐drivenmaterialsscience pages 5-6).

## 6. Effects of Open Access on Citations, Innovation, and Patents

The best-identified causal study of open access and downstream innovation exploits the 2008 NIH Public Access Mandate, which required NIH-funded research to be deposited in PubMed Central within 12 months. This mandate produced a 50-percentage-point increase in the free availability of funded articles. Using a novel measure of in-text patent citations and difference-in-differences estimation, Bryan and Ozcan (2021) found that after 2008, patents cited NIH-funded research 12–27% more often. Their primary estimates indicate a 25.3% increase in total patent citations and a 21.3% increase in the probability of at least one patent citation. Non-funded research, funded research in journals unaffected by the mandate, and academic citations showed no comparable change, supporting a causal open-access interpretation (bryan2021theimpactof pages 28-32, bryan2021theimpactof pages 1-5). The effects extend to citations by patents that themselves receive subsequent citations, suggesting that the additional knowledge flows have technological influence (bryan2021theimpactof pages 32-35).

Notably, the study found no measurable increase in conventional academic citations from the mandate, which the authors attribute to researchers' existing institutional journal access (bryan2021theimpactof pages 1-5). This contrasts with broader meta-analytic literature, which has estimated an approximately 8% open-access citation advantage for scholarly articles, though with substantial heterogeneity (bryan2021theimpactof pages 1-5). The effects may be especially important for small biomedical firms lacking institutional subscriptions (bryan2021theimpactof pages 35-39).

A large-scale descriptive study of 22 million patent families indexed in Google Patents (2010–2020) found that approximately one-third referenced non-patent literature, and the proportion of cited articles that were openly accessible grew over time to nearly half by 2020. Openly accessible articles in hybrid journals and green repositories were disproportionately cited in patents, though country and subject variation was substantial (jahn2022quantifyingtheinfluence pages 1-3). A study of 117,590 papers (2014–2023) found that openly accessible papers—particularly hybrid open access and green repository papers—were significantly more likely to be cited in patents, approximately seven times more than those mentioned only in blogs (dortagonzalez2025linkingscienceand pages 9-11).

## 7. Open Scientific Data as Training Data for AI Models

The convergence of open scientific data and machine learning represents perhaps the most transformative recent development in open science. AlphaFold2 is the paradigmatic example: trained on the openly available Protein Data Bank's corpus of approximately 230,000 experimentally determined structures plus openly available genomic sequence databases, AlphaFold2 achieved near-experimental accuracy in protein-structure prediction (hey2020machinelearningand pages 1-2, berman2025proteindatabank pages 10-11). The resulting AlphaFold Database, hosted at EMBL-EBI and provided in open access, expanded from 300,000 to over 214 million predicted structures—a 500-fold increase that raised human structural coverage from approximately 48% to 76% of residues (chakraborty2026thetransformativeimpact pages 5-6).

In materials science, the Materials Project's extensive electronic-structure calculations and relaxation trajectories have enabled universal machine-learning interatomic potentials (M3GNet, CHGNet) that provide accurate predictions across broad structural and compositional spaces (horton2025accelerateddatadrivenmaterials pages 14-16). Open materials databases also serve as standardized benchmarks (MatBench) for fair comparison of ML methods, while text-mining of open materials science literature has been used to train unsupervised word embeddings that capture latent scientific knowledge and can predict novel materials discoveries (wang2023scientificdiscoveryin pages 11-11). Open imaging datasets such as ImageNet (over 14 million labeled images) have been foundational for the deep-learning revolution that now permeates scientific data analysis (hey2020machinelearningand pages 1-2).

More broadly, self-supervised learning on vast quantities of openly available scientific data—including protein sequences, chemical structures, genomic data, and spectroscopic measurements—enables AI models to learn useful representations without manual labeling, supporting generative design of drugs and proteins (wang2023scientificdiscoveryin pages 1-2). The importance of open data for AI is evidenced by initiatives such as the SciML benchmark suite for scientific machine learning, which advocates standardized open datasets to evaluate algorithms across domains (hey2020machinelearningand pages 18-20).

## 8. Critiques and Methodological Limitations

Several cross-cutting critiques apply to economic impact estimates in this domain. First, most valuation studies rely on survey-based methods (contingent valuation, willingness-to-pay, efficiency self-reports) that are subject to hypothetical bias, self-selection, and social-desirability effects (tsipouri2025theeconomicimpact pages 14-16). Second, attribution remains a fundamental challenge: open research infrastructure is typically one contributing factor among many, and isolating its causal effect from parallel investments, technological trends, and complementary research is rarely possible (tsipouri2025theeconomicimpact pages 8-10, beagrie2016thevalueand pages 29-31). Third, many headline return-on-investment figures (such as the HGP's $141:$1) use input-output multiplier models that capture associated economic activity rather than causally attributable returns, and may not be robust to alternative assumptions (tsipouri2025theeconomicimpact pages 8-10). Fourth, evidence is geographically concentrated in the United States and United Kingdom and focused on high-resource settings, leaving gaps for other regions and sectors (tsipouri2025theeconomicimpact pages 16-17). Fifth, potential negative effects—such as reduced incentives to generate primary data when mandatory sharing is required, or strategic delays in publication—have been theorized but lack empirical support (tsipouri2025theeconomicimpact pages 2-4). Finally, the scoping review notes that the evidence base is "patchy and diverse," with only 27 empirical studies identified across all open-science types, and no formal quality assessment of included studies was conducted (tsipouri2025theeconomicimpact pages 4-5, tsipouri2025theeconomicimpact pages 5-7).

Despite these limitations, the consistency of findings across diverse domains—life sciences, earth observation, materials science, and the innovation literature—supports the conclusion that open science and open data generate substantial economic and scientific returns, often many multiples of their operating costs. The emergence of AI systems trained on open scientific data represents a qualitatively new return channel whose full economic impact is only beginning to be measured.
