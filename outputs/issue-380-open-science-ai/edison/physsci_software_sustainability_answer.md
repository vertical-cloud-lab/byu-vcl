Question: How have open-source scientific software packages in the physical sciences (e.g., LAMMPS, Quantum ESPRESSO, OpenFOAM, GROMACS, RDKit, pymatgen, ASE) been funded, sustained and commercialized compared with proprietary packages (e.g., VASP, Gaussian, ANSYS, COMSOL, Schrodinger)? Include evidence on usage and citations, funding sources, business models (support, consulting, dual licensing), and sustainability or failure cases.

# Funding and commercialization of scientific software

**The principal difference is who pays for continuing development, not whether the software is used commercially.** The open-source packages examined here have been sustained through combinations of public research funding, national laboratories, university staff, industrial contributions, foundations, and volunteer maintenance. Some also support paid services or proprietary products built *around* a freely available engine. Proprietary packages instead charge for access or use, making license income a more direct means of financing product engineering and support. Neither model guarantees long-term maintenance or reproducibility. (kohlmeyer2025lammpsacase pages 1-2, giannozzi2019softwareforquantum pages 4-6, abraham2015gromacshighperformance pages 7-8, talirz2108trendsinatomistic pages 2-4)

## Open-source packages: documented mechanisms and reach

| Package | Funding, maintenance, or commercialization evidence | Evidence of reach and qualification |
|---|---|---|
| **LAMMPS** | Originated in a Sandia–Lawrence Livermore collaboration with Cray, Bristol Myers Squibb, and DuPont; its principal subsequent funding has come from the US Department of Energy. Its GPL-licensed development combines a core team with extensive outside contributions. The maintainers explicitly identify succession, testing, refactoring, and the low academic reward for maintenance as continuing concerns. | A 2021 inventory counted **51 source-code committers in 2020** and about **1.04 million lines of code** in the surveyed release; these measure development activity and scale, *not* users or downloads. | **Quantum ESPRESSO** | Began with institutional support from Italy’s DEMOCRITOS simulation centre. The nonprofit Quantum ESPRESSO Foundation subsequently supported development and raised funds through commercial agreements; EU **MaX** funding supported work toward new HPC architectures. A vendor has distributed the open engine alongside its **proprietary GUI**—commercialization of a complementary product, not evidence that the engine itself became proprietary. | A project leader reported, **as of 2019**, more than **4,000 downloads per release**, **1,000 mailing-list subscribers**, **1,600 school attendees**, and **15,000 citations since 2009** to papers documenting the suite. These are reported supports for the work and team, not a disclosed annual project budget. | Its developers described **thousands of publications using GROMACS each year** in 2015. That measures breadth of integration, not the number of installations. (larsen2017theatomicsimulation pages 1-5, george2026pymatgen15years pages 5-7) |

These cases show several distinct ways to capture value without selling exclusive rights to the core: **support and training** around OpenFOAM; **commercial agreements and a proprietary companion GUI** around Quantum ESPRESSO; **industry-supported development and permitted commercial deployment** of GROMACS; and publicly funded infrastructure that industry can use and extend. Consulting or custom integration is economically possible in these ecosystems, but the retrieved evidence does not justify assigning a consulting-revenue figure to any named project. Likewise, **dual licensing** means offering the *same code* under alternative licenses; neither paid support for GPL code nor a proprietary GUI beside a free engine proves dual licensing. A free or discounted *academic* license paired with a paid industrial license is another, different arrangement unless an open-source license is also offered. (giannozzi2019softwareforquantum pages 4-6, abraham2015gromacshighperformance pages 1-3, condicjurkic2026thegrowingrole pages 2-4, talirz2108trendsinatomistic pages 6-8, calderonsanchez2015modelingtheimpact pages 1-5)

## Comparison with proprietary packages

**VASP and Gaussian are commercially licensed but scientifically prominent.** In a small 2020 survey of **16 Philippine materials-modeling specialists**, 67% of respondents using DFT reported Quantum ESPRESSO and 47% VASP; the author associated the difference with VASP’s license cost. The survey also reported Gaussian use by 13% of DFT respondents and GROMACS use by 32% of all respondents. These are *local, small-sample* observations, not global market shares. A broader analysis of atomistic-software references found that paid commercial codes led free codes in scholarly citations over the 2010s, with **Gaussian and VASP together responsible for more than half of citations to its 23 commercial codes**. Commercial success therefore cannot be inferred to be lower scientific use. (arevalo2020quantumandatomic pages 6-8, talirz2108trendsinatomistic pages 6-8)

**ANSYS and COMSOL illustrate the engineering-suite alternative** to OpenFOAM: payment for an integrated product and its vendor support, rather than obtaining a GPL solver and choosing whether to buy expertise around it. An OpenFOAM application paper describes commercial CFD subscriptions as costing on the order of **thousands of dollars per subscription year**, but does **not** establish a price for a particular ANSYS or COMSOL configuration. Proprietary Schrödinger tools likewise illustrate licensed molecular-modeling workflows, while Schrödinger’s support for the open-source **PyMOL** illustrates that a commercial company can also steward open software. The material examined does not substantiate comparable company-level revenue, profit, customer, or R&D-spending figures for **VASP, Gaussian, ANSYS, COMSOL, or Schrödinger**; precise financial comparisons would be unwarranted. (calderonsanchez2015modelingtheimpact pages 1-5, condicjurkic2026thegrowingrole pages 7-9, giannozzi2019softwareforquantuma pages 1-4)

The difference in access also affects independent verification. Historical research documents disputes over **Gaussian’s source-available but restrictive licensing**: permission to read source is not the same as permission to modify, rebuild, or redistribute it. Conversely, open source does not automatically supply a supported, validated workflow. The citation survey found that **at least about 90% of atomistic-engine references went to *source-available* software**, a category broader than OSI-approved open source, and that citations to the **24 open-source engines** in its collection grew by **more than 300% since 2010**. Its authors stress that citations approximate published references, not executions; industrial users may publish less, and software citation conventions vary. The survey’s commercial-versus-free trajectories are shown in its Figure 7. (hocquet2017“onlytheinitiates pages 18-21, talirz2108trendsinatomistic pages 5-6, talirz2108trendsinatomistic pages 6-8, talirz2021trendsinatomistic media d1df6b19)

## Sustainability and failure modes

The strongest open projects have **institutions that pay people to maintain infrastructure**, mechanisms for reviewing outside contributions, and downstream users who depend on continued operation. LAMMPS’s DOE-backed core and explicit succession planning, the Quantum ESPRESSO Foundation’s fundraising role, and the Materials Project’s commitment to pymatgen are concrete examples. OpenFOAM demonstrates the trade-off in another direction: GPL access lets development continue in multiple lines, but forks and variant-specific integration can fragment maintenance. Proprietary vendors can pay professional product and support teams from licenses, yet access, version availability, and permitted modification remain dependent on license terms and vendor decisions. (kohlmeyer2025lammpsacase pages 13-14, george2026pymatgen15years pages 15-17, giannozzi2019softwareforquantum pages 4-6, bna2020petsc4foamalibrary pages 2-6, virgilio20203dcfdsimulationof pages 21-26, giannozzi2019softwareforquantuma pages 1-4)

**Failure should not be equated with an open license, nor survival with high citations.** A study of **18,247 scientific repositories** found longer lifespans associated with infrastructure status, downstream dependencies, publication mentions, and government participation; its matched comparison found scientific open-source projects lived longer than non-scientific counterparts. These are associations across repositories, not a prediction that any named package will survive. Conversely, the historical pattern of graduate-student code left undocumented and unmaintained, insufficient credit for maintenance, succession risks, hardware-porting demands, and fragmentation illustrate credible ways that otherwise valuable software can decay. The named flagship packages above are primarily *survival cases*; the reviewed evidence does not establish that any one of them has failed. (thakur2025scientificopensourcesoftware pages 1-3, giannozzi2019softwareforquantum pages 1-4, kohlmeyer2025lammpsacase pages 2-3, kohlmeyer2025lammpsacase pages 13-14, bna2020petsc4foamalibrary pages 2-6)

**Bottom line:** free distribution shifts the financing problem from collecting license fees to securing durable maintenance labor through grants, employers, foundations, commercial partners, or paid adjacent services. Proprietary licensing can make that labor a funded product obligation, but it does not remove questions about access, scientific verification, or continuity. Usage and citation indicators support the importance of **both** models; they cannot, on their own, establish which is financially self-sustaining. (thakur2025scientificopensourcesoftware pages 1-3, talirz2108trendsinatomistic pages 2-4, talirz2108trendsinatomistic pages 6-8, giannozzi2019softwareforquantum pages 4-6)
