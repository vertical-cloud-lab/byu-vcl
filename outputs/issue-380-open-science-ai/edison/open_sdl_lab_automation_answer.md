# Open-source self-driving laboratories in chemistry and materials science

**State of the field, November 2026.** Open tools now cover much of the laboratory stack: inexpensive demonstrations of autonomous experiment selection, configurable liquid handlers and motion systems, cross-vendor control software, and automated characterization. There are credible research demonstrations and some industrial use, but few open systems have evidence of reliable, independently reproduced, end-to-end *autonomous discovery* across sites. Affordability depends on whether the price describes parts, an operational instrument, an integrated laboratory, or access to a service. (lo2024reviewoflowcost pages 3-4, mishra2023opensourceframeworksfor pages 5-6, pelkie2025democratizingselfdrivinglabs pages 14-16)

## What counts as self-driving?

An automated instrument executes a prescribed protocol. A self-driving laboratory (SDL) also uses measurements to **choose subsequent experiments** and execute an iterative design–make–measure–learn loop. Even an instrument that adjusts dosing in response to a sensor need not choose new scientific hypotheses: the distinction matters when evaluating reported demonstrations. Lo and colleagues call inexpensive physical surrogates of more capable SDLs *frugal twins*. Their cost–capability comparison runs from dye mixing and simple titration through Jubilee and OT-2 workflows to integrated commercial synthesis and analysis; lower cost can mean narrower experiments, less precise handling, or less powerful characterization, not equivalent scientific output. (lo2024reviewoflowcost pages 3-4, lo2024reviewoflowcost pages 5-6, lo2024reviewoflowcost media 50b41b4d, golas2026apurposebuiltopen pages 2-3)

Baird and Sparks’s *What is a minimal working example for a self-driving laboratory?* supplies the conceptual starting point; their accessible, subsequent **CLSLab:Light** build protocol makes it tangible. A Pico W controls RGB LEDs, a light sensor reports the result, and Python/Bayesian optimization selects the next settings. Required parts were listed at **$68.61 in March 2022**, excluding shipping, with setup described as under an hour. This is a genuine, small closed-loop optimization exercise—not a chemical reactor or proof that an inexpensive kit can characterize a new material. The original *Matter* article was not available in full for this review; the implementation details here come from the authors’ published protocol. (baird2023buildinga“hello pages 1-4)

### Where the open stack is working

* **Accessible motion and liquid handling.** Jubilee/Science-Jubilee provides an open, reconfigurable tool-changing gantry rather than a fixed-purpose chemistry instrument. The cited CdSe workflow examined **625 conditions in triplicate in less than two months**; a separate Science-Jubilee study automated silica sol–gel synthesis and connected it to small-angle X-ray scattering, reporting reproducible composition-dependent structural outcomes. These studies establish useful research workflows, but a roughly $2,000 motion platform does not include all the pipetting, sensing, X-ray access, labor, and integration necessary to reproduce them. (lo2024reviewoflowcost pages 10-11, pelkie2025acceleratedsol–gelsynthesis pages 1-2)
* **Pragmatic hybrids.** The **commercial OT-2** offers accessible, programmable liquid handling rather than wholly open-source hardware. Its open interface makes it useful as the core of a *custom-built* SDL, provided the group adds measurement, experiment-selection software, and orchestration. An approximately **$160 Kamome** open scanner connected OT-2 preparation to optical measurements: reported timing tests were up to fourfold faster than manual positioning, and a 96-well integrated scan took less than one-third of manual time. Spectrometer and OT-2 costs are additional, and sample heating and transfer to the scanner still required human handling. (lo2023reviewoflowcost pages 2-5, yin2025kamomea3dprintable pages 5-9, yin2025kamomea3dprintable pages 9-13)
* **Portable control software.** PyLabRobot supplies a shared Python representation of liquid-handling commands, labware and deck layouts, with demonstrations involving **Hamilton STAR, Tecan EVO and OT-2** instruments. It addresses proprietary-interface lock-in without making robot hardware free or making protocols chemically equivalent across machines. Other relevant interoperability and workflow approaches include **SiLA 2, Bluesky, ChemOS, open electronic laboratory notebooks and standards-based metadata**; instrument adapters, liquid-specific calibration, provenance and maintenance remain local work. (wierenga2023pylabrobotanopensource pages 1-4, mishra2023opensourceframeworksfor pages 5-6, canty2025scienceaccelerationand pages 5-6)
* **Open measurement hardware.** OpenFlexure is a motorized, configurable, 3D-printed microscope rather than a complete SDL. Its authors reported **more than 100 built in Tanzania and Kenya**, substantially better evidence of distributed fabrication than a prototype demonstrated only by its inventors. Different optics and camera choices change both cost and performance; the accessible primary text does not justify a single universal parts price. A microscope can be integrated into an SDL, but open-source imaging alone does not close the discovery loop. (collins2020roboticmicroscopyfor pages 1-3)

More ambitious open hardware is emerging above the maker-kit price range. A **2026 bioRxiv preprint** describes an eight-channel, PyLabRobot-compatible liquid handler with parts costing **under $40,000**. It maintained roughly 200 cultures through measurement-driven dilution; a second build by two laboratory members took approximately **one to one-and-a-half weeks**. The authors reported lower application-specific dispensing error than manual pipetting, but this was within-team replication, the full build package was still planned for release, and the turbidostat maintained predefined setpoints rather than autonomously designing a scientific campaign. (golas2026apurposebuiltopen pages 2-3, golas2026apurposebuiltopen pages 7-8, golas2026apurposebuiltopen pages 9-10)

## Costs, adoption and reproducibility

The 2024 *Chemical Reviews* comparison puts **open hardware at roughly $100–$2,000**, general-purpose commercial hardware at **roughly $10,000**, and specialized commercial installations at **roughly $1 million**. These are *broad classes*, not matched bids with the same assay, precision, containment, service and analysis. A roughly **$7,500 starting OT-2 price** appears in the frugal-twin literature; published purchase prices vary by date and configuration. Commercial characterization may dominate the budget: NMR or HPLC–MS can cost hundreds of thousands of dollars, and neither an inexpensive gantry nor a dye-mixing demo substitutes for them. (tom2024selfdrivinglaboratoriesfor pages 5-6, lo2023reviewoflowcost pages 1-2, lo2024reviewoflowcost pages 5-6)

A particularly important omitted cost is engineering. In a 2025 workshop report, contributors described bills of materials **one to two orders of magnitude below** comparable commercial equipment, but also **hundreds to thousands of development hours**. Among **58 workshop respondents**, more than 70% said they were willing to publish hardware and software; that measures interest in sharing, **not** the fraction of laboratories using SDLs. Documentation, safety clearance, assembly, commissioning, upkeep and consumables can reverse an apparent purchase-price advantage for a one-off installation. Savings become more convincing when a well-documented design is repeatedly built and supported. (pelkie2025democratizingselfdrivinglabs pages 14-16)

There is stronger evidence for **particular workflows** than for widespread cross-laboratory reproducibility. At Roche, two OT-2s were integrated into its AutoLab system for FACS-control preparation and metabolite-identification work. In the reported MetID comparison, agreement with manual execution had an **intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.998**, automated replicate variation averaged **less than one-third** of manual variation, and hands-on time decreased by **30 minutes per assay**. That validates a specific industrial workflow, not autonomous experiment selection or all OT-2 assays. Jubilee’s triplicates, the silica study, Kamome’s timing and positioning tests, and OpenFlexure’s distributed manufacture likewise answer different questions: within-workflow repeatability, task performance, or buildability. The literature still lacks comparable multi-site statistics for open systems’ drift, recalibration, downtime and failure rates. (thieme2024deepintegrationof pages 5-6, thieme2024deepintegrationof pages 1-2, lo2024reviewoflowcost pages 10-11, pelkie2025acceleratedsol–gelsynthesis pages 1-2, yin2025kamomea3dprintable pages 5-9, mishra2023opensourceframeworksfor pages 5-6)

**Reproducibility therefore needs more than a repository.** A useful replication package specifies a versioned bill of materials and substitutions; mechanical designs, wiring and firmware; executable protocols, labware and liquid-class definitions; calibration and acceptance tests; instrument/software versions; raw measurements, failures and metadata; and recovery and safety procedures. The need is empirical, not merely administrative: an open frugal liquid handler required different pump calibration for different liquids, while the OT-2-to-Kamome work documented positional drift. Open interfaces facilitate provenance, but they do not remove these physical sources of variation. (lo2024reviewoflowcost pages 5-6, yin2025kamomea3dprintable pages 1-5, pelkie2025democratizingselfdrivinglabs pages 14-16, mishra2023opensourceframeworksfor pages 5-6)

## Commercial and cloud-laboratory landscape

There are distinct businesses here. **Instrument and integration vendors** sell supported equipment and its installation. **Cloud laboratories** sell remote access to instruments, workflow execution and records, shifting capital expenditure into operating expenditure. **AI-for-science firms** may instead sell prediction software, research services, licensed data or discoveries—or invest in their own autonomous physical laboratories. Calling every one of these businesses an SDL obscures what the customer actually buys. (arnold2022cloudlabswhere pages 2-2, arias2024scientificdiscoveryat pages 8-8, shen2026aiformaterials pages 10-12, shen2026aiformaterials pages 12-14)

For cloud labs, *Nature* reported Emerald Cloud Lab’s entry offering in **2022** at **$24,000 per month for three concurrent experiments**; Strateos used customized pricing. Emerald emphasized broad remote instrumentation, whereas Strateos emphasized defined drug-discovery and synthetic-biology workflows and discussed on-site installations. Shared facilities can spare users equipment purchases, allow remote scheduling and maintain standardized experimental histories. They are not automatically cheaper than an OT-2 or a local shared instrument: price depends on utilization, experiment complexity, shipping and service needs. Researchers have reported difficulty implementing custom methods, such as nonstandard gel buffers, as well as limits on troubleshooting and useful parallelization. Centralized experimental data also creates confidentiality and security obligations. (arnold2022cloudlabswhere pages 2-2, arias2024scientificdiscoveryat pages 8-8, arias2024scientificdiscoveryat pages 6-7)

The AI-for-science startup market is broader than cloud access. In a **2026, non-peer-reviewed industrial-landscape analysis**, **Periodic Labs and Lila Sciences** are grouped with autonomous/full-stack experimental ventures; **CuspAI** is described as an AI materials foundry and partner/data-network model; **Citrine** as a models/platforms business; and **Chemify** as translating chemical synthesis plans into robotic execution. Kebotix is discussed as an earlier chemistry/materials experiment-planning and automation venture. The available evidence does **not** substantiate a specific present-day operating or financial claim for Atinary. A provider of an AI model, an operator of a physical factory and an operator of a rentable cloud laboratory should not be treated as interchangeable. (shen2026aiformaterials pages 12-14, shen2026aiformaterials pages 14-17, shen2026aiformaterials pages 10-12)

That same preprint tallied **approximately $1.98–$2.29 billion of disclosed AI-for-materials funding from September 2025 to July 2026**, with **Periodic, CuspAI and Lila accounting for approximately 86–88%**. The range incorporates **reported but company-unconfirmed rounds** and is an author-compiled funding estimate—not revenue, measured SDL adoption, or proof of commercially qualified materials. Its principal warning is that generating candidates and rapid laboratory measurements is easier than establishing synthesis robustness, device performance, manufacturing scale-up and customer qualification. (shen2026aiformaterials pages 1-4, shen2026aiformaterials pages 14-17, shen2026aiformaterials pages 17-19)

## Should SDL hardware and software be open-sourced?

**The case for openness is strongest at shared infrastructure layers.** Releasing design files, bills of materials, drivers, instrument interfaces, protocol schemas, calibration routines and representative datasets can enable independent checking, local manufacture and repair, cross-vendor programming and education. It can spread the fixed cost of engineering across laboratories, let chemists change hardware to answer questions a vendor did not anticipate, and reduce dependence on a single supplier. OpenFlexure’s regional manufacture, PyLabRobot’s cross-vendor interface, and the transferable Jubilee platform illustrate different parts of this argument. Commercial manufacturing, training and service can coexist with open designs. (collins2020roboticmicroscopyfor pages 1-3, wierenga2023pylabrobotanopensource pages 1-4, pelkie2025democratizingselfdrivinglabs pages 14-16, wenzel2210globaltechnologyaccess pages 17-20)

**The case against indiscriminate release is also substantive.** A CAD file without commissioning and troubleshooting instructions is not a reproducible instrument. Open systems transfer maintenance, validation, cybersecurity and liability work to users, while commercial equipment may be preferable for high pressure, radiation and specialized validated measurement. Fully disclosed autonomous-control software or hazardous experimental recipes can increase misuse risk; poorly constrained agents can also initiate unsafe experiments unintentionally. Public datasets may expose confidential research or create security problems. Finally, a firm may rationally protect valuable process windows, failures and customer data even while publishing common interfaces—the source of industrial differentiation need not be a closed pipetting command. (pelkie2025democratizingselfdrivinglabs pages 14-16, lo2023reviewoflowcost pages 24-26, canty2025scienceaccelerationand pages 5-6, arias2024scientificdiscoveryat pages 8-8, shen2026aiformaterials pages 10-12)

A defensible **layered approach** is to open reusable, well-tested hardware and interoperability code, publish calibration methods and non-sensitive benchmark datasets, and retain controls appropriate to particular hazardous workflows and confidential data. Validation should include independent builds and cross-site experiments, explicit operating envelopes, interlocks, access control and human review where risks warrant it. Hybrid systems—open orchestration around supported commercial pipettors or analytical instruments—may deliver more scientific value and safety than insisting that every component be either wholly open or wholly proprietary. **The unresolved challenge is not demonstrating that inexpensive robots can move or optimize; it is making complete experimental loops dependable, auditable and transferable without concealing their true labor and safety costs.** (pelkie2025democratizingselfdrivinglabs pages 14-16, canty2025scienceaccelerationand pages 5-6, mishra2023opensourceframeworksfor pages 5-6)
