## The Estimated Economic Value of Open-Source Software: A Synthesis of Quantitative Studies

### 1. Overview

The economic value of open-source software (OSS) has been estimated by a growing body of research using fundamentally different valuation approaches, yielding headline figures that range from billions of dollars in production costs to trillions of dollars in counterfactual use value. These discrepancies are not primarily disagreements about the same quantity but rather reflect the measurement of distinct economic objects: the cost to produce OSS (supply side), the cost firms would bear to replace it (demand side), the investment flow that should be recorded in national accounts, the marginal contribution to GDP, and the downstream effects on entrepreneurship and venture capital. The following synthesis covers the principal studies, their methods, quantitative estimates, US-specific figures, and acknowledged limitations.

### 2. Supply-Side vs. Demand-Side Valuation: Hoffmann, Nagle & Zhou (2024)

The most widely cited recent study is the SSRN working paper by Hoffmann, Nagle, and Zhou (2024), "The Value of Open Source Software." Using a census of OSS repositories on GitHub, the authors estimate the **supply-side value** of globally used OSS at approximately **$4.15 billion**, representing the labor cost of producing the software (osborne2024atoolkitfor pages 2-4, brown2024measuringsoftwareinnovation pages 1-2). The **demand-side value**—what firms would need to spend to recreate OSS internally if it ceased to be freely available—is estimated at approximately **$8.8 trillion** (osborne2024atoolkitfor pages 2-4, luhrsen2025organizingpublicsupport pages 6-9). This implies a demand-to-supply ratio of roughly 2,120:1 (das2026projectlifecycles pages 9-10). The enormous gap highlights what Osborne et al. (2024) call a "risk of underproduction" stemming from the misalignment between the supply of OSS development labor and the demand for OSS (osborne2024atoolkitfor pages 2-4). Other citing sources describe the cost of recreating all OSS as ranging "from billions of dollars in the US to trillions globally" (gortmaker2024opensourcesoftware pages 1-4).

**Method and limitations:** The supply-side figure is based on COCOMO-style cost estimation of developer labor from GitHub activity, while the demand-side figure uses a counterfactual replacement framework. The two numbers measure fundamentally different concepts—one is an observed input cost, the other a hypothetical expenditure under autarky—and should not be directly compared as market values. The estimates are from a working paper and are sensitive to assumptions about developer costs, package usage, dependency networks, and counterfactual scenarios.

### 3. OSS Investment in US National Accounts: Robbins, Korkmaz et al. (BEA/NSF)

A separate line of work by Robbins, Korkmaz, Guci, and Santiago Calderón—developed jointly with the US Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) and the National Science Foundation (NSF)—attempts to measure OSS as intangible investment consistent with US national accounting methods for software.

**Methodology:** The researchers collected approximately 5.2 million public GitHub repositories with OSI-approved licenses created between 2008 and 2019. Annual additions to lines of code serve as the quantity measure. These are converted to estimated person-months using the **COCOMO II** software cost model: Effort = 2.4(KLOC)^1.05, with nominal development time = 2.5(Effort)^0.38. Development cost equals nominal development time multiplied by monthly programmer wages (from BLS Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics) and a **2.02 factor** to cover intermediate inputs and capital services (robbins2021afirstlook pages 5-9).

**Key US-specific estimates:** US OSS investment rose from **$733 million in 2009** to **$38.4 billion in 2019** in current dollars, implying nearly 50% average annual real growth (robbins2021afirstlook pages 18-23, robbins2021afirstlook pages 9-14). Real investment (in constant 2012 dollars) reached **$39.1 billion** in 2019, and the accumulated real OSS capital stock exceeded **$118 billion** using a perpetual-inventory method with 30% annual depreciation (robbins2021afirstlook pages 9-14). For context, total US software investment in 2019 was approximately **$492 billion** ($428 billion private, $62.6 billion public), making OSS roughly 8% of the total and approximately 50% of own-account software investment (santiago2022measuringthecost pages 1-4, robbins2021afirstlook pages 9-14). A related BEA Working Paper (Santiago Calderón, 2022) produced a slightly lower estimate of **$36.2 billion** for 2019 US OSS investment using a revised framework (santiago2022measuringthecost pages 1-4, santiago2022measuringthecost pages 24-27). An earlier preliminary estimate found that resource costs for packages in R, Python, Julia, and JavaScript alone exceeded **$3 billion** in 2017 dollars, and federally developed software on code.gov exceeded **$1 billion** (santiago2022measuringthecost pages 10-13).

(robbins2021afirstlook media f214276c)

**Limitations:** GitHub coverage is incomplete; lines of code imperfectly measure software output and quality; attribution of contributions by country, sector, and organization is approximate; and the resulting estimates may overlap with own-account software and R&D already captured in GDP. The model's parameters (COCOMO coefficients, depreciation rate, wage assumptions) materially affect results (santiago2022measuringthecost pages 24-27, robbins2021afirstlook pages 9-14).

### 4. Digital Dark Matter: Greenstein & Nagle (2013/2014)

Greenstein and Nagle introduced the concept of "digital dark matter" to describe economically valuable OSS that is omitted from conventional GDP and investment statistics because it has no market price. Using the case of the **Apache HTTP Server**, they developed a **replacement-cost methodology**: a random scan of approximately 1% of US IPv4 addresses identified about 44,211 Apache servers in the sample, extrapolated to roughly **4.28 million** outward-facing Apache servers nationally (greenstein2013digitaldarkmatter pages 16-20).

**Key estimates:** Applying Microsoft IIS/Windows Server license prices as the shadow value of Apache, the replacement cost ranged from **$514 million to $12.8 billion**, with a central estimate of approximately **$2 billion** (greenstein2013digitaldarkmatter pages 20-24). This equates to **1.3%–8.7%** of the US stock of prepackaged software in private fixed investment (greenstein2013digitaldarkmatter pages 1-5, greenstein2013digitaldarkmatter pages 5-9). A productivity-based calculation estimated Apache's incremental income contribution at **$1.0–$1.8 billion** in 2012 alone, with accumulated economic activity of **$2.6–$4.5 billion** through 2012 (greenstein2013digitaldarkmatter pages 28-32). Against an estimated **$400 million** in NSF and DARPA Internet R&D spending (1985–1995), the implied rate of return was approximately **10.5%–14%** (greenstein2013digitaldarkmatter pages 28-32, greenstein2013digitaldarkmatter pages 24-28).

**Limitations:** The study covers only one OSS product and mainly outward-facing servers, excluding intranets and servers behind shared IP addresses. The authors emphasize that the figures are lower bounds and that broader digital dark matter—including Linux, PHP, Firefox, TCP/IP software, and Wikipedia—could be much larger (greenstein2013digitaldarkmatter pages 20-24, greenstein2013digitaldarkmatter pages 9-13).

### 5. European Union Estimates: Blind et al. (2021)

Blind et al. (2021), commissioned by the European Commission's DG CONNECT, estimated that EU companies invested approximately **€1 billion** in OSS in 2018 (measured through over 30 million commits by EU-based contributors), which generated an economic impact on the EU economy estimated at **€65–95 billion** (blindUnknownyeartheimpactof pages 1-6, blindUnknownyeartheimpactof pages 6-8). On a cumulative basis through 2018, the benefit-to-cost ratio was slightly above **10:1** when only personnel costs were counted, and above **4:1** when hardware and other investment costs were included (blindUnknownyeartheimpactof pages 6-8). The study projected that a **10% increase** in OSS contributions could generate **0.4%–0.6% additional GDP** annually and create more than **600 additional technology startups** in the EU each year (blindUnknownyeartheimpactof pages 1-6). Startups and micro-enterprises were found to contribute disproportionately to OSS relative to their size, with several small firms deriving more than half their revenue from OSS or related services (blindUnknownyeartheimpactof pages 6-8).

In a peer-reviewed follow-up, Blind and Schubert (2023) analyzed a panel of **32 countries** (25 EU members plus the US, Japan, Korea, Canada, China, Norway, and Switzerland) over 2000–2018, matching GitHub commits to OECD macroeconomic data. They found that average-country GDP would be approximately **2.2% lower in the long run** if no country contributed to OSS (blind2023estimatingthegdp pages 1-3, blind2023estimatingthegdp pages 19-21). The global stock of OSS increases GDP across countries, but a country's own contributions can paradoxically reduce its measured GDP—especially for smaller countries—because knowledge spills abroad without compensation. The net effect is nonetheless positive, and the losses from outward spillovers are smaller for countries with higher R&D and patenting intensity (blind2023estimatingthegdp pages 1-3, blind2023estimatingthegdp pages 21-23).

### 6. Effects on Entrepreneurship and Venture Capital: Wright, Nagle & Greenstein (2020)

Wright, Nagle, and Greenstein (2020) examined whether country-level OSS development activity is associated with entrepreneurial formation using data from **207 countries** over 2000–2016, combining GitHub commits with venture data from Crunchbase and Preqin. Using OLS and instrumental-variable analysis, they found that a **1% increase** in a country's GitHub commits was associated with:

- **0.2%–0.4%** more IT ventures founded the following year (wright2020opensourcesoftware pages 7-11)
- **0.4%–0.8%** increase in the value of new venture-financing deals (wright2020opensourcesoftware pages 7-11, wright2020opensourcesoftware pages 44-47)
- **0.2%–0.5%** increase in the number of financing deals (wright2020opensourcesoftware pages 7-11)
- **0.1%–0.3%** increase in technology acquisitions (wright2020opensourcesoftware pages 7-11)

As an illustrative prediction, a 1% increase in 2016 GitHub commits was associated with approximately **133 additional ventures in the United States**, 24 in India, 11 in Brazil, 5 in China, 2 in Indonesia, and 1 in Mexico (wright2020opensourcesoftware pages 31-34). The relationship was complementary to existing country endowments—OSS participation had stronger effects in countries with greater human capital and income, and the resulting ventures tended to be more globally oriented and of higher quality as proxied by financing outcomes (wright2020opensourcesoftware pages 31-34, wright2020opensourcesoftware pages 1-7).

### 7. Structural Policy Estimates: Gortmaker (2026)

Gortmaker (2026, initially circulated 2024) built a structural equilibrium model of the global web development industry to quantify OSS policy tradeoffs. Key findings include that a Chinese domestic OSS subsidy of $100 per hour raised Chinese OSS investment by approximately **20%** and reduced global web-development costs by roughly **$11 per dollar of subsidy** ($5 within China) (gortmaker2024opensourcesoftware pages 4-6). A comparable US subsidy produced approximately **three times** the global cost reduction because firms predominantly use US-built OSS (gortmaker2024opensourcesoftware pages 4-6, gortmaker2024opensourcesoftware pages 1-4). A globally coordinated subsidy generated cost reductions **six times** greater than unilateral domestic-only subsidies (gortmaker2024opensourcesoftware pages 4-6, gortmaker2024opensourcesoftware pages 6-9). By contrast, Chinese restrictions on cross-border OSS collaboration reduced Chinese investment by 3% and raised global costs by approximately **$7 per dollar of disincentive** (gortmaker2024opensourcesoftware pages 1-4, gortmaker2024opensourcesoftware pages 42-44).

### 8. Disagreements and Limitations

Several important caveats apply across this literature:

**Different concepts, not competing estimates.** The $4.15 billion supply-side figure, the $8.8 trillion demand-side figure, the $38 billion US national-accounts investment, the €65–95 billion EU GDP impact, and the 2.2% GDP counterfactual all measure different economic objects. They should not be compared directly as if they were estimates of the same quantity (osborne2024atoolkitfor pages 2-4, osborne2024atoolkitfor pages 4-5).

**GitHub as a data source.** Most studies rely on GitHub, which, while the world's largest OSS platform, provides incomplete coverage of all OSS activity. Lines of code are an imperfect proxy for effort, quality, and economic value. Geographic and organizational attribution of contributors is approximate (santiago2022measuringthecost pages 24-27, santiago2022measuringthecost pages 13-17).

**COCOMO model sensitivity.** Supply-side estimates depend heavily on parameters of the COCOMO II cost model, wage assumptions, the blow-up factor for non-labor costs, and depreciation rates. The 2.02 resource-cost multiplier, 30% depreciation, and organic-class complexity assumptions all materially affect results (robbins2021afirstlook pages 5-9, santiago2022measuringthecost pages 10-13).

**Double-counting risk.** Some OSS investment may already be captured in BEA accounts as own-account or custom software. The overlap between measured software investment and OSS investment is difficult to quantify (robbins2021afirstlook pages 9-14).

**Causality.** Country-level studies face challenges of reverse causality (economically dynamic countries may produce more OSS) and omitted variables. Even with instrumental-variable designs, causal identification is imperfect (wright2020opensourcesoftware pages 19-23, wright2020opensourcesoftware pages 11-16).

**Working-paper status.** Several key estimates (Hoffmann et al. 2024; Wright et al. 2020/2024; Gortmaker 2024/2026) circulate as working papers rather than peer-reviewed publications, and their estimates may be revised. The Nagle (2019) firm-level productivity study, published in *Management Science*, was unobtainable for direct review but is referenced across the literature as finding that OSS use raises firm productivity conditional on complementary IT capabilities (blind2023estimatingthegdp pages 19-21).

### 9. Conclusion

The cumulative evidence suggests that OSS is an economically significant form of intangible capital, with US production-cost investment on the order of **$36–38 billion annually** as of 2019, a US capital stock exceeding **$118 billion**, and a global demand-side (replacement) value potentially reaching **$8.8 trillion**. At the macroeconomic level, GDP would be an estimated **2.2% lower** without OSS contributions. OSS activity is positively associated with startup formation and venture capital flows, with the United States experiencing the largest absolute effects. The EU Commission's study estimated a benefit-to-cost ratio exceeding 4:1 from €1 billion in annual OSS investment. These estimates, while subject to substantial methodological uncertainty, consistently indicate that OSS generates economic value far exceeding its direct production costs, that it is substantially undermeasured in conventional national accounts, and that policy interventions (subsidies, procurement, cross-border collaboration) can generate high returns.
