Question: How have industry AI labs' publication, code-release and model-weight release practices changed between 2018 and 2026? Include evidence on declining or increasing publication by companies such as OpenAI, Google DeepMind, Meta and Anthropic, staged or gated releases, the Foundation Model Transparency Index, and effects on academic research and reproducibility.

# Industry AI research disclosure, 2018–2026

**The main change is selective disclosure, not a uniform end to publishing.** In the late 2010s, influential industry papers could arrive with reusable code and pretrained models, as Google’s BERT did. By the mid-2020s, leading labs still published research, but increasingly separated a paper or safety report from access to the system it described: a flagship might be available only through an API, while a different or smaller model was released with downloadable weights. Meta’s Llama family is an important counterexample to a blanket shift toward closed weights, although downloadable weights do not necessarily include training code or data. (devlin2019bertpretrainingof pages 1-2, team2024gemmaopenmodels pages 1-2, nobel2026untanglingaiopenness pages 82-83, openai2023gpt4technicalreport pages 1-3)

## Publication output: what has—and has not—declined

A study of generative-AI research at selected institutions found **some broad decline in publicly available publications from 2023 to 2024, particularly at Google DeepMind**. Across its *January 2020–March 2025* window, fractional-authorship-adjusted totals were 969 papers for Google DeepMind, 64 for Meta, 64 for OpenAI, and 62 for Anthropic. These are totals for a defined generative-AI corpus—not each lab’s complete output, annual counts, or evidence of a continuous decline since 2018. The authors supplemented OpenAlex with OpenAI and Anthropic website publications and warn that Meta may be undercounted. Their annual plot identifies DeepMind’s especially large 2023–2024 fall; it does **not** establish that all four companies followed the same trajectory. (strauss2025realworldgapsin pages 11-13, strauss2025realworldgapsin pages 31-34, strauss2025realworldgapsin pages 8-11, strauss2025realworldgapsin media cd386d58)

Other measures capture different phenomena. In Stanford’s *2025 AI Index*, industry-only papers among the **100 most-cited AI publications** numbered 17 in 2021, 19 in 2022, and 7 in 2023; that is a decline in this highly cited subset, **not** a count of all industry papers. Conversely, industry originated **55 notable models, or 90.2% of the notable-model set, in 2024**. Academia produced **84.9% of AI publications globally in 2023**. Taken together, these findings show a divergence between who publishes most papers and who develops most notable models; they do not justify saying that industry research or its total publication output fell throughout 2018–2026. (maslej2025artificialintelligenceindex pages 39-41, maslej2025artificialintelligenceindex pages 47-48, maslej2025artificialintelligenceindex pages 35-39)

## How release practices diverged

| Lab | Documented progression | What the progression means |
|---|---|---|
| **OpenAI** | GPT-2’s full 1.5-billion-parameter model was initially withheld in 2019 and released after smaller models—a staged release. The 2020 GPT-3 paper described a 175-billion-parameter model and its training and evaluation, but the frontier model was not released as downloadable weights. In 2023, the GPT-4 *technical report* expressly omitted model size, architecture details, hardware, training compute, dataset construction, and fuller training methods, citing competition and safety. By the 2025 Transparency Index, its assessed flagship **o3** was an API release. OpenAI also released the separate, smaller **gpt-oss** open-weight models in 2025: its policy was not uniformly closed. (stoltz2026selectinglanguagemodels pages 5-6, radfordUnknownyearlanguagemodelsare pages 1-2, brown2020languagemodelsare pages 9-11, openai2023gpt4technicalreport pages 1-3, wan2026the2025foundation pages 14-16, nobel2026untanglingaiopenness pages 82-83, trisovic2026openscienceclosed pages 2-3) | Publication, API availability, and weight release became distinct decisions. GPT-4 demonstrates that even a substantial public report can withhold information needed to reconstruct training. (openai2023gpt4technicalreport pages 1-3) |
| **Google / Google DeepMind** | Google’s BERT paper, first circulated in 2018, explicitly linked **code and pretrained models**. DeepMind’s 2021 AlphaFold paper made **source code, trained weights, and an inference script** available. Google DeepMind’s 2024 Gemma report released **2B and 7B pretrained and instruction-tuned checkpoints plus open-source inference/serving code**, while its later assessed flagship **Gemini 2.5** was API-accessed. (devlin2019bertpretrainingof pages 1-2, jumper2021highlyaccurateprotein pages 8-9, team2024gemmaopenmodels pages 1-2, wan2026the2025foundation pages 14-16) | This is a mixed portfolio, not abandonment of open scientific artifacts: accessible models and code coexist with a closed-weight flagship and the measured 2023–2024 publication decline. (strauss2025realworldgapsin pages 11-13, wan2026the2025foundation pages 14-16) |
| **Meta** | The first LLaMA models were described in a 2023 paper but distributed initially to approved researchers under a non-commercial research arrangement. **Llama 2** followed in 2023 with pretrained and chat-tuned releases under conditions permitting commercial use. In 2024 Meta publicly released pretrained and post-trained **Llama 3.1 405B** weights under its Community License, while saying multimodal extensions were *not yet* broadly released. The 2025 Index classified **Llama 4** as open-weight. (touvron2023llamaopenand pages 1-2, singh2025tracingtheevolution pages 28-29, touvron2023llama2open pages 1-3, grattafiori2024thellama3 pages 1-3, wan2026the2025foundation pages 14-16) | Access widened substantially relative to the initial gate, but release remained selective and licensed. A comparative openness assessment lists Llama 4’s weights as public under restrictions while its training data and training code remained private. (nobel2026untanglingaiopenness pages 82-83) |
| **Anthropic** | Anthropic publicly described its **Constitutional AI** training method in a 2022 paper. Yet the **Claude 4** flagship assessed in the 2025 Index was an **API** release, not a public-weight release. (bai2212constitutionalaiharmlessness pages 1-3, wan2026the2025foundation pages 14-16) | Publishing alignment methods and describing a deployed model do not grant researchers its trained parameters or full training pipeline. (bai2212constitutionalaiharmlessness pages 1-3, wan2026the2025foundation pages 14-16) |

Here, *code release* means software is shared; *open weights* means trained parameters can be downloaded and examined or run subject to their license; *full training reproducibility* also requires such inputs as training data, preprocessing, training code and compute information. An API permits experiments on model behavior, but not inspection or local rerunning of its parameters. Neither a paper nor an open-weight download, on its own, establishes that the original training run can be reproduced. The distinction matters particularly for Llama and Gemma: a comparative assessment found publicly available inference code and restricted-license weights for Llama 4 and Gemma 3, but private training data and training code for both. (stoltz2026selectinglanguagemodels pages 6-8, nobel2026untanglingaiopenness pages 82-83)

## What the Foundation Model Transparency Index measures

The **Foundation Model Transparency Index (FMTI)** measures disclosed information about companies and their selected flagship models across upstream development, model properties and downstream deployment; it is **not a publication-count index or a simple open-weights ranking**. Its reported mean score was **37/100 in 2023, 58/100 in 2024, and 40.69/100 in 2025**. For the four companies, the directly reported **2024 → 2025** scores were **Anthropic 51 → 46; Google 47 → 41; Meta 60 → 31; and OpenAI 49 → 35**. Thus Meta and OpenAI fell sharply on this measure, while Anthropic and Google fell more modestly. Meta and OpenAI, first and second among the six firms assessed in every edition in 2023, ranked last and second-to-last among those six in 2025. (wan2026the2025foundation pages 3-4, wan2026the2025foundation pages 33-35)

The 2025 Index scored **13 developers on 100 indicators**. Only **seven** submitted their own reports; the researchers assembled public-information reports for six others, including Anthropic. Indicators and the company sample changed, some scoring standards became stricter, and the 2024 and 2025 reporting arrangements differed. Consequently, the change in the *overall mean* is not a like-for-like estimate of how much every firm reduced disclosure; individual changes are informative but also need those methodological qualifications. (wan2026the2025foundation pages 1-3, wan2026the2025foundation pages 14-16, wan2026the2025foundation pages 33-35)

The Index pinpoints why a model announcement can be scientifically thin: its 2025 **training-data-properties** indicators averaged just **15%**, with eight companies scoring zero on that subdomain. Training compute and post-deployment usage and impact were also poorly disclosed. Developers often reported capability and risk evaluations without enough methods for external reproduction, and the Index found inadequate disclosure of overlap between training material and evaluation sets. Its stricter 2025 reproducibility criterion required shared evaluation **code and prompts**. These are information deficits even when a lab publishes a system card or offers model access. (wan2026the2025foundation pages 1-3, wan2026the2025foundation pages 14-16, wan2026the2025foundation pages 20-21)

## Consequences for academic research and reproducibility

Restricted weights and undisclosed data limit the questions an outside laboratory can answer. Researchers can test a permitted API’s outputs, but cannot directly inspect its parameters, independently fine-tune or rerun it locally, reconstruct its data selection, or fully check benchmark contamination and training claims. Access and compute also affect participation: an observational study of **104,226 scientific papers from 2018–2025** associated industry cloud credits with greater reliance on closed/API models, whereas public funding was associated with greater open-weight use. The study does **not** establish that credits *caused* the difference; it instead documents an access-and-control divide that can persist even when academic authors publish their own code. (trisovic2026openscienceclosed pages 9-10, trisovic2026openscienceclosed pages 2-3, wan2026the2025foundation pages 20-21)

There is an important countertrend. An analysis of **56,800 papers** at five AI conferences found that the share documenting **both open code and open datasets** rose from **11% in 2014 to 64% in 2024**; open-code reporting alone rose from 13% to 69%. Its estimated rise in reproducibility from 28% to 64% was **inferred from documentation and earlier replication rates, not established by rerunning every paper**. The study also found no systematic academia-versus-industry documentation advantage across its comparisons. Therefore, the best-supported conclusion is **improving openness for published conference experiments alongside persistent—and in some flagship cases worsening—opacity of the most consequential proprietary systems**. Neither trend cancels the other. (coakley2026theshifttoward pages 6-8, coakley2026theshifttoward pages 10-12, openai2023gpt4technicalreport pages 1-3, wan2026the2025foundation pages 33-35)

**Bottom line:** By 2026, industry disclosure had become more *layered and lab-specific*: papers, model cards, safety evaluations, API access, inference code, downloadable weights and training resources could be released separately, at different times, to different users. Evidence supports a notable 2023–2024 fall in Google DeepMind’s measured public generative-AI papers and declining FMTI scores for several flagship developers, but it also documents major open-weight and code releases and improving code/data sharing across AI conference research. A claim that *all* of OpenAI, Google DeepMind, Meta and Anthropic simply stopped publishing or opening models between 2018 and 2026 would overstate the evidence. (strauss2025realworldgapsin pages 11-13, team2024gemmaopenmodels pages 1-2, wan2026the2025foundation pages 33-35, grattafiori2024thellama3 pages 1-3, coakley2026theshifttoward pages 6-8)
