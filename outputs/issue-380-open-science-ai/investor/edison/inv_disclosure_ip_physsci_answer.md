## Open Disclosure and Science-Based Startups: Patents, Venture Capital, and the Appropriability Trade-Off

### 1. The Causal Value of Patents for Startup Financing and Growth

The strongest evidence on how patents affect startup outcomes comes from Farre-Mensa, Hegde, and Ljungqvist (2020), who exploit the quasi-random assignment of patent applications to USPTO examiners with different grant propensities—a "patent lottery"—to identify causal effects. Startups that win their first patent experience, on average, 55% higher employment growth and 80% higher sales growth over five years, corresponding to approximately 16 additional employees and $10.6 million in cumulative additional sales (farre‐mensa2020whatisa pages 1-5, farre‐mensa2020whatisa pages 5-8). The proposed mechanism is improved access to external finance: a first patent raises the probability of obtaining venture capital within three years by 3.0 percentage points—a 46.9% increase over the 6.4% unconditional baseline—increases the likelihood of securing a collateralized loan by 76%, and more than doubles the odds of raising public funding through an IPO (farre‐mensa2020whatisa pages 25-29, farre‐mensa2020whatisa pages 5-8, farre‐mensa2020whatisa pages 29-32). These effects are especially pronounced for inexperienced founders, where patent approval increases the three-year VC probability by nearly 33 percentage points (farre‐mensa2020whatisa pages 32-35). Patent winners also pursue more follow-on innovation, with subsequent patent applications increasing by 56.5% and citation-weighted quality rising by 33% per patent (farre‐mensa2020whatisa pages 22-25).

Hsu and Ziedonis (2013) complement this evidence by studying 370 venture-backed semiconductor startups and demonstrating a dual role for patents: they serve both as exclusion rights providing product-market protection and as quality signals that reduce information asymmetry between founders and investors (hsu2013resourcesasdual pages 2-4, hsu2013resourcesasdual pages 4-5). The signaling function is especially important in early financing rounds and when founders lack alternative credibility mechanisms such as prior entrepreneurial experience or affiliation with a prominent VC (severinUnknownyeartemporaldynamicsof pages 142-143, hsu2013resourcesasdual pages 5-6). Patents are costly to obtain (~$35,000 per typical U.S. patent), and this cost helps ensure that the signal is credible (hsu2013resourcesasdual pages 4-5). The implication for science-based startups in the physical sciences is clear: patents are not merely legal protections but are critical financial instruments that unlock capital.

### 2. Patent-Paper Pairs and the Disclosure–Appropriability Trade-Off

Academic researchers routinely face a tension between publishing scientific results promptly (to establish priority, build reputation, and advance knowledge) and protecting commercially valuable discoveries through patents. Gans, Murray, and Stern (2013) formalize this as a negotiation between scientists' preference for publication and firms' interest in appropriability, showing that "patent-paper pairs"—where the same research generates both a patent filing and an academic publication—represent a jointly determined disclosure strategy rather than conflicting outputs (gans2013contractingoverthe pages 7-9, gans2013contractingoverthe pages 1-4). Their model demonstrates that under certain conditions, patents and publications are complements: stronger patent protection can actually increase both the likelihood of patenting and the extent of academic publication, because secure exclusionary rights reduce the competitive cost of disclosure (gans2013contractingoverthe pages 25-28, gans2013contractingoverthe pages 35-38).

However, coordination is essential. Firms commonly conduct internal IP review before publication to identify patentable subject matter, and patent applications are generally filed before papers are submitted (ahmed2022scientificlabormarket pages 7-10). Publication can establish prior art and trigger a one-year USPTO filing deadline, forcing firms to patent before they might otherwise be ready (ahmed2022scientificlabormarket pages 7-10). Ching, Gans, and Stern (2018) further show that academic entrepreneurs face an endogenous choice between "control" (investing in formal IP) and "execution" (commercializing rapidly). Faculty-led ventures were nearly twice as likely as student-led ventures to obtain formal IP, while student-led ventures commercialized faster—revealing a trade-off between patenting and speed (ching2018controlversusexecution pages 2-3, ching2018controlversusexecution pages 3-4).

For physical-science startups, this trade-off has specific implications: materials compositions, device architectures, and manufacturing processes may be fully disclosed in a single paper, eliminating the ability to protect them through trade secrecy afterward. The patent-paper pair strategy allows researchers to publish while maintaining appropriability, but only if the patent application is filed before or simultaneously with the publication.

### 3. Novelty Bars, Grace Periods, and the Geography of Risk

The legal treatment of pre-filing disclosure differs dramatically between the United States and Europe, creating a critical risk for any open-by-default policy.

Under the America Invents Act, 35 U.S.C. § 102(b)(1) provides a one-year grace period: an inventor's own public disclosure does not destroy U.S. patentability if a patent application is filed within twelve months (ozyhar2022whenspeedmatters pages 19-21, ozyhar2022whenspeedmatters pages 5-7). However, this protection is not absolute—a third party's independent disclosure of a variation may still constitute prior art, and the grace period does not protect against priority challenges from competitors who file first (ozyhar2022whenspeedmatters pages 19-21, struve2012endingunnecessarynovelty pages 11-13).

Under the European Patent Convention, the principle of absolute novelty applies: anything made publicly available before the filing date—by written or oral description, use, or any other means—generally enters the state of the art and destroys novelty (struve2012endingunnecessarynovelty pages 3-5, takenaka2002rethinkingtheunited pages 4-6). The only exceptions are narrow six-month protections under EPC Article 55 for "evident abuse" and display at specified international exhibitions (ozyhar2022whenspeedmatters pages 5-7, struve2012endingunnecessarynovelty pages 15-17). Consequently, the same paper, preprint, conference presentation, or open repository that remains safely within the U.S. grace period may permanently destroy European patent rights (ozyhar2022whenspeedmatters pages 3-5, struve2012endingunnecessarynovelty pages 5-7).

For a university spin-out in materials science or engineering seeking global markets, an open-by-default policy that publishes results before filing creates a severe risk: it may permanently foreclose patent protection in Europe and other absolute-novelty jurisdictions, limiting the company to U.S.-only patent coverage and reducing its licensing and commercial reach.

### 4. University Technology Transfer Under Bayh-Dole

The Bayh-Dole Act of 1980 fundamentally reshaped university-industry relationships by allowing universities to retain ownership of inventions arising from federally funded research and to grant exclusive licenses for commercialization (bagley2005academicdiscourseand pages 13-16, miteu2024patentingthebayh–dole pages 1-2). Technology transfer offices (TTOs) emerged to manage this process, handling invention disclosures, patent prosecution, license negotiation, and spin-out formation (tahvanainen2008valuecreationin pages 4-7, bagley2005academicdiscourseand pages 16-19). The Act requires universities to actively promote commercialization and preserve the government's march-in rights for cases where public health or safety needs are unmet (miteu2024patentingthebayh–dole pages 2-3).

A central tension exists between TTOs' mandate to protect IP and academic norms of rapid dissemination. TTOs routinely encourage researchers to delay publication until patent applications are filed, because public disclosure can jeopardize patent rights (bagley2005academicdiscourseand pages 1-3). Bagley (2005) proposes an extended grace period and early publication of patent applications as mechanisms to reconcile these competing demands (bagley2005academicdiscourseand pages 1-3). Miteu (2024) recommends open-innovation models in which selected patents are available for noncommercial and academic research alongside continued knowledge exchange (miteu2024patentingthebayh–dole pages 3-4).

Critically, Lee (2020) demonstrates that patents alone are insufficient for effective technology transfer. Faculty cooperation was required for successful commercialization in 71% of licensed inventions, and approximately 40% of university licenses involved faculty participation (lee2020tacitknowledgeand pages 23-25). University inventions are typically embryonic, requiring ongoing tacit knowledge transfer—experiential know-how, heuristics, and "tricks of the trade"—that cannot be fully captured in a patent document (lee2020tacitknowledgeand pages 13-17, lee2020tacitknowledgeand pages 21-23, lee2020tacitknowledgeand pages 19-21). This is especially important for complex physical-science technologies where production processes, scale-up parameters, and material-specific expertise remain with the inventor (lee2020tacitknowledgeand pages 17-19). The implication is that the founding scientist's continued involvement is itself a form of appropriability that complements formal IP.

### 5. When Openness Increases Investment and Adoption

Despite the risks, there is growing evidence that selective openness can attract venture capital and accelerate market adoption. Conti, Peukert, and Roche (2021) studied 160,065 U.S. startups linked to GitHub accounts and found that engagement with open-source communities was associated with at least a 36% increase in the likelihood of receiving funding (conti2021beefingitup pages 5-8, conti2021beefingitup pages 1-5). The effect was strongest for startups developing novel technologies, where open-source engagement was associated with an eight percentage-point increase in funding probability relative to other engaged startups (conti2021beefingitup pages 17-20). Importantly, investors responded to substantive technical activity—not cosmetic README changes—suggesting that open disclosure functions as a credible signal of development capability (conti2021beefingitup pages 26-28, conti2021beefingitup pages 23-26). However, startups in highly competitive markets were 14 percentage points less likely to have been financed after engaging with open-source communities, implying that appropriability concerns limit the benefits of openness when imitation risk is high (conti2021beefingitup pages 17-20).

In the hardware domain, Li et al. (2021) interviewed 65 open-source hardware founders and found that firms such as SparkFun and Ultimaker built viable businesses around openly shared designs (li2021understandingthemotivations pages 1-2). Value capture came through complementary mechanisms: services, consulting, manufacturing quality, community-driven innovation, standard-setting, and brand differentiation rather than design exclusivity (li2021understandingthemotivations pages 15-18, li2021understandingthemotivations pages 4-6, li2021understandingthemotivations pages 12-14). Open designs reduced development costs by recruiting communities for prototyping and testing, lowered customer-support costs, and helped firms educate nascent markets and establish technical standards (li2021understandingthemotivations pages 14-15). However, many firms that started open eventually patented or closed their designs as they grew, particularly when targeting mainstream customers, responding to investors, protecting quality, or finding community management incompatible with business operations (li2021understandingthemotivations pages 15-18).

### 6. Risks of an Open-by-Default Policy for Investors in University Spin-Outs

An open-by-default publication policy creates several specific risks for investors in science-based university spin-outs:

**Loss of patent rights.** The most immediate risk is that premature disclosure destroys novelty—irreversibly in absolute-novelty jurisdictions and within a constrained window even in the United States (struve2012endingunnecessarynovelty pages 3-5, ozyhar2022whenspeedmatters pages 19-21). For a physical-science startup whose core value lies in a novel material composition, device architecture, or manufacturing process, the loss of patent protection can eliminate the exclusionary rights that underpin both competitive positioning and investor returns (hsu2013resourcesasdual pages 2-4).

**Elimination of signaling value.** Because patents serve as credible quality signals to venture capitalists—especially for inexperienced founders and in sectors with high information asymmetry—the inability to obtain patents deprives spin-outs of a critical fundraising tool (farre‐mensa2020whatisa pages 32-35, hsu2013resourcesasdual pages 4-5, conti2013patentsassignals pages 1-5). Without patents, startups may be unable to access VC funding, secure collateralized loans, or achieve favorable valuations.

**Reduced appropriability in competitive markets.** Open disclosure of technical results, designs, and data reduces barriers to imitation. Conti et al. (2021) found that open-source engagement had zero net effect on funding in highly competitive markets (conti2021beefingitup pages 17-20). For physical-science startups where competitors can replicate disclosed compositions or processes, open disclosure without patent protection can destroy the venture's competitive moat.

**Rigid university IP structures.** University spin-outs already face challenges from non-standardized spin-off terms, excessive university equity stakes, and restrictive licensing arrangements that deter investors (sokka2025…universityintellectual pages 13-16, sokka2025…universityintellectual pages 16-19). Adding an open-disclosure policy compounds these problems by reducing the value of the IP being licensed.

**Geographic market limitation.** Even when the U.S. grace period is preserved, European and other international patent rights may be permanently lost, limiting the spin-out's addressable market and reducing potential licensing revenue (takenaka2002rethinkingtheunited pages 4-6, ozyhar2022whenspeedmatters pages 3-5).

### 7. How Successful Science Companies Have Managed These Risks

The evidence suggests several strategies used by successful science-based ventures:

**File-then-publish (patent-paper pairs).** The most common approach coordinates patent application filing with academic publication, ensuring that at least a provisional application is on file before any public disclosure occurs (gans2013contractingoverthe pages 9-12, ahmed2022scientificlabormarket pages 7-10). This preserves both patent rights and academic credit, and is the standard practice recommended by TTOs (bagley2005academicdiscourseand pages 1-3).

**Selective openness.** Rather than adopting blanket open disclosure, successful ventures practice strategic selectivity: publishing foundational science broadly while protecting specific compositions, process parameters, device designs, and manufacturing know-how through patents and trade secrets (gans2013contractingoverthe pages 25-28, ching2018controlversusexecution pages 2-3). Open-source participation in complementary areas (e.g., software tools, analytical methods) can attract talent and investment without compromising core IP (conti2021beefingitup pages 5-8).

**Leveraging tacit knowledge.** For complex physical-science technologies, significant commercialization knowledge remains tacit and cannot be replicated from publications alone (lee2020tacitknowledgeand pages 13-17, lee2020tacitknowledgeand pages 21-23). Faculty inventors possess experiential know-how essential for scale-up, troubleshooting, and adaptation (lee2020tacitknowledgeand pages 23-25). This tacit dimension provides a natural appropriability mechanism that persists even when formal IP is weak.

**Progressive IP formalization.** Deep-tech hardware startups typically show progressive strengthening of IP protection across funding stages: at pre-seed, patents may be filed or in preparation; by Series A, most have granted patents (schmitz2024exploringtheintersection pages 48-51). This progression signals maturity and reduces investor risk.

**Complementary value capture.** Hardware and materials companies can combine open disclosure with service-based revenue, consulting, manufacturing expertise, and ecosystem orchestration (li2021understandingthemotivations pages 15-18, li2021understandingthemotivations pages 4-6, li2021understandingthemotivations pages 22-24). These complementary assets provide defensibility even when product designs are partially open.

**Execution speed as appropriability.** The control-versus-execution framework of Ching, Gans, and Stern (2018) shows that rapid commercialization can itself serve as a source of competitive advantage, substituting for formal IP when speed-to-market matters more than exclusion (ching2018controlversusexecution pages 2-3, ching2018controlversusexecution pages 1-2). This is most viable when the technology requires significant process development that competitors cannot easily replicate from published results alone.

### 8. Synthesis and Implications

The evidence strongly indicates that for science-based startups in the physical sciences and engineering, patents are not merely legal protections but critical financial instruments. They causally improve access to venture capital, enable collateralized lending, and signal quality to investors (farre‐mensa2020whatisa pages 1-5, farre‐mensa2020whatisa pages 5-8, hsu2013resourcesasdual pages 2-4). An open-by-default policy that publishes results, data, designs, and code before patent filing directly threatens these benefits by destroying novelty—particularly in Europe's absolute-novelty regime—and eliminating the signaling function of patents (struve2012endingunnecessarynovelty pages 3-5, ozyhar2022whenspeedmatters pages 5-7).

However, the relationship between openness and commercial success is not uniformly negative. Selective, strategic openness can attract venture capital for novel technologies (conti2021beefingitup pages 5-8), accelerate development through community participation (conti2021beefingitup pages 1-5), and build adoption in nascent markets (li2021understandingthemotivations pages 14-15). The key is sequencing: filing patent applications before public disclosure preserves both appropriability and the ability to publish openly. The patent-paper pair model demonstrates that patents and publications can be complements rather than substitutes when properly coordinated (gans2013contractingoverthe pages 25-28, gans2013contractingoverthe pages 7-9).

For investors in university spin-outs, the principal risk of open-by-default policies is the irreversible loss of patent rights and the associated signaling, financing, and exclusionary benefits. Successful science companies manage this risk through file-then-publish protocols, selective disclosure strategies, reliance on tacit knowledge, progressive IP formalization, and complementary value-capture mechanisms. These approaches allow ventures to participate in open science while preserving the IP foundation that underpins venture financing and competitive positioning in capital-intensive physical-science markets.
