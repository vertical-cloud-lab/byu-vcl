# Independent investment-committee review

**Reviewer:** independent reanalysis performed 25 November 2026.  
**Scope:** the nine supplied files, independent price retrievals, selected primary sources, and the first-round sources recoverable through the memo's own public links. I distinguish a reproduced calculation from a verified underlying fact and from an investment inference.

## Executive judgment

**The memo has overcorrected toward a negative view of openness, especially for physical-science ventures.** It is substantially better than a promotional open-science brief: it includes losses, entry prices, public/private distinctions, and counterevidence. But its headlines repeatedly turn contingent risks into general rules, and its test design makes the skeptical position unusually easy to retain. A few passages still favor openness without sufficient causal support. This is an asymmetrically skeptical memo, not an even-handed estimate of the openness effect.

**I reproduce the central H1 arithmetic. I do not accept “no premium” as an established fact.** At the memo snapshot, the selected 14 open-origin listings have median terminal wealth relative to QQQ of **0.5206**, versus **0.8361** for 25 closed-core listings. Median nominal wealth from a one-dollar offer/reference-price investment is **0.9306 dollars** versus **1.5747 dollars**. The annualized excess-return median gap is **−14.21 percentage points**; my bootstrap 95% interval is **−24.96 to +2.17 points**. This is evidence of a negative descriptive sample gap, not evidence of equality.

Listing-year controls yield a relative-wealth ratio of **1.165**, with a **0.521–2.607** 95% interval and **p = 0.700**. Those bounds permit economically large benefits and penalties. Moreover, completing the preplanned first-day-close sensitivity changes its unadjusted gap to **−14.49 points** and its bootstrap interval to **−24.40 to −0.91**. A permutation test is less decisive (**p = 0.0678**), and year adjustment is still inconclusive. “None of the differences is significant” is therefore not robust; nor does this sensitivity establish a causal openness penalty.

**My replacement conclusion:**

> In this small, incompletely screened listing sample, open-origin companies have lower unadjusted median benchmark-relative wealth. The comparison does not identify a causal premium or penalty from openness; year-adjusted estimates are imprecise and depend on cohort, entry-price and inference choices. These public-market observations do not establish the venture economics of an autonomous-alloy spin-out.

**Committee implication:** I would not approve an investment *on this memo alone*, but I would not reject the company because its work is open. I would require a product-specific financing, qualification and rights case, with staged funding against industrial validation and repeat paid demand.

## 1. Framing: where the memo is unfair

### The direction of the bias

The strongest negative framing conflates four different decisions: publishing papers, releasing code, releasing experimental data, and granting away exclusive patent rights. It also conflates a university research program, an independent company, and several very different funders. None of these choices has a single investment effect.

The preregistration is useful discipline, but not a neutral null hypothesis. H1 requires open origin to beat closed peers by at least **5 percentage points per year** before the concern is weakened. A zero or modest positive true advantage would still fail this rule. H3 asks whether open hardware has **ever** reached a billion-dollar value, whereas the memo discounts a historical precedent because its later valuation fell and its newest products are less open. H4 and H6 also allow selected adverse examples or patent studies to sustain broad openness concerns without a matched causal comparison.

### Specific examples of unequal treatment

- **The open-hardware goalpost moves.** The historical Opentrons 1.8-billion-dollar mark is independently reported, and OT-2 hardware files are public. That does not settle whether its main products met a strict open-hardware licence definition at that moment. But a later down-round cannot refute “ever reached a billion.” Its pandemic-testing operations also complicate attribution of the valuation to robot openness. The correct result is **qualified precedent / classification unresolved**, not a clean failure of the preregistered test. [1, 2, 3]
- **Private success is discounted by current valuation while historical failure is amplified with buyer impairments.** A buyer's later goodwill write-off is not the sellers' return. MakerBot's historical open phase, the later closure of designs, its acquisition, and the buyer's subsequent operational problems are separate events.
- **The proposed solution is assumed to be closed.** “Venture money only for the closed product” is not implied by the memo's own Red Hat, MongoDB, Supabase or hosted-service examples. Venture money needs scalable capture, not necessarily closed source.
- **The opposite overreach remains in the favorable section.** Similar margins among surviving listings do not prove the absence of a structural penalty. Agent-created Supabase databases do not prove its licence caused its growth. Open provenance may help qualification, but there is no measured reduction in alloy qualification time here.

## 2. Independent H1 recomputation

### Definition and interpretation

For company i, with entry price P, terminal proceeds or share value V, and matched-window QQQ total-return factor B:

\[M_i=V_i/P_i,\qquad R_i=M_i/B_i.\]

M is the value of one initial dollar. R is its wealth relative to a same-date dollar in QQQ. **R = 0.52 means 52% of benchmark terminal wealth; it does not mean 52% of QQQ's net percentage return.** The cohort medians of M and R need not refer to the same company and must not be divided to construct a hypothetical median benchmark.

I also compute the preregistered annualized statistic:

\[E_i=M_i^{1/T_i}-1-\left(B_i^{1/T_i}-1\right).\]

This is the arithmetic difference of compound annual growth rates, not the compound growth rate of relative wealth. I use 365.25 days per year and calculate the difference between the cohorts' medians of E.

### Data lineage and independent checks

1. **Original-snapshot reconstruction:** I join the raw listing offers and deal terms to the supplied endpoint observations, then recompute all multiples, holding periods, benchmark-relative wealth and annualized returns. I do not use the supplied return or PME columns as calculated answers. This reproduces the primary summary and all memo sensitivity point estimates.
2. **Independent same-date check:** I separately reconstruct from the raw listing table and retrieved Yahoo histories plus timestamped regular-market quotes, without copying the supplied endpoint/benchmark fields. All 20 live-company memo endpoint prices agree with independent quotes to within **0.000009 dollars**. Historical QQQ ratios also agree within floating-point precision. This raw-input-only calculation again gives median R **0.5206 vs 0.8361**, and median M **0.9306 vs 1.5747**, after explicit USD/SolarWinds/MuleSoft corrections; those corrections do not change the central medians. Its adjusted wealth ratio is **1.159**, CI **0.518–2.591**. [4, 5, 6, 7, 8]
3. **Daily-bar-only refresh:** the retrieved daily bars have null closes on 24 November, despite timestamped closing-time metadata quotes. I do not silently mix the two. A separate sensitivity uses the last common complete daily close, **23 November 2026**: median M **0.9306 vs 1.6804**, median R **0.5206 vs 0.8073**, and median excess gap **−13.65 points**. Different fields in live vendor metadata can disagree; the cache and method distinction are retained.
4. **Independent inference:** I use 50,000 within-cohort bootstrap resamples for median-gap intervals, seed 20261125. Original bootstrap code/seed are absent, so small endpoint differences are not errors by themselves. For listing-year controls I fit ordinary least squares to log R, with an open-origin indicator and categorical year effects, and use heteroskedasticity-robust HC3 standard errors with a small-sample t reference distribution.

## 3. Numerical audit: errors, unsupported claims and numbers that check out

The accompanying **numeric_claim_audit.csv** contains **93 audit entries** covering the substantive numbers, dates, counts, ratios and associated inference claims in the memo, with line references. Repeated claims are consolidated. I do not treat issue numbers, section numbering, file names or hash identifiers as investment statistics.

### Definite errors or materially misleading numerical descriptions

1. **Closed survivor EV/revenue is 5.906, not 6.2.** The attached snapshot's closed gross margin is **75.441%**, conventionally rounded to **75%**, not 76%; open is **75.619%**. The difference is small, but the statement that the four metrics all use a consistent cohort is false. The displayed open numbers use **JFrog included**, while H1 excludes it. Reassigning JFrog to closed gives a closed EV/revenue of **6.2325**, explaining the 6.2 figure, but then the open EV/revenue must be **5.068**, not 6.0. The memo appears to mix definitions.
2. **The NSF −3.4% comparison mixes fiscal baselines.** It is **8.75 / 9.06 − 1 = −3.42%**, comparing FY2026 with FY2024. Against the evidence file's FY2025 available amount **8.826**, FY2026 is **−0.86%**. NIH +1.0% and DOE +1.9% use FY2025→FY2026. The same row should label its denominators.
3. **The 4.15-billion-dollar OSS figure is not what OSS “cost to write.”** It is a modeled **cost to recreate the observed widely-used OSS once**. The 8.8-trillion-dollar figure is modeled firm-level avoided replacement expenditure; neither is producer revenue, market capitalization or realized shareholder return. [18]
4. **“Strateos (folded 2023)” is wrong.** Its company announcement describes a pivot and expansion toward on-site cloud-lab implementations. Abandoning a hosted service is not closing the company. [19]
5. **The kit profit is a stated figure, not reconciled economics.** The original author's table says **80** list price, **15.57** platform fee, **51.19** parts, **6** labor, and **2.24** profit. Listed costs imply **7.24** left, not 2.24: the table has an unexplained **5-dollar gap**. The memo accurately says “stated,” but should not build a financing conclusion on it. “About ten times the price to fund one engineer” has no supporting volume, salary, elasticity or cost model. [20, 21]
6. **The “none significant at 95%” limit is not robust to completing the preplanned first-day sample.** The primary offer-price comparison remains nonsignificant, but complete first-day and narrow-category bootstrap intervals exclude zero. Test dependence and multiplicity make a categorical penalty claim inappropriate too.
7. **Reference prices are mislabeled as offers.** Palantir and the SPACs already have caveats in the raw table; Amplitude is another direct-list reference, not a fourth comparable offer price. I independently verified **Tenable 23**, **UiPath 56**, and **C3.ai 42** in issuer pricing releases. [22, 23, 24, 11]

## 4. What matters most for an autonomous-alloy university spin-out

The memo does mention manufacturing transfer, integration cost, legal clarity and customer validation. **Its problem is weighting and specificity, not complete silence.** I would put the following ahead of a general openness narrative.

| Priority | Concern underweighted or missing | What I would require before financing |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **A paid industrial use case, not discovery throughput.** Who buys: alloy producer, powder maker, OEM, qualification laboratory, or a research department? Which existing specification will change? Are the incumbent alloy, heat treatment, geometry redesign or conventional design-of-experiments cheaper alternatives? | Named application, incumbent baseline, quantified customer value, procurement owner, paid pilot with a conversion/qualification plan, and separate service-versus-IP-versus-equipment revenue models. |
| 2 | **Capital-to-qualification and investor dilution.** Multi-year scale-up may require repeated rounds before sales; success at a headline valuation can still produce poor common/preferred returns. | Bottom-up cash schedule through independently qualified product, follow-on funding plan, downside salvage value, option pool and university equity/royalty stack, liquidation-preference waterfall, and milestone-based dilution scenarios. |
| 3 | **Manufacturability across the full process–structure–property chain.** Small coupons are not production parts. Powder chemistry, oxygen/moisture contamination, recycling, atomization yield, cracking, porosity, heat treatment, orientation and lot variability can erase a modeled property gain. | Blind holdout batches; cross-machine and cross-site replication; full composition/process/thermal history; fatigue, corrosion, creep and fracture tests relevant to the application; yield/cost and feedstock availability at production scale. These are application-specific diligence gates, not claimed failures of this lab. |
| 4 | **Autonomy must beat a serious human/physics baseline.** More proposed candidates or simulator evaluations are not more validated discoveries. Simulation-to-experiment bias, leakage, batch effects and drifting instruments can defeat active learning. | Randomized or prespecified comparisons with expert design, random search and conventional Bayesian optimization; cost/time per validated improvement; failed-run capture; out-of-distribution calibration; calibrated error bars; explicit human intervention and uptime logs. |
| 5 | **Data and vendor rights may be the binding constraint.** Academic-only CALPHAD/property databases or vendor output terms may prohibit commercial reuse, redistribution or training. Owning a machine or an alloy does not confer all data/software rights. | Licence-by-licence commercial rights matrix, model-training/output provenance, FTO search, vendor API/export rights, contractual raw-data portability, and a signed allowed-publication/embargo plan. The original DAL audit already flags inconsistent Thermo-Calc partner/IP positions and questionable “open” data labels. [37] |
| 6 | **University and sponsor transaction risk.** Inventorship/assignment, multiple institutions, student departures, background IP, public-funding rights and sponsor exclusivity can block a fundable exclusive field even after filing. | Signed exclusive option/licence with field, geography, improvements, sublicensing, diligence milestones, royalties and termination clauses; inventor assignments; conflict-of-interest and lab-use agreements; counsel-approved grant/OT terms. Domestic manufacturing obligations may constrain exclusive US licences; this is award-specific. [39] |
| 7 | **Safety, quality and cyber-physical liability.** Autonomous handling of reactive metal powders, lasers/furnaces and robots creates operational and product risk; remote-control compromise is not just a software bug. | Hazard analysis, interlocks and independent shutdown paths, traceable metrology/calibration, cybersecurity and access control, insurance, customer-relevant quality systems and certification plan. Defence/export-controlled work also limits indiscriminate release. |
| 8 | **Operational gross margin and customer concentration.** A fully automated demo can hide technicians, cleaning, calibration, failed experiments, service calls, idle capex and application-engineer costs. Industrial pilots may be nonrecurring. | Utilization/downtime-adjusted cost per *accepted result*, sensitivity to failed-run rate and operator intervention, service-level agreements, recurring versus one-off revenue, concentration, sales-cycle and working-capital stress tests. |
| 9 | **Who is selling an investable asset?** A public research platform, open benchmark, contract research organization and alloy licensor have different ownership, mission and capital needs. Grant money is not free commercial capital; separating layers can create transfer-pricing/IP conflicts. | A clean organizational and cash/IP boundary, explicit publication obligations, customer data ownership, realistic maintenance budget and governance surviving founder/student turnover. |

**My decision gates:** first settle which business is being proposed; then obtain a signed rights package and a paid industrial validation contract; then fund bounded validation/qualification milestones with independently measured costs. Only after that would I price a venture round or decide which code/data layer should be open. No openness category substitutes for these gates.

## 5. Important evidence favoring openness that is missing or underweighted

1. **A closer physical-science causal analogue is omitted.** Probst and colleagues study the DOE national-laboratory open-access mandate and report **42% more use of affected papers in patents**, with **small firms the primary beneficiaries**. This is much closer to a university materials spin-out's access to upstream research than a Tailwind documentation funnel or a software IPO median. It does not prove a 42% revenue/return gain or justify releasing all proprietary process data. [40]
2. **The NIH result is real and should not be cordoned off as “only public value.”** The published paper finds **12–27% more in-text patent citations** to NIH-funded research after the mandate. That is private inventive use of publicly accessible science, although not producer returns. Small companies can be recipients of the commons as well as donors. [41]
3. **The lab's own upstream dependence is an investment benefit of openness.** Open optimization code, materials data, interoperability schemas and reproducible benchmarks can reduce development and integration costs and permit vendor switching. The relevant investor calculation is net saved cost/risk plus induced sales minus disclosure/maintenance cost—not simply how much a hyperscaler extracts. The original DAL audit inventories both open dependencies and proprietary bottlenecks. [37]
4. **Openness can make a small supplier safer to buy from.** Inspectable methods, data portability, open drivers and a community-maintained fallback can reduce customer lock-in and vendor-abandonment risk. That can matter in long-lived capital equipment and qualification workflows. This is a plausible demand/continuity mechanism to test with procurement teams, not a measured sales uplift in the present data.
5. **The attached evidence itself has material financial counterpoints that the headline underweights.** Stack Overflow FY2026 revenue rose **115 to 129 million dollars** (about **12.2%**) and adjusted EBITDA moved **−9 to +12 million** despite collapsing questions. NIH research-grant dollars rose **3.0%** to **26.503 billion**, partly because of front-loading, despite fewer awards. Wikimedia signed AI licensing deals; Tailwind gained sponsors. Traffic, grant counts and monetization need not move together. These facts do not eliminate risk, but they defeat a one-way “AI/funding simply destroys the funnel” reading.
6. **An open complement can enlarge private capture without requiring closed hosted code.** Supabase's reported growth, Bun/Astral acquisition interest, foundation continuity, and actual open-origin acquisition proceeds are counterexamples to “closed product only.” Conversely, company-controlled relicensing can destroy partner confidence. A durable, credibly governed open standard may make the commercial integration/service layer more valuable. Which effect dominates must be tested in this company's customer economics.
7. **Open-first dissemination and patenting are not opposites.** Adequately enabled filings followed by publication, field-limited licences, time-bounded data exclusivity and publication of validation methods can preserve both adoption and patent options. A provisional filing is not magic protection: it must adequately support later claims, and prosecution/FTO still cost money. The right architecture may be layered openness, but it should be selected from economics and obligations rather than imposed as an ideological compromise.

## 6. Limits of my review

- I independently retrieved the memo-date live quotes, benchmark histories, FX and selected corporate-action prices. I did not independently audit every historical deal filing or all raw-source confidence ratings. A label “high” in the attached listing table is not my certification.
- I recovered linked first-round case data and DAL sources, which allowed checks beyond the packet. Their own AI-generated descriptions and private-company marks can still be wrong. The round-two Edison directory, exact regression scripts and some primary underlying studies remain absent from the supplied materials.
- The complete listing universe remains uncertified. I have not converted plausible candidates into a supposedly exhaustive corrected census. H1 still conditions on reaching public markets and is not a venture failure-denominator study.
- Bootstrap intervals assume independent listing observations. Hortonworks/Cloudera and repeated Instructure listings violate that approximation; the small sample makes more sophisticated clustered inference unstable. No risk-adjustment beyond benchmark/vintage is sufficient to identify licence causality.
- The median statistic underweights the power-law winners relevant to venture funds. The arithmetic means provided are informative but are not a reconstructed time-weighted portfolio. Taxes, transaction costs, allocation constraints and private liquidation preferences are not modeled.
- I flag legal issues for counsel; this is not a legal opinion. An audit of a proposal is not evidence of a signed award or current compliance status.

## 7. Discretionary analytical decisions

- **I separated replication from independent validation.** This avoids presenting reuse of computed return columns as an independent recomputation.
- **I retained the memo's primary cohort for replication, without endorsing its completeness.** Candidate additions and ambiguous classifications remain explicit sensitivities rather than outcome-driven edits.
- **I used log relative wealth for the year-controlled wealth ratio, and separately modeled annualized excess returns.** This makes the changed estimands visible instead of saying the same “gap disappeared.”
- **I chose HC3 t intervals for small-sample robust inference and disclosed alternatives.** I did not force an exact match to an unexplained original interval.
- **I completed the preplanned first-day sample with sourced prices and reported competing tests.** I did not ignore the adverse result or call a marginal bootstrap interval causal proof.
- **I used a USD investor perspective for SUSE, and made corporate-action liquidation/reinvestment policies explicit.** An EUR return or tax-basis value is not automatically the value of an invested USD dollar.
- **I treated unavailable evidence as unverified, not false, and updated the audit when linked originals were recovered.** I independently reproduced H2 and the original 10/15 event count instead of repeating the memo's reviewer verdict.
- **I did not pool the sixteen sensitivities as independent studies or choose the significant one as definitive.** Their role is to expose reasonable specification dependence.
