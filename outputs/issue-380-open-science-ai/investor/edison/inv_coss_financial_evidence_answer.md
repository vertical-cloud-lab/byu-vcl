# Financial Performance of Commercial Open-Source Software (COSS) Companies Versus Proprietary Software: An Evidence Review for Equity Investors

## 1. Overview and State of Evidence

The empirical evidence on the comparative financial performance of commercial open-source software (COSS) companies relative to proprietary software companies is thinner, more methodologically contested, and more subject to survivorship bias than the investment thesis typically implies. Despite the existence of several headline-grabbing exits and high private valuations, no rigorous, peer-reviewed matched-sample study comparing COSS and proprietary software company financial metrics—revenue growth, margins, valuation multiples, or post-IPO returns—on a like-for-like basis was identified in the retrieved literature. What follows synthesizes the best available evidence while flagging where investors should exercise caution.

## 2. Revenue Growth

Red Hat's fiscal year 2019 revenue was $3.4 billion, growing at approximately 15% year-over-year at the time IBM acquired the company for $34 billion—the largest software acquisition in history at the time (batista2026opensourcevs. pages 39-42). HashiCorp achieved sufficient enterprise revenue scale to IPO at a roughly $14 billion valuation in 2021 (pandey2025theeconomicsof pages 23-27). Among a purposive sample of 44 visible open-source developer tools studied by Pandey (2025), Open Core + SaaS projects achieved median total funding of $40 million, with five reaching unicorn valuations: Supabase ($5B), Grafana ($6B), Hasura ($1B), PlanetScale ($1B), and Sentry ($1B) (pandey2025theeconomicsof pages 18-23). These are funding and valuation figures, not revenue growth rates, and the sample was deliberately constructed to include approximately 15 unicorn or major-acquisition successes, creating substantial survivorship bias (pandey2025theeconomicsof pages 8-13, pandey2025theeconomicsof pages 31-37).

No controlled comparison of COSS versus proprietary revenue growth was found. The absence of this basic benchmark is itself informative: it suggests that the category is too heterogeneous and the successful population too small to support reliable statistical inference.

## 3. Gross and Operating Margins

No reliable COSS-versus-proprietary gross-margin or operating-margin comparison was identified. Fermigier (2026) provides a conceptual framework distinguishing three revenue families in COSS—Assurance (subscriptions with scale economies), Adaptation (labor-intensive consulting with minimal scale), and Assistance (support)—and explicitly states that the treatment is conceptual and no magnitudes are provided (fermigier2026valuecapturein pages 7-9). The implication is that COSS companies with predominantly services-based revenue should have structurally lower gross margins than managed-SaaS or proprietary-license businesses, but those emphasizing hosted SaaS offerings (like MongoDB Atlas or Databricks) may approach conventional SaaS margins.

Critically, an analyst cited by Foster (2024) found "no evidence that relicensing improved the financial results of the vendors studied"—meaning that even the most aggressive margin-protection strategy available to COSS companies has not demonstrably improved profitability (foster2411thenewdynamics pages 3-6).

## 4. Conversion of Free Users to Paying Customers

No defensible COSS-specific conversion rate benchmark was identified. The general SaaS freemium literature reports that the majority of firms achieve free-to-paid conversion rates of approximately 2–5% (smart2025creatingab2b pages 86-89, kessler2026scalingasaas pages 1-3, smart2025creatingab2b pages 89-92). In one anonymized B2B SaaS case study ("CloudMetrics"), conversion improved from 3.8% under a feature-only model to 7.4% under a fully optimized product-led approach through a combination of usage limits, hybrid freemium design, and lifecycle onboarding (kessler2026scalingasaas pages 16-19). Open-core COSS companies face an analogous but structurally distinct challenge: the free tier must provide sufficient value that the open-source community builds around it, while withholding enough differentiated functionality—enterprise authentication, compliance tooling, managed hosting—to motivate upgrade (batista2026opensourcevs. pages 39-42, smart2025creatingab2b pages 86-89). The governance boundary between community and enterprise editions "requires constant negotiation between the commercial imperatives of the firm and the expectations of the open source community" (batista2026opensourcevs. pages 39-42).

## 5. Net Revenue Retention

No COSS-specific net revenue retention (NRR) benchmark was found. The CloudMetrics case reported 127% NRR for freemium-acquired customers versus 109% for sales-led enterprise cohorts, with team-workspace adopters reaching 142% NRR (kessler2026scalingasaas pages 16-19, kessler2026scalingasaas pages 1-3). Top-quartile SaaS companies generally achieve NRR above 120% (kessler2026scalingasaas pages 3-5). These figures pertain to general freemium SaaS, not to open-source licensing specifically, and cannot be attributed to openness versus product design or cohort selection.

## 6. Valuation Multiples

The Linux Foundation (2025) reportedly claims that "commercial open-source startups average seven times greater valuations at IPO compared to closed-source peers" (krause2026theopensourcefrontier pages 4-7). This claim is cited secondhand in Krause (2026) and could not be independently verified from the original source. It warrants substantial skepticism, as discussed in Section 9 below.

Specific COSS private valuations in the retrieved data include Supabase ($5B), Grafana ($6B), and Vercel ($9.3B), all achieved during the 2020–2025 period (pandey2025theeconomicsof pages 18-23, pandey2025theeconomicsof pages 31-37). However, these are headline valuations for selected successful companies, not standardized EV/revenue multiples measured at comparable dates across a representative sample. Pandey (2025) cautions that "the 2020-2025 timeframe captured an unusually favorable funding environment" and that "the subsequent market correction in 2023-2024 showed some valuation compression, suggesting that the highest multiples may have reflected temporary market conditions" (pandey2025theeconomicsof pages 31-37).

## 7. Post-IPO Stock Returns and Acquisition Prices Relative to IPO Prices

No systematic study of COSS post-IPO total returns versus proprietary software was identified. The most informative data point is HashiCorp, which IPO'd at approximately $14 billion in 2021 and was acquired by IBM for $6.4 billion in February 2025—roughly 54% below its IPO valuation (pandey2025theeconomicsof pages 23-27). While this decline also reflects the broader 2022–2024 tech correction, it demonstrates that a successful COSS IPO does not guarantee durable public-market value.

Krause (2026) provides event-study evidence from the other side: open-source AI model releases from Chinese laboratories caused cumulative abnormal returns of −0.63% on proprietary software stocks on a single announcement day (Kimi K3 release), extending to −1.08% over the subsequent five trading days (krause2026theopensourcefrontier pages 7-10, krause2026theopensourcefrontier pages 10-13). The tech-to-broad-market volatility ratio (VXN/VIX) reached 1.73 during the 2025–2026 open-source AI shock cycle—higher than during the 2008 Financial Crisis (~1.60) or COVID-19 (~1.55)—indicating that equity markets view open-source competition as a potent, sector-specific threat to proprietary software profits (krause2026theopensourcefrontier pages 1-4, krause2026theopensourcefrontier pages 4-7).

## 8. Venture-Capital Exit Outcomes, Failures, and Shutdowns

The headline COSS acquisitions are well documented: IBM–Red Hat at $34 billion (2019), Microsoft–GitHub at $7.5 billion (2018), and IBM–HashiCorp at $6.4 billion (2025) (batista2026opensourcevs. pages 44-47, pandey2025theeconomicsof pages 23-27). These demonstrate strong strategic demand for open-source ecosystems.

However, the denominator is poorly documented. Mann (2006), using VentureXpert data through August 2006, identified only 135 U.S. VC-backed firms whose business descriptions mentioned "Linux," "Apache," or "open source"—compared with nearly 8,000 VC-backed software and Internet firms during 1998–2006—and noted that many of the 135 did not directly commercialize open-source products (mann2006commercializingopensource pages 12-15). No exit returns, failure rates, or fund-level performance figures were reported.

Pandey (2025) classified outcomes as active, acquired, public, or shut down but acknowledged that "failed projects that never gained traction or shut down early may be underrepresented, potentially inflating apparent success rates" and that "failed commercialization attempts likely outnumber successes but remain less documented" (pandey2025theeconomicsof pages 8-13, pandey2025theeconomicsof pages 31-37). No purely community-funded project in the sample received acquisition offers at meaningful valuations (pandey2025theeconomicsof pages 23-27, pandey2025theeconomicsof pages 27-31). This missing failure denominator is decisive: success-only acquisition and IPO statistics cannot support an expected-return conclusion for a prospective VC or public-equity investor.

## 9. Critical Assessment of the 2025 Linux Foundation / Serena / COSSA Report

The Linux Foundation (2025) report on COSS venture outcomes, reportedly produced in collaboration with Serena Capital (an open-source-focused venture capital firm) and COSSA (a trade association for commercial open-source companies), claims that COSS startups average seven times greater valuations at IPO compared to closed-source peers (krause2026theopensourcefrontier pages 4-7). Several methodological and conflict-of-interest concerns apply:

**Conflicts of interest.** All three co-authors have direct financial or institutional stakes in favorable COSS outcomes. The Linux Foundation hosts COSS projects and receives corporate sponsorship from COSS companies; Serena Capital invests in open-source ventures and would benefit from fundraising narratives emphasizing COSS outperformance; COSSA is an advocacy organization for commercial open-source firms. This configuration mirrors what Batista (2026) identifies as a structural limitation of the field: "accounts of successful proprietary products dominate over analyses of commercial software that failed to achieve adoption," and conversely, the COSS advocacy community selectively promotes success stories (batista2026opensourcevs. pages 8-11).

**Survivorship and selection bias.** The 7× claim is extraordinary and would require disclosure of how the COSS and proprietary cohorts were defined and matched. COSS companies that reached IPO represent a tiny fraction of all venture-backed COSS attempts; comparing IPO survivors against a broader proprietary population, or against proprietary IPOs from different cohort years, could mechanically generate inflated multiples.

**Definitional discretion.** The boundary of "commercial open source" is inherently contestable. GitHub, Slack, and many other companies occupy ambiguous positions between open and proprietary (batista2026opensourcevs. pages 8-11, batista2026opensourcevs. pages 39-42). Including or excluding specific firms can materially change reported outcomes.

**Absence of independent replication.** No peer-reviewed academic study in the retrieved literature independently confirms the 7× figure. Krause (2026) cites it descriptively but does not validate it (krause2026theopensourcefrontier pages 4-7).

Investors should treat this claim as an advocacy statistic rather than a peer-reviewed finding until its methodology, sample, and matching criteria are independently audited.

## 10. Evidence Cutting Against Open Source as a Business Model

### 10.1 Cloud-Provider Free-Riding

Cloud providers can commercialize managed services based on open-source software while the original vendor bears development costs. Amazon Web Services' Elasticsearch offering was the paradigmatic case: Elastic alleged that AWS profited from Elasticsearch without contributing according to open-source norms, prompting Elastic's 2021 relicensing from Apache 2.0 to the non-OSI SSPL and Elastic License (batista2026opensourcevs. pages 16-18, foster2411thenewdynamics pages 8-11). Notably, Elastic employees already supplied over 95% of Elasticsearch code additions and deletions, challenging claims that broad external community development materially subsidized Elastic's R&D costs (foster2411thenewdynamics pages 8-11). Fermigier (2026) formalizes this dynamic: when a hyperscaler captures the service layer's revenue, the COSS vendor's service margin falls, and both periods of the open strategy's value decline—pushing impatient, venture-backed firms toward restrictive relicensing (fermigier2026valuecapturein pages 9-12, fermigier2026valuecapturein pages 14-16).

Fermigier (2026) proves formally that the community's fork capability shrinks the enclosure region—the set of conditions under which relicensing is optimal for the vendor (fermigier2026valuecapturein pages 9-12). Developer-facing infrastructure projects, whose user bases can reconstitute the software, have a built-in deterrent against enclosure; end-user applications with less technically capable user populations do not (fermigier2026valuecapturein pages 12-14). The practical implication for investors is that the COSS moat is fragile precisely where the community is strongest.

### 10.3 Structural Underinvestment and Fragility

A 2024 Harvard Business School study estimated the supply-side value of widely used open-source software at $4.15 billion versus a demand-side replacement value of $8.8 trillion—a 2,000-fold disparity indicating chronic underinvestment in the infrastructure that sustains the digital economy (batista2026opensourcevs. pages 44-47). The XZ Utils backdoor (CVE-2024-3094), where a threat actor spent 2.6 years building credibility with a sole overworked maintainer to inject a sophisticated backdoor, illustrated the governance fragility that results from this underinvestment (batista2026opensourcevs. pages 37-39).

## 12. Conclusions

From an equity investor's perspective, the empirical evidence on COSS financial performance is dominated by a handful of spectacular exits (Red Hat, GitHub, HashiCorp) and high private valuations during a favorable funding environment, while systematic comparative evidence—controlled for growth stage, revenue model, sector, and market cycle—is essentially absent. Key findings include:

1. **Revenue growth and margins**: Individual COSS success stories show strong growth (Red Hat at 15% YoY pre-acquisition), but no representative comparison exists. Margin profiles depend heavily on revenue mix (services vs. managed SaaS) rather than openness per se (batista2026opensourcevs. pages 39-42, fermigier2026valuecapturein pages 7-9).

2. **Conversion and NRR**: General freemium benchmarks (2–5% conversion, 120%+ NRR for top-quartile SaaS) apply, but no COSS-specific data has been published (smart2025creatingab2b pages 86-89, kessler2026scalingasaas pages 1-3, kessler2026scalingasaas pages 3-5).

3. **Valuations**: The 7× IPO valuation claim from the Linux Foundation / Serena / COSSA report has not been independently validated and comes from organizations with direct financial stakes in COSS success (krause2026theopensourcefrontier pages 4-7).

4. **Post-IPO returns**: HashiCorp's acquisition at roughly 54% below its IPO valuation illustrates that COSS IPO success does not guarantee durable public-market value (pandey2025theeconomicsof pages 23-27).

5. **Exit outcomes**: The missing failure denominator is the most critical gap. Successful acquisitions prove that some COSS assets are strategically valuable, not that the average COSS investment outperforms proprietary software (pandey2025theeconomicsof pages 8-13, pandey2025theeconomicsof pages 31-37, mann2006commercializingopensource pages 12-15).

6. **Structural risks**: Cloud free-riding, the right to fork, maintainer fragility, and the governance tension between community expectations and VC exit timelines all represent material risks that cut against simple narratives of COSS superiority (foster2411thenewdynamics pages 1-3, foster2411thenewdynamics pages 3-6, batista2026opensourcevs. pages 44-47, fermigier2026valuecapturein pages 9-12).

The overarching finding is not that COSS is a poor investment, but that the evidence base does not support the claim that it is systematically superior to proprietary software from an equity-return perspective. What determines commercial outcomes is not openness itself, but control of the service and distribution layer adjacent to the open-source code—managed hosting, enterprise features, and deployment infrastructure (pandey2025theeconomicsof pages 23-27, pandey2025theeconomicsof pages 27-31). Investors should evaluate COSS companies on these commercial capabilities rather than on community metrics or advocacy statistics.
