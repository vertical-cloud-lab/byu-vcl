Question: Has any company built a profitable or venture-backed business by selling open-source self-driving laboratory or open-source laboratory automation systems (published hardware designs and software) for chemistry or materials science?

Yes. **Yes, with an important qualification: Opentrons is a venture-backed company selling laboratory automation used in chemistry and materials research, but the evidence does not establish that its current robots are *fully* open-source hardware-and-software systems.** It is a stronger example of commercial success for an *open-source-associated automation platform* than of a company selling a completely reproducible, open-design self-driving laboratory. (tachibana2026ot2eyedetectionof pages 1-4, mcgrath2023repurposingamineand pages 1-4, york2026systemicshocksand pages 28-32, pelkie2025automationandautonomous pages 86-90)

Opentrons sells the OT-2 liquid-handling robot. Researchers have used it for automated organic-synthesis library preparation and as the basis of an automated catalyst-electrodeposition and electrochemical-testing platform. A 2026 paper identifies an Opentrons-hosted OT-2 hardware repository at https://github.com/Opentrons/ot2; published descriptions also document its Python control interface. These findings support *published hardware materials and programmable software*, but the cited papers do not verify that the repository contains complete buildable designs for **every** component of the sold robot. Notably, a 2025 materials-science dissertation explicitly calls the OT-2 “not fully open source.” Nor is the research-built electrochemical system itself a complete self-driving laboratory sold by Opentrons. (tachibana2026ot2eyedetectionof pages 1-4, mcgrath2023repurposingamineand pages 1-4, fiskerbødker2025ampere2anopenhardware pages 1-2, wells2022bioclonebotalow pages 27-35, pelkie2025automationandautonomous pages 86-90)

For the business test, a 2026 **non-peer-reviewed** startup study reports approximately **$261 million in cumulative funding** for Opentrons, citing a 2024 Tracxn company profile; its Opentrons row was also checked in the source table. This is evidence for a *venture-backed* business, not for profitability. I found no verified company profit figure, and the underlying Tracxn figure was not independently audited here. (york2026systemicshocksand pages 28-32, york2026systemicshocksand pages 25-28, york2026systemicshocksand media 4572ee37)

The distinctions matter. The fully open-source Jubilee platform has been used in nanomaterials work, and a review says its components can be purchased as a kit; that does **not** establish a profitable or venture-backed kit seller. Chemspeed sells chemistry/materials robots that can be operated with the open-source *Chemspyd* interface, but the paper describes that interface as an add-on to Chemspeed’s **proprietary** software suite, not an open hardware-and-software product. (lo2024reviewoflowcost pages 10-11, lo2024reviewoflowcost pages 11-12, seifrid2024chemspydanopensource pages 1-2)

**Bottom line:** There is a credible **venture-backed, commercially sold, partly open automation precedent—Opentrons—for chemistry and materials science.** On the stricter interpretation requiring verified, complete published hardware designs *and* open control software for the particular system sold, **I could not establish a qualifying profitable or venture-backed company from the available evidence**. No profitability claim is supported. (pelkie2025automationandautonomous pages 86-90, tachibana2026ot2eyedetectionof pages 1-4, york2026systemicshocksand pages 28-32, seifrid2024chemspydanopensource pages 1-2)
