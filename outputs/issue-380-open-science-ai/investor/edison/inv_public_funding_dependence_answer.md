Question: How much do open scientific infrastructure, open-source research software and the academic market for laboratory tools depend on government research funding, and what happens to them when that funding is cut or becomes volatile? Include evidence on maintainer concentration and the bus factor (for example the finding by Hoffmann, Nagle and Zhou 2024 that a small share of developers create most of the value of open-source software), sustainability failures of open research infrastructure, the share of laboratory-equipment and life-science-tools revenue that comes from academic and government customers, and the effects of US federal science budget changes in 2025 and 2026 (NSF, NIH, DOE, caps on indirect costs) on research spending and on companies that sell to academic labs.

# Government funding and the research-tools ecosystem

**The dependence is substantial, but it takes different forms.** Federal agencies financed **$64.6 billion of the $118 billion spent on US higher-education R&D in FY2024—55%**. That money pays for researchers and purchases of equipment and supplies; indirect-cost reimbursement also helps universities operate shared facilities, IT, libraries, and research administration. Open databases and software often receive support through short-lived project grants rather than dependable operating budgets. A funding shock can therefore interrupt maintenance and purchasing even when an annual agency appropriation does not fall. The 55% figure describes *university research spending*, not the federal share of every software project or equipment supplier’s revenue. (gallo2026universitiesandindirect pages 4-5, azoulay2026indirectcostrecovery pages 1-4, imker2025sustainabilityduringinstability pages 1-4)

## Open software and infrastructure: valuable, but thinly sustained

A secondary account of Hoffmann, Nagle and Zhou’s 2024 study reports that **approximately 5% of developers produce 95% of the measured supply-side value** of widely used open-source software. It distinguishes an estimated **$4.15 billion cost to recreate that software once** from **$8.8 trillion in aggregate value to its users**. These are estimates for broadly used open source, *not* a measured government-funding share for scientific software. The original study was unavailable for direct verification here, so the 5%/95% figure should be read as a reported result rather than an independently checked calculation. (batista2026opensourcevs. pages 42-44, linaker2026makingimpactvisible pages 3-5)

Concentrated value is related to, but is **not itself a bus-factor measurement**. The bus factor asks how many indispensable people a project could lose before it could no longer be maintained. A code repository may remain downloadable after a maintainer leaves, while security fixes, scientific validation, documentation, user support, and knowledge of how to operate the service deteriorate. In a research-software study, about **41% of responding project leaders had active grant funding** and **62% wanted more funding**; even a very heavily used project had only partial developer salary support. The authors caution that downloads and commits alone do not establish whether a project is sustainable. (yehudi2309individualcontextfreeonline pages 5-8, yehudi2309individualcontextfreeonline pages 32-35, linaker2026makingimpactvisible pages 3-5)

The failure risk is observed, not merely hypothetical. Strecker and colleagues identified **191 closed research-data repositories**, representing **6.2% of those indexed** in the registry they studied; the median age at closure was **12 years**. They identified **27 closures involving economic failure or funding cuts**. Among the 191, **84 (44%)** showed evidence of data migration, but **90 (47.1%)** neither retained access nor named a successor custodian—*a risk of data loss, not proof that every underlying dataset was destroyed*. One formerly certified repository, BIIACS, closed without accessible data or resolving persistent identifiers. (strecker2023disappearingrepositoriestaking pages 11-12, strecker2023disappearingrepositoriestaking pages 9-10, strecker2023disappearingrepositoriestaking pages 10-11)

In a 2025 study of **nine long-lived life-science databases** previously supported by US agencies, operators described reduced support, staff and expertise at risk, diverted work, and potential loss of usable access even when raw data survived elsewhere. NIH’s NIAID had **ended funding for VEuPathDB in September 2024**; its subsequent user survey received **1,862 responses**, of which **80% reported weekly use**. The Arabidopsis Information Resource had earlier introduced user charges after NSF began phasing out support. These cases illustrate possible responses—service contraction, replacement funding, charging, or closure—not that all nine databases shut down in 2025. Open-infrastructure principles accordingly recommend recurring revenue for recurring operations, reserves, and plans for transferring data and services if an organization fails. (imker2025sustainabilityduringinstability pages 1-4, imker2025sustainabilityduringinstability pages 13-16, bilder2020theprinciplesof pages 1-3)

## How exposed are laboratory-tool suppliers?

The clearest company-specific figure recovered is **10x Genomics**: the company has said that **US academic and government research funding supports approximately 40–50% of its revenue**. This is exposure to *research-funded customers*, **not** a claim that federal agencies buy 40–50% of its products directly. No comparably verified percentage for the laboratory-tools industry as a whole, or for Thermo Fisher, Agilent, Bruker, or Waters individually, was established from the retrieved material; 10x’s share should not be extrapolated to them. (philippidis202510xrocheprognosys pages 4-7)

The purchasing mechanism is straightforward: labs uncertain about grants can defer an instrument order, run fewer assays, or constrain reagent and service purchases. In October 2025, an analyst explicitly linked 10x’s near-term revenue risk to NIH funding and uncertainty about the proposed indirect-cost cap. The company had eliminated about **8% of its workforce** in a broader **$50 million cost-reduction** effort. That does **not** establish that federal cuts caused those layoffs or an overall sales decline: 10x’s reported first-half 2025 revenue was **up 11.5%** year on year. Exposure, management response, and demonstrated causation must be kept separate. (philippidis202510xrocheprognosys pages 4-7)

## What changed in 2025–2026?

| Measure | What the evidence shows | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| **FY2025 grant flow** | NIH competing research-project-grant awards fell from **10,265 in FY2024 to 8,161 in FY2025** (−20.5%); their success rate fell from **19% to 13%**. NSF competitive awards fell from **10,591 to 8,377**, while its funding rate fell from **26% to 19%**. | Fewer *new* projects received awards, despite NIH university award dollars of approximately **$26.7 billion** and **$26.8 billion** in the two years. Continuing awards can mask a weaker new-project pipeline. These comparisons come from an analysis of agency award data, not a direct measure of lab-equipment sales. (koomullilUnknownyearwhofundsuniversity pages 24-26) |
| **FY2026 presidential request versus enacted funding** | The administration sought roughly a **55% NSF cut** and **40% NIH cut**. Congress instead provided approximately **$8.8 billion for NSF**, **$48 billion for NIH**, and **$8.2 billion for DOE’s Office of Science**—broadly near prior-year levels. DOE’s Office of Science request had been **$7.092 billion versus $8.240 billion in FY2025** (−14%). | The proposed across-the-board NIH and NSF cuts **were not enacted**. Nor was every DOE program protected: FY2026 funding for its Energy Efficiency and Renewable Energy office fell **44%** to **$1.950 billion**. A request, an appropriation, and the pace of actual grant awards are different measures. (fineberg2026howtorevive pages 1-2, fineberg2026howtorevive pages 2-4, hall2026thelastingeffects pages 126-129, lee2026stateofgovernment pages 39-40) |
| **15% indirect-cost policies** | NIH, NSF, DOE, and DOD issued policies seeking **15%** university indirect-cost rates in 2025, instead of institution-specific negotiated rates commonly around **30–70%**. The NIH policy encountered a **permanent injunction in April 2025**; by January 2026, the Congressional Research Service reported congressional prohibitions on implementing the agency policies, including FY2026 protections for negotiated NSF and DOE rates. | **Do not count the proposed cap as an implemented reduction in every university’s revenue.** An FY2024-award-based simulation estimated that, *had the NIH cap operated*, universities would have lost about **$5.24 billion** in annual funding—**$2.99 billion public** and **$2.25 billion private**. Another study estimated that at least a dozen institutions would each lose over **$100 million annually** under its stated scenario. (gallo2026universitiesandindirect pages 1-4, ford2025thefinancialimpact pages 1-2, azoulay2026indirectcostrecovery pages 4-7) |

Even where proposed cuts were defeated, **disruption occurred**: a study identified **405 NIH predoctoral F31 awards affected by 2025 terminations**, while noting subsequent freezes, appeals, and reinstatements. That distinction matters when interpreting a termination count as lasting lost spending. Flat nominal FY2026 appropriations likewise do not erase delayed awards, altered priorities, or the difficulty of planning purchases and retaining staff during uncertainty. (jaramillo2026patternsandimplications pages 1-2, koomullilUnknownyearwhofundsuniversity pages 24-26, fineberg2026howtorevive pages 1-2)

**Bottom line.** Government funding anchors a majority of US university research expenditure and, through grants and overhead, indirectly anchors much of the labor, infrastructure, and equipment demand around academic labs. Open resources can be particularly fragile because their users need continuous service while maintainers often have temporary funding; the observed outcomes include lost expertise, curtailed access, migrations, and closures. For suppliers, the documented 10x example shows material exposure but not a universal industry percentage. The 2025 award slowdown and interruptions show how volatility can reach labs and their vendors **before—or despite—the rejection of major proposed FY2026 budget cuts**. (gallo2026universitiesandindirect pages 4-5, yehudi2309individualcontextfreeonline pages 32-35, strecker2023disappearingrepositoriestaking pages 10-11, philippidis202510xrocheprognosys pages 4-7, koomullilUnknownyearwhofundsuniversity pages 24-26, fineberg2026howtorevive pages 1-2)
