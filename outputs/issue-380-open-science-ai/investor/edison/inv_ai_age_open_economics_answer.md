# Generative AI and the economics of open infrastructure

**Bottom line.** Generative AI raises the usefulness of open code, data and scientific literature, but it can separate *use of an open resource* from *visits, contributions, revenue and feedback received by its producer*. At the same time, automated collection and submission can increase hosting and review costs. These are shifts in bargaining power and unit economics, not evidence that openness has stopped creating value—or that every AI-assisted contribution is harmful. (li2023starcodermaythe pages 1-2, lozano2025biomedicaanopen pages 1-2, riochanona2024largelanguagemodels pages 1-3, noroozian2025generativeaiand pages 1-3, song2026theimpactof pages 1-4)

## Where the value goes

**Open code becomes a model input.** BigCode assembled *The Stack* from approximately 3.1 TB of permissively licensed source code and used it to train the publicly released StarCoder family; StarCoderBase was trained on one trillion tokens from GitHub repositories. The project provided inspection, opt-out and attribution tools, illustrating a reciprocal model. Commercial coding assistants also draw on public-code ecosystems, but these studies do **not** establish how much of any assistant’s revenue is attributable to particular repositories or how much reaches their maintainers. A platform controlling the assistant, customer relationship and distribution can monetize capabilities derived partly from code maintained elsewhere. Public availability of code is not, by itself, evidence that every training or output use complies with its license. (kocetkov2022thestack3 pages 1-3, li2023starcodermaythe pages 1-2)

**Open science supplies inputs—and can receive valuable outputs.** Decades of publicly deposited protein structures and sequence research underpinned DeepMind’s AlphaFold. Google DeepMind and EMBL–EBI subsequently made more than 214 million predicted structures accessible through the AlphaFold database, which DeepMind funds: this is a substantial return to the research commons, not an example of a demonstrated AlphaFold subscription business. In biomedicine, the BIOMEDICA researchers converted over six million articles in PubMed Central’s open-access subset into more than 24 million image–caption pairs, then released a dataset, code and trained models. These examples show that the organization building an AI layer may gain model expertise, distribution or downstream application opportunities, while scientists and the public can also gain genuinely open assets; neither example quantifies a transfer of profit away from the original data stewards. (varadi2024alphafoldproteinstructure pages 1-2, varadi2024alphafoldproteinstructure pages 6-7, lozano2025biomedicaanopen pages 1-2)

That distinction matters economically: the costs of producing, validating, updating and serving source data remain with laboratories, repositories, publishers and contributors even when a downstream model makes the information easier to consume elsewhere. An analysis of reported AI training-data transactions found several pricing arrangements but that most documented deals excluded original creators from compensation; its deal sample should not be interpreted as a measured revenue share for *all* open-science or open-source producers. (oderinwale2510theeconomicsof pages 1-4)

## Demand, participation and operating costs

**AI answers can displace the visit and the public question.** A difference-in-differences study estimated that Stack Overflow’s weekly questions-and-answers fell about **16%** relative to comparison forums after ChatGPT launched, reaching roughly **25% within six months**. Votes on remaining posts did not materially improve, so the evidence does not support assuming that only trivial or low-quality contributions disappeared. A separate study found declines in Stack Overflow visits and questions but no comparable participation decline in the Reddit developer communities examined; it also found greater exit among newer Stack Overflow users. The loss at issue is not just a page view: a privately resolved question no longer automatically becomes a searchable answer, contributor interaction or fresh training example for the public commons. These are comparative observational studies, not proof that ChatGPT explains every subsequent decline. (riochanona2024largelanguagemodels pages 1-3, burtch2024theconsequencesof pages 5-7, burtch2024theconsequencesof pages 1-2)

Evidence for **documentation traffic specifically is narrower**. In a single-institution study of University of Ottawa library research guides—a form of open research documentation—**175 of 210** guides had fewer views across matched pre- and post-ChatGPT periods, with a **median change of −38.35%**. This is a before/after association, not a causal estimate for ChatGPT or a measured decline for software documentation generally. A separate study of requests to a developer-documentation endpoint shows why visits may also become a worse measure of consumption: AI agents can retrieve material in one or two requests instead of a human’s multi-page session. Thus an investor should examine human visits, machine retrieval and resulting product use separately rather than infer them from a single traffic chart. (lee2026changingpatternsof pages 1-3, lee2026changingpatternsof pages 3-5, borysenko2604developerexperiencewith pages 1-2)

**“Free to read” is not free to serve.** Citing its April 2025 operating report, researchers record Wikimedia’s finding that crawlers generated **65% of its most expensive traffic**, while bandwidth for multimedia downloads had grown **50% since January 2024**. The 65% figure refers to the costly traffic category—not 65% of all Wikimedia requests. In a separate, self-selected survey of 43 cultural-heritage institutions, 39 reported increased traffic and 27 of those attributed some or all of it to bots. Some operators reported disruptive bursts, but bot identity and purpose can be difficult to verify, and institutions experiencing trouble were more likely to respond. The investor-relevant exposure is variable bandwidth, capacity, bot mitigation and degraded service for human users, even where content remains openly licensed. (noroozian2025generativeaiand pages 1-3, weinberg2025areaibots pages 1-6, weinberg2025areaibots pages 14-18)

## Code production is cheaper; trusted integration is not automatically cheaper

Studies of Copilot find real contribution gains alongside extra coordination. One project-level analysis using GitHub usage data estimated **5.9% more OSS code contributions**, **3.4% more participating developers** and **8% more coordination time**; timely merges still increased overall. A Python-versus-R comparison found more package updates and disproportionately more maintenance-oriented than new-code contributions. In another study of Microsoft-owned open-source projects, peripheral contributors submitted more commits and pull requests while core contributors did more reviewing and fewer of their own commits. Designs, projects and outcome definitions differ, so these estimates should not be combined into a universal productivity rate. (song2026theimpactof pages 1-4, song2026theimpactof pages 4-7, yeverechyahu2024theimpactof pages 4-7, xu2025aiassistedprogrammingdecreases pages 3-5)

A randomized test provides an important counterweight: **16 experienced open-source developers**, assigned **246 tasks** in projects they knew well, took **19% longer** when permitted early-2025 AI tools. This does not negate the contribution gains above: task-completion time, code volume and the amount successfully integrated are different outcomes, and tools and users vary. Nor is *all* agent-generated work slop: a study of **567 Claude Code-assisted pull requests** across **157 projects** found **83.8%** were merged, although **45.1% of merged PRs** required further changes. That sample concerns identifiable agent-assisted PRs, not unsolicited submissions across the entire ecosystem. (becker2507measuringtheimpact pages 1-3, watanabe2026ontheuse pages 1-3)

**The risk is an asymmetry between cheap submission and scarce human judgment.** In an analysis of **294 active, popular repositories**, 2025 PR volume was **6.8% above** its modeled counterfactual and the one-time-contributor merge ratio was **18.18% below** it. The researchers did not observe AI use for each PR: concurrent ecosystem changes could contribute to either trend, and their issue-related results had weaker placebo-test support. A more targeted study identified **1,786 PRs explicitly flagged as AI slop by maintainers** across **76 affected repositories**; **11** were merged. That is evidence of a costly rejection queue, *not* evidence that only 11 of every 1,786 AI-assisted PRs are useful. Labels and maintainer judgments determine entry into this selected sample, and two repositories accounted for a large share. (afroz2026"aislopis pages 5-6, afroz2026"aislopis pages 8-9, afroz2026"aislopis media 37156ac1, raskin2026anempiricalstudy pages 3-4, raskin2026anempiricalstudy pages 5-6)

Bug reports show why blanket rejection has costs too. After low-quality vulnerability submissions drove curl’s confirmed rate below **5%**, it ended its bug-bounty program; later reporting put its confirmation rate near **16%** by April 2026. Across **120,918 reports** in **101 projects**, a 2026 study found AI-assisted reproduction and diagnosis associated with higher validity, but AI-generated reproducers were rejected more often than other reproducers (**23.53% versus 5.14%**). Maintainers therefore need reproducible evidence and triage capacity for both false alarms *and* genuine additional findings. (afroz2026"aislopis pages 1-2, liu2026copilotorculprit? pages 1-2, liu2026copilotorculprit? pages 2-3)

## Does AI make an open product easier to copy?

**Plausibly yes for implementation; not necessarily for a dependable replacement.** Agents can accelerate writing an alternative interface or reproducing observed functionality, while existing open components lower the initial build cost. A 2026 analysis found **575 screened candidate** open-source alternatives to twelve commercial products and rising rates of repositories describing themselves as alternatives. It explicitly cautions that some functional matches were unverified, that a rapidly built suite of applications had unverified completeness and reliability, and that repositories do **not** establish customer switching or lost subscription revenue. A reported organizational survey found that **32%** of respondents said their organization had forgone at least one software purchase or feature because it could build with agents; spending displaced and the quality of those builds were not measured. Verification, integrations, security, service and continued maintenance remain part of the make-or-buy calculation. Legal analysis likewise argues that AI is reducing the effort of reverse engineering, but that is not a measured rate of successful commercial cloning. (malo2026thecustomeris pages 1-3, malo2026thecustomeris pages 15-16, malo2026thecustomeris pages 18-21, mandel2026cheapaiassistedreverse pages 1-3)

## Implications for investors

1. **Underwrite the whole value chain, not repository popularity.** Identify who pays for curation and maintenance; who controls AI-facing discovery, customer distribution and usage data; and whether the producer receives fees, reciprocal contributions or other demonstrable returns. Open assets can create enormous public benefit without creating exclusive pricing power for their host. (li2023starcodermaythe pages 1-2, lozano2025biomedicaanopen pages 1-2, riochanona2024largelanguagemodels pages 1-3, oderinwale2510theeconomicsof pages 1-4)
2. **Stress-test demand and cost simultaneously.** Track human documentation and community participation separately from crawler/API requests; budget for bandwidth, caching, bulk access, rate limits, triage and maintainer retention. A growing number of AI fetches should not be booked as growing human adoption, and falling web visits need not mean the underlying data has become less useful. (lee2026changingpatternsof pages 1-3, borysenko2604developerexperiencewith pages 1-2, noroozian2025generativeaiand pages 1-3, weinberg2025areaibots pages 14-18)
3. **Test the defensibility of the *service*, not the difficulty of writing its code.** Vulnerability is greater where customers can substitute a narrow, easy-to-test feature. The investment case is stronger where value depends on continually refreshed, quality-controlled data, expert validation, integration into workflows, reliability, compliance, support or an enduring contributor network. This is an investment inference from the observed build-versus-buy limits, not a demonstrated ranking of companies. (malo2026thecustomeris pages 15-16, malo2026thecustomeris pages 18-21, becker2507measuringtheimpact pages 1-3, liu2026copilotorculprit? pages 2-3)
4. **Measure net, quality-adjusted output.** Require evidence on accepted contributions, time to review and deploy, defects, customer retention and maintenance cost—not generated lines of code or proposed PRs alone. Fund contribution gates and stewardship without indiscriminately closing newcomer pathways; both the productive agent-PR sample and the slop sample show why selection matters. (song2026theimpactof pages 1-4, becker2507measuringtheimpact pages 1-3, watanabe2026ontheuse pages 1-3, raskin2026anempiricalstudy pages 5-6, afroz2026"aislopis pages 8-9)

The resulting investment thesis is conditional: AI can expand the reach of open knowledge and reduce some production costs, while weakening traffic-based monetization and making unfunded hosting and verification less sustainable. The durable advantage is likely to lie in **trusted, continuously operated complements to openness**, rather than assuming that open code or open data alone guarantees either revenue capture or protection from imitation. (lozano2025biomedicaanopen pages 1-2, song2026theimpactof pages 1-4, noroozian2025generativeaiand pages 1-3, malo2026thecustomeris pages 15-16)
