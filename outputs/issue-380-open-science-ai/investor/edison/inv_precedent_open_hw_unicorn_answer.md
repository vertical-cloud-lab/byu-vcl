Question: Has any company whose main products are open-source hardware (complete design files published under an open-source hardware licence) reached a valuation or an exit of US$1 billion or more?

Yes. ## Conclusion

**Yes—Opentrons is a documented example, with a qualification about the completeness of the published designs.** Its principal products are laboratory liquid-handling robots. A 2024 study, drawing its company valuations from CB Insights, lists Opentrons at **US$1.8 billion** and identifies robotics as its main business. This is a reported *private-company valuation*, not an acquisition price or demonstrated cash exit. (thielscher2024unicornsinoncology pages 1-4, thielscher2024unicornsinoncology pages 7-10, thielscher2024unicornsinoncology pages 10-12)

The hardware evidence is stronger than a claim that its *software* alone is open: researchers describe the **OT-2 robot’s code and hardware designs as publicly available** and cite an Opentrons publication titled *OT2 Robot Hardware Files* (2021). Other scholarly accounts describe Opentrons as releasing its hardware and software sources. (benedict2022cadweshare? pages 10-13, martin2022methodforinterfacing pages 1-3, martin2022methodforinterfacing pages 16-17)

**Strict-definition caveat:** The retrieved evidence does not itself identify the licence attached to *every* OT-2 design file, audit whether the published files cover the *complete* manufacture-ready robot, or establish equivalent disclosure for every subsequent Opentrons model. The Creative Commons licence reported in one OT-2 research paper applies to the researchers’ **plate-reader accessory**, not demonstrably to Opentrons’ robot. Thus, Opentrons is a compelling **reported billion-dollar open-hardware company**, but the available documentation does **not conclusively verify** the question’s stricter requirement of *complete design files published under an open-source hardware licence* for its main products. (florian2020principlesofcomputercontrolled pages 6-8, benedict2022cadweshare? pages 10-13, martin2022methodforinterfacing pages 1-3)

This distinction matters for other frequently suggested examples. **Raspberry Pi** is explicitly distinguished in the literature from Arduino: the Arduino board is open-source hardware, whereas the Raspberry Pi board is not. **MakerBot** was acquired for a reported **US$403 million** in 2013—below the threshold—and had moved to a proprietary printer licence in 2012. **Prusa Research** published manufacturing data for some earlier printers, but a later examination reports withheld manufacturing data for several newer models and commercial-use restrictions on its CORE One CAD licence; no billion-dollar valuation for Prusa was established here. (wenzel2023openhardwarefrom pages 13-17, pearce2017emergingbusinessmodels pages 8-9, viseur20223dprinterbuilders’ pages 3-4, guhathakurta2026prusa3dsopensourcemanufacturing pages 1-4, guhathakurta2026prusa3dsopensourcemanufacturing pages 4-7)

**Bottom line:** If independently documented publication of *every necessary hardware design file and its qualifying licence* is indispensable, I cannot certify an unambiguous **yes** from the sources retrieved. If published robot hardware designs and scholarly characterisation as open hardware meet the intended standard, **Opentrons’ reported US$1.8 billion valuation answers yes**. (thielscher2024unicornsinoncology pages 7-10, benedict2022cadweshare? pages 10-13, martin2022methodforinterfacing pages 1-3)
