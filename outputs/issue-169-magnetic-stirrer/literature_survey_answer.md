## Survey of Open-Source Hardware Magnetic Stirrers for Laboratory and Self-Driving-Lab Use

### 1. Introduction

Open-source magnetic stirrers have emerged as critical components in low-cost laboratory automation, continuous-culture bioreactors, and self-driving laboratory (SDL) platforms. This survey covers designs reported in peer-reviewed journals and preprints, spanning standalone stirrers, stirring subsystems embedded in multi-vial bioreactors (eVOLVER, Chi.Bio, EVE, Pioreactor, Flexostat, OpenEvo, OptoPACE), and specialized analytical stirring devices. The designs fall into three broad categories: (i) PC cooling-fan or brushless-motor drives with permanent magnets on the hub, (ii) small DC motors (including repurposed CD-player or hard-drive motors) with attached magnets, and (iii) motorless rotating-field (coil) concepts. The following sections detail each design's specifications, and the comprehensive comparison table below consolidates findings across all surveyed platforms.

### 2. Fan-Based and Brushless-Motor Stirrers in Bioreactor Platforms

**eVOLVER.** The eVOLVER platform uses computer cooling fans with permanent magnets mounted on the fan hub to drive conventional magnetic stir bars inside 20–40 mL glass culture vessels (wong2018ageneralizableexperimental pages 18-20). The system provides a software-selectable stirring range of 0–1500 rpm and uses separate 5 V and 12 V power supplies, with a specific startup sequence (5 V first, then 12 V) (heins2019designingautomatedhighthroughput pages 3-5). Each culture vial is housed in a modular "Smart Sleeve" containing a machined aluminum temperature-control tube, a PCB with sensors and actuators, and an electronics module with Arduino microcontrollers (wong2018ageneralizableexperimental pages 1-3). Stirring is disabled before optical-density measurements to allow liquid to settle and reduce noise, but no explicit tachometer-based closed-loop RPM feedback, decoupling threshold, or sample-heating characterization has been reported in the surveyed publications.

**Chi.Bio.** Chi.Bio uses an off-the-shelf fan positioned beneath a heat plate to magnetically drive standard laboratory stir bars inside 30 mL flat-bottom screw-top test tubes with a 12–25 mL working volume (steel2020insitucharacterisation pages 2-4, steel2019chi.bioanopensource pages 2-3, steel2019chi.bioanopensource pages 1-2). The stirring rate is software-adjustable and can produce a powerful vortex at high settings. The complete reactor costs approximately US$300, built from custom PCBs and off-the-shelf components (steel2020insitucharacterisation pages 1-2). Fan model, magnet arrangement, and explicit RPM range are not specified in the main publication but are referenced in supplementary hardware documentation.

**EVE (EVolutionary biorEactor).** EVE uses fans fitted with magnets that rotate magnetic stir bars in small glass vials containing approximately 12 mL of medium (gopalakrishnan2022alowcostopensource pages 3-3, gopalakrishnan2022alowcostopensource pages 1-3). Reported operating speed is approximately 225 rpm, controlled by a custom PCB interfaced with a Raspberry Pi. The total system cost is US$115–200 for triplicate experiments plus a negative control, excluding the incubator, glassware, and 3D printer; assembled PCBs cost about US$42 each including the power supply (gopalakrishnan2022alowcostopensource pages 3-5). Culture-unit stands are 3D-printed. Small differences in 3D-printed holder geometry were found to alter diode positioning and introduce optical-density measurement variability, and fluid pumps require individual calibration to prevent accidental overflow (gopalakrishnan2022alowcostopensource pages 7-8).

**Liu–Yang Morbidostat.** This low-cost morbidostat uses a cooling fan with a permanent magnet glued directly to the fan shaft (liu2016designanduse pages 1-2). The fan is initially powered at 5 V, and stirring speed is precisely adjusted using pulse-width modulation (PWM). The authors emphasize that reliable stirring requires careful alignment of the fan shaft with the culture vial and that the spacing between the vial and the magnet is critical for stable operation (liu2016designanduse pages 6-7). Biofilm formation within 2–3 days necessitates daily vial replacement.

**Flexostat (Takahashi Turbidostat).** The Flexostat is an open-source, multiplexed turbidostat with eight parallel culture chambers, each with up to 15 mL capacity, integrated OD sensing, and stirring using magnetic stir bars (takahashi2015alowcost pages 2-3, takahashi2015alowcost pages 1-2). The complete eight-chamber system can be built for under US$2,000 using hand tools, standard lab equipment, inexpensive fabrication services, and 3D printing. Modular chamber electronics allow distributed sensing and mixing control. Detailed stirring motor, magnet, and RPM specifications are not provided in the main publication text.

**Pioreactor.** The Pioreactor is a small, customizable, 3D-printed bioreactor with a 15 mL working volume, controlled via a Raspberry Pi web interface (pageroth2024pioreactorsatsea pages 6-10). Controllable magnetic stirring uses a small sterile stir bar, with reported experiments operating at 500 rpm (pageroth2024pioreactorsatsea pages 10-15) and up to 1100 rpm in other studies. The motor type, magnet configuration, and validated RPM range are version-dependent and not fully specified in the surveyed publications.

**OpenEvo.** OpenEvo is a fully open-source, low-cost (~US$300) turbidostat platform for automated continuous culture and directed evolution. Its stirring system uses magnets attached to a fan that spins a stir bar inside growth vials. The platform includes optional PC-based remote access, programmable LED stimulation, and data visualization.

**OptoPACE Bioreactor.** The OptoPACE uses two small DC motors originally designed for CD players, each with an attached permanent magnet, to drive dual magnetic stir bars in turbidostat and lagoon vessels (crane2026theoptopacebioreactorb pages 3-9, crane2026theoptopacebioreactor pages 3-9). Both motors are controlled by a common circuit: an Arduino Uno sends PWM signals on pin 5 to trigger a MOSFET, with the motors powered from a 5 V rail provided by a Mean Well AC/DC converter that also supplies a 12 V rail for the peristaltic pump (crane2026theoptopacebioreactora pages 3-9). The electronics are housed in a custom 3D-printed ABS enclosure (165.1 × 114.5 × 44.45 mm) (crane2026theoptopacebioreactorb pages 9-12). The laser for OD measurement is positioned just above the stir bar to avoid interference from bubbles and vortexing.

### 3. Repurposed Hard-Drive Motor Stirrers

**OpenTCC.** The OpenTCC temperature-control chamber integrates magnetic stirrers made from old hard drives, using the existing brushless DC (BLDC) spindle motors and their drive electronics (sanchez2020opentccanopen pages 2-4). The system is Arduino-controlled and costs approximately €400 overall (electronics BOM ~€315), but the stirrer-specific cost and detailed specifications (magnet dimensions, RPM range, speed control) are not separately documented.

### 4. Standalone Analytical Stirring Devices

**Markovitch UPLC Continuous-Stirring Device.** This battery-operated, 3D-printed device replaces the standard UPLC autosampler holder and uses a standard DC motor to rotate a plate carrying magnets (25 × 8 × 1 mm) beneath standard UPLC vials with conventional stir bars (markovitch2020automateddevicefor pages 1-3, markovitch2020automateddevicefor pages 3-4). It provides a selectable stirring range of 200–1200 rpm in 100-rpm increments. Speed control employs closed-loop feedback: a magnetic sensor provides actual rotation rate to an Arduino Pro Mini (3.3 V, 8 MHz), achieving 1–2% deviation from setpoint. An error is flagged when measured speed deviates by more than 10% from the set value for over 60 seconds (markovitch2020automateddevicefor pages 1-3). Battery charge typically declines to ~35% after approximately 72 hours of continuous stirring (markovitch2020automateddevicefor pages 3-4). The magnetic field weakens away from the plate center, reducing stirring reproducibility at peripheral positions.

### 5. Lab-in-Syringe Stirring Systems

The Lab-in-Syringe technique uses NdFeB disk magnets stacked on a motor hub to drive commercial stirring bars inside analytical syringes (horstkotte2020theautomationtechnique pages 13-15). For low-speed applications, old VCR motors suffice, while brushless computer-fan motors with PWM control are recommended for faster stirring due to better speed stability, starting torque at low speed, and longer lifetimes than inexpensive hobby motors (horstkotte2020theautomationtechnique pages 13-15). An opposing-magnet stir-driver design can reach up to 3000 rpm with synchronous rotation, using NdFeB magnets to focus the field and levitate the stir bar to minimize friction (horstkotte2020theautomationtechnique pages 15-16). Reported failure modes include tumbling and bouncing of the stir bar (mitigated by using short bars or stirring crosses), retention of the external magnet assembly by the internal stir bar at startup (requiring slow initial acceleration), and degradation of rubber-ring drive bands in laboratory air (horstkotte2020theautomationtechnique pages 15-16, horstkotte2020theautomationtechnique pages 9-13).

### 6. LEGO-Based SDL Stirrer

**BrickSDLab.** This self-driving lab platform uses a LEGO MINDSTORMS NXT motor coupled through multiple gear stages to rotate a small LEGO magnet, which drives a 15 mm QWORK PTFE (Type B) magnetic stir bar in a 50 mL borosilicate glass beaker (boser2025bricksdlabalowcost pages 15-19, boser2025bricksdlabalowcost pages 39-42). Maximum stirring speed is up to 700 rpm, with an integrated LEGO tachometer for monitoring rotation. The system is programmed using LEGO MINDSTORMS Education NXT software. No 3D printing is required, as the entire mechanism is built from LEGO components.

### 7. Motorless Rotating-Field (Coil) Designs

Motorless electromagnetic stirrers using rotating magnetic fields (RMF) generated by phased coils have been described primarily in the metallurgical and microbioreactor literature. Tan et al. (2015, *J. Chem. Technol. Biotechnol.* 90, 1927–1936) described electromagnetic stirring in a microbioreactor with non-conventional chamber morphology and multiplexed mixing, using coils rather than rotating permanent magnets. Bicakci et al. (2024, *Eng. Sci. Technol. Int. J.* 58, 101850) presented a new RMF stirrer design to reduce mixing time, and related work explored FPGA-based fuzzy logic control for energy-efficient electromagnetic stirring. However, these papers were not obtainable for full-text analysis in this survey, and open-source implementations of coil-based laboratory stirrers remain rare compared to motor-and-magnet designs.

### 9. Cross-Cutting Observations

**Motor and magnet selection.** The dominant design pattern across bioreactor platforms (eVOLVER, Chi.Bio, EVE, Liu morbidostat, OpenEvo) is a PC cooling fan with permanent magnets (typically neodymium) attached to the fan hub, driving a conventional PTFE-coated stir bar through magnetic coupling (gopalakrishnan2022alowcostopensource pages 3-3, liu2016designanduse pages 1-2, horstkotte2020theautomationtechnique pages 13-15). The OptoPACE uses repurposed CD-player DC motors instead (crane2026theoptopacebioreactorb pages 3-9), and the OpenTCC uses reclaimed hard-drive BLDC spindle motors (sanchez2020opentccanopen pages 2-4).

**Speed control.** PWM is the most common speed-control method, explicitly reported for the Liu morbidostat (liu2016designanduse pages 1-2), OptoPACE (crane2026theoptopacebioreactora pages 3-9), and recommended for Lab-in-Syringe fan motors (horstkotte2020theautomationtechnique pages 13-15). Closed-loop RPM feedback using a magnetic sensor is documented only for the Markovitch UPLC device (markovitch2020automateddevicefor pages 3-4). Tachometer-based monitoring (without confirmed closed-loop control) is reported for BrickSDLab (boser2025bricksdlabalowcost pages 15-19). Most bioreactor platforms command stirring via open-loop software settings without explicit RPM verification.

**Key failure modes.** The most commonly reported failure modes are: (1) stir-bar decoupling or failure to start, driven by insufficient magnetic coupling or improper magnet-to-vial spacing (liu2016designanduse pages 6-7, liu2016designanduse pages 1-2); (2) tumbling, bouncing, or erratic bar motion at low speeds or with long stir bars (horstkotte2020theautomationtechnique pages 15-16, horstkotte2020theautomationtechnique pages 13-15); (3) interference with optical measurements due to vortexing, bubbles, or moving cells, requiring stirring to be paused during readings (steel2020insitucharacterisation pages 2-4); (4) biofilm formation necessitating frequent vial replacement (liu2016designanduse pages 6-7); and (5) battery depletion in portable devices (markovitch2020automateddevicefor pages 3-4). Notably, quantitative characterization of sample heating from the stirrer motor or magnets, vibration transmission, or maximum decoupling speed is largely absent from the surveyed literature.

**Cost.** System costs range from approximately US$115–200 for the EVE educational morbidostat (gopalakrishnan2022alowcostopensource pages 3-5) and ~US$300 for Chi.Bio (steel2020insitucharacterisation pages 1-2), Pioreactor, and OpenEvo, to under US$2,000 for the eight-chamber Flexostat (takahashi2015alowcost pages 1-2). Stirrer-specific costs are rarely itemized separately from the complete platform.

**Fabrication.** Nearly all designs incorporate 3D-printed structural components (PLA, ABS, or equivalent) for vial holders, enclosures, and mounting brackets, combined with custom PCBs and off-the-shelf electronics (Arduino, Raspberry Pi). The BrickSDLab is unique in requiring no 3D printing, relying entirely on LEGO components (boser2025bricksdlabalowcost pages 15-19).

### 10. Conclusions and Gaps

This survey reveals that while open-source magnetic stirrers are widely deployed in laboratory automation and SDL platforms, detailed engineering characterization—particularly of magnet-to-stir-bar gap, quantitative decoupling thresholds, sample heating, vibration, and closed-loop RPM control—remains sparse in the published literature. Most designs rely on empirical tuning of fan voltage and magnet-vial spacing rather than systematic optimization. Future work would benefit from standardized reporting of stirrer specifications, validated RPM calibration methods, and explicit characterization of failure envelopes to improve reproducibility across laboratories.
