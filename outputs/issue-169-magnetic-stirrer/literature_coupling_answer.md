# Magnetic stirring in a 20 mL glass vial

**Scope.** The retrieved literature does not establish a universal decoupling speed, maximum torque, or motor-induced temperature rise for the exact combination of a **25-mm-inner-diameter vial and a 10–15-mm PTFE-coated bar**. Published quantitative results below come from different vessel sizes and stirrers; an operating RPM must not be mistaken for a measured slip threshold. (bruyker2011rapidmixingof pages 4-5, alam2013alowcost pages 2-3, isakov2024realtimerheologicalmonitoring pages 3-4)

## 1. Coupling: gap, magnets, and poles

A bar follows the drive while magnetic torque can balance liquid drag, bottom-contact friction, and the torque needed to accelerate the bar. In the usual dipole approximation, its driving torque is approximately **τ = mB sin θ**, where *m* is the bar’s magnetic moment, *B* the rotating field **at the bar**, and *θ* their angular offset. Thus the ideal peak is *mB*; increasing speed or viscosity increases the required offset until synchronous rotation fails. This is a model, not a measured torque specification for the proposed vial. A bioreactor-drive review explicitly notes that magnetic coupling has a field-limited maximum torque **independent of motor power**: adding motor power cannot restore a coupling that has exceeded its torque limit. (schirmer2021anoverviewof pages 5-7, isakov2024realtimerheologicalmonitoring pages 3-4, berret2016localviscoelasticityof pages 3-6)

**Distance and size.** Increasing the magnet-to-bar separation generally weakens the usable rotating field and therefore its torque reserve; the separation includes the vial bottom, platform, and any mounting clearance. Magnet grade and size matter through the field they deliver *at that separation*, rather than through their advertised surface field alone. No retrieved controlled experiment establishes a percentage torque loss per millimetre, or a ranking of N-grade/diameter combinations, for the specified vial. As a *geometry example*, De Bruyker and colleagues used an axially magnetized neodymium cylinder, approximately **1.59 mm in diameter × 9.53 mm thick**, specified at **6,570 G at its surface**, positioned **1–2 mm below** a drop array. They centered the rotation axis under their microscopic stir bar because misalignment caused undesirable sideways bar motion; their geometry cannot supply a torque or permissible gap for a conventional vial. (bruyker2011rapidmixingof pages 4-5, schirmer2021anoverviewof pages 5-7)

**Pole arrangement.** A drive must present a field that rotates *across* the bar—typically a rotating horizontal N–S pair or an appropriately oriented diametrically magnetized drive. A symmetric arrangement whose horizontal field largely cancels at the bar can retain attractive force but furnish poor useful turning torque; consequently, pole orientation and spacing must be evaluated at the bar’s position. In a related instrumented-bar experiment, **two cylindrical N52 NdFeB magnets with opposite field orientations** were incorporated *in the bar*; their fields cancelled at its central magnetometer. This documents one working arrangement, **not** a controlled comparison of one versus two drive magnets or proof that N52 maximizes vial-scale torque. Its magnetic-torque simulation plotted about **12.5 mN·m at 0.5 rad**, whereas modeled viscous torques reached several mN·m. These are plotted values for the **special Smart Stirrer in 150 mL of fluid**, not the peak torque of a 10–15-mm PTFE bar. (isakov2024realtimerheologicalmonitoring pages 3-4, isakov2024realtimerheologicalmonitoring pages 2-3, isakov2024realtimerheologicalmonitoring media 2950c62a)

## 2. Water versus viscous liquids: what speeds are actually reported?

**There is no defensible published pair of “typical decoupling RPMs” for this exact bar and vial** among the retrieved studies. In a 300-µL chamber with a **7 × 2-mm bar**, water was mixed at **100–300 rpm**, and the drive motor could reach approximately **600 rpm**; neither number was reported as the bar’s slip onset. Submicrolitre-drop experiments operated a *micromachined* bar at **600 and 1,500 rpm**, again without measuring a step-out threshold. These disparate examples cannot establish that the proposed vial will or will not remain coupled at 600, 1,000, or 1,500 rpm. (bruyker2011rapidmixingof pages 5-7, alam2013alowcost pages 2-3, alam2013alowcost pages 5-6, isakov2024realtimerheologicalmonitoring pages 3-4)

The useful viscosity comparison is qualitative and conditional. An instrumented Smart Stirrer was operated at **200, 300, and 400 rpm** in glycerol/water and aqueous-polymer solutions in **150 mL**, with beaker inner diameters approximately **65 or 78 mm**. Its magnetic lag and modeled viscous torque increased with viscosity and RPM; stable, uniform rotation became inadequate at about **1 Pa·s (1,000 mPa·s)**. That is a viscosity limit for that apparatus, **not a decoupling RPM at 1 Pa·s and not a limit for the proposed small bar**. Magnetic-wire experiments independently demonstrate a synchronous-to-asynchronous transition whose critical frequency decreases with viscosity, but involve micrometre-scale wires and cannot be used to calculate a vial RPM without the vial’s field and drag parameters. (isakov2024realtimerheologicalmonitoring pages 2-2, isakov2024realtimerheologicalmonitoring pages 3-4, berret2016localviscoelasticityof pages 3-6, isakov2024realtimerheologicalmonitoring pages 5-6)

## 3. Ramping and feedback

**Practical control inference:** start the bar at low speed, then increase the RPM set point gradually and stop below the speed at which the *bar itself* wobbles, skips, or stops following. Ramping reduces the extra torque required for abrupt acceleration; it cannot prevent steady-state slip if viscous drag at the final speed exceeds magnetic torque. A measured example of closed-loop operation used a **Hall sensor reading the rotating drive magnet**, a **PI controller**, and a DC motor in a 300-µL magnetic-stirring reactor. The authors reported approximately **±1 rpm** tracking after settling, about **2 rpm** transient overshoot/undershoot, and approximately **8 s** settling following set-point changes. These are **drive-speed-control** results, not a measured reduction in decoupling events. (alam2013alowcost pages 3-5, alam2013alowcost pages 5-6)

A fan tachometer feeding a PID/PI controller plays the same *drive-side* role: adjust fan command from measured fan RPM rather than relying on open-loop voltage or PWM. It **does not measure whether the bar remains synchronized**. The instrumented Smart Stirrer instead measured bar orientation and angular lag directly, showing the distinction between motor feedback and bar feedback. An anti-decoupling implementation would combine a ramp and a conservative speed ceiling with direct observation or sensing of the bar; on detected slip it should reduce the speed, not command still more RPM. The cited studies do **not** establish a validated PID tuning or safe acceleration rate for a fan-driven 20-mL vial. (alam2013alowcost pages 3-5, isakov2024realtimerheologicalmonitoring pages 3-4, isakov2024realtimerheologicalmonitoring pages 4-5)

## 4. Mixing time versus RPM

In the **300-µL, 12-mm-diameter** PMMA chamber, a water/dye visual-dispersion endpoint fell from approximately **33 s at 100 rpm** to **3 s at 300 rpm** with a **7-mm bar**. In a distinctly different **90-mL, 51-mm-diameter baffled** vessel with a magnetically levitated impeller, measured mixing times were **2.8 ± 0.1 s at 180 rpm** and **0.37 ± 0.1 s at 2,000 rpm**. Neither result predicts a mixing time for an unbaffled 25-mm glass vial. (alam2013alowcost pages 2-3, alam2013alowcost pages 6-8, schneider20233dprintedsingleuse pages 9-13)

More RPM need not provide a proportional gain: for **two 250-nL merged drops**, time to 90% of a FRET mixing signal was **3.0 ± 0.1 s at 600 rpm** versus **3.5 ± 0.4 s at 1,500 rpm**—no significant improvement at the higher speed. This is a microscopic-drop endpoint, not vial homogenization. Accordingly, for the specified vial the relevant test is a tracer added near the surface and observed or sampled for uniformity through the liquid depth, at *verified coupled* bar speeds; vortex appearance alone is not a quantitative mixing-time measurement. (bruyker2011rapidmixingof pages 5-7, bruyker2011rapidmixingof pages 10-14)

## 5. Heating without an intentional heater

A permanent-magnet drive or resistive coil/motor can warm its surroundings through electrical losses, while the mechanical work delivered to the liquid ultimately dissipates as heat. **No retrieved study isolates and quantifies the temperature rise of an unheated 20-mL glass vial attributable to a fan motor versus stirring coils.** Published temperature-control figures should not be misreported as this effect: the 300-µL device intentionally incorporated a heater and reported **±0.15 °C** set-point control and a **0.36 °C s⁻¹** *heater-on* warming rate. Likewise, a **90-mL** culture reaching **43.7 °C** despite a **37 °C** set point occurred during high-density cultivation with insufficient cooling; it does not measure stir-motor heating. (alam2013alowcost pages 3-5, alam2013alowcost pages 5-6, schneider20233dprintedsingleuse pages 13-16)

For scale, **20 mL of water has heat capacity ≈84 J °C⁻¹**. If *a full 1 W actually enters the liquid*, an **adiabatic upper-bound calculation** gives approximately **0.012 °C s⁻¹**, or **0.72 °C min⁻¹**; real warming depends on the unknown fraction of motor/coil power reaching the vial and heat losses to air and the support. This is a heat-balance illustration, **not a literature-measured temperature rise**. For temperature-sensitive work, compare liquid temperature at ambient with the motor/coil on and off, heater disabled, using the same vial, fill volume, and stirring speed; measure the **liquid**, not only the enclosure or plate. (alam2013alowcost pages 3-5, schneider20233dprintedsingleuse pages 13-16)

**Bottom line.** Minimize unnecessary gap, center a rotating horizontal field under the bar, ramp gently, and verify *bar* rotation rather than trusting a fan tachometer. Determine the maximum reliably coupled speed and the tracer mixing time experimentally **in the actual liquid and vial**. The available quantitative studies substantiate these design principles, but do not justify a universal water-versus-viscous slip-RPM specification, an N-grade torque multiplier, or a motor/coil heating figure for this exact setup. (bruyker2011rapidmixingof pages 4-5, schirmer2021anoverviewof pages 5-7, isakov2024realtimerheologicalmonitoring pages 4-5, alam2013alowcost pages 5-6, isakov2024realtimerheologicalmonitoring pages 5-6)
