# Sieving and Automated Powder Handling for Ultrasonic-Atomized Aluminum Alloy Powders: A Literature-Based Assessment

## Q1. Sieving Fundamentals: Bed Depth, Loading, and Overloading for Fine Cohesive Metal Powders

Standard sieve analysis of metal powders (ISO 4497, ASTM B214) typically uses 100–300 g charges on a vibrating or tapping sieve stack operated for 15–20 minutes (neikov2019powdercharacterizationand pages 3-4). However, there is no universal quantitative rule such as "maximum grams per cm² of screen area" that applies across all powders, apertures, and excitation modes. The literature consistently states that capacity depends on screen width (which governs bed thickness), screen length or residence time (which governs separation efficiency), aperture size, wire diameter, open area, material properties, moisture, static charge, and particle shape (sastry1997solidsolidoperationsand pages 22-22, idowu2025designandfabrication pages 2-3). Overloading is identified as a major problem: excessive oversized material can wedge near-mesh particles into apertures, while fine particles electrostatically agglomerate with one another or adhere to larger particles, preventing passage (neikov2019powdercharacterizationand pages 3-4). The concern that "a thick layer won't pass even with tapping or vibration" is justified for fine cohesive aluminum powders. The standard remedy is to feed slowly or in repeated small charges, use a larger screen, apply deblinding aids, or add continuous thin-bed feeding (neikov2019powdercharacterizationand pages 3-4, sastry1997solidsolidoperationsand pages 22-22). For fine dry sieving, Neikov and Yefimov note that most dry sieving techniques are "deficient" below approximately 45 µm (325 mesh), motivating the use of air-jet or ultrasonic methods for cuts approaching 20 µm (neikov2019powdercharacterizationand pages 4-6).

For a 76 mm (3-inch) screen with an active area of approximately 45 cm², a single-layer bed of 50 µm aluminum powder (bulk density approximately 1.3–1.5 g/cm³) corresponds to roughly 3–4 g of powder covering the screen. Keeping the bed at or below this depth maximizes the probability that each particle has an opportunity to encounter an aperture during vibration.

**Ultrasonic deblinding** is the most important advancement for sub-100 µm production sieving. Russell Finex developed this technology using a piezoelectric transducer operating at a resonant frequency of 35,000 Hz, bonded to a velocity transfer plate (VTP) attached to the mesh wires (oconnell2002ultrasonicdeblinding pages 3-4, oconnell2002ultrasonicdeblinding pages 1-3). The ultrasonic frequency breaks down surface tension and renders mesh wires effectively friction-free, preventing particles slightly greater or smaller than the aperture from lodging (oconnell2002ultrasonicdeblinding pages 1-3). Pulsed or modulated operation allows light-density powder to settle and sieve when ultrasound is off, with renewed pulses dispersing any accumulated blinding (oconnell2002ultrasonicdeblinding pages 3-4). Supplier literature reports operation down to 625 mesh (approximately 20 µm) (oconnell2002ultrasonicdeblinding pages 3-4).

Quantitative throughput data from ultrasonic-deblinded production screens include: an iron powder operation that increased from 2,000 to 2,500 kg/h through a 200 µm mesh after fitting ultrasonic deblinding (on large 1,200 mm diameter screens), and glass frit throughput of approximately 400 kg/h through 100 µm mesh (oconnell2002ultrasonicdeblinding pages 1-3). These figures are from large industrial screens and cannot be linearly scaled to a 76 mm laboratory sieve, but they illustrate the magnitude of improvement.

**Air-jet sieving** (Alpine/Hosokawa micromesh jet sieve) works by drawing fine particles through the mesh under vacuum while a rotating blowback nozzle redistributes powder, breaks agglomerates, and clears aperture blockages (neikov2019powdercharacterizationand pages 3-4). This method can sieve down to approximately 10 µm and is described as rapid compared to conventional vibrating-table sieving (neikov2019powdercharacterizationand pages 3-4, neikov2019powdercharacterizationand pages 4-6). It is primarily used for analytical particle-size measurement rather than bulk production sieving.

The **LPBF industry** predominantly uses vibratory screening combined with ultrasonic excitation. The SLM Solutions PSM100 sieve station feeds powder across a vibrating sieve using combined low-frequency and ultrasonic vibration, discharging oversize into a waste bottle (cordova2020effectsofpowder pages 4-6). Published Al-alloy LPBF recycling studies report mesh sizes of 60 µm, 70 µm, 75 µm, 90 µm, and 100 µm, depending on the machine and study (smolina2022influenceofthe pages 4-6, weiss2021investigationtowardsalsi10mg pages 1-3, weiss2021investigationtowardsalsi10mg pages 3-6).

## Q3. Automation Precedents: Sieving, Dosing, and Powder Handling Systems

**LPBF powder recycling stations** represent the most mature automated sieving systems for metal powders. The SLM Solutions PSM100 uses a closed system with combined vibration and ultrasonic sieving, automatic oversize discharge, and powder return to the machine (cordova2020effectsofpowder pages 4-6, smolina2022influenceofthe pages 4-6). Some facilities use double sieving, processing the entire batch first and then re-sieving the oversize fraction (smolina2022influenceofthe pages 4-6, smolina2022influenceofthe pages 2-4).

**Self-driving laboratory powder dispensing** systems include Chemspeed (cartridge-based dosing), Mettler Toledo Quantos (dosing heads), and vision-guided robotic scooping systems (radulov2026visionguidedadaptivescooping pages 1-2, seifrid2022autonomouschemicalexperiments pages 8-9). Common failure modes include blockages in Chemspeed cartridges or Quantos dosing heads from large or abrasive particles, mechanical damage to internal components from abrasive solids, and the need for time-consuming material-specific calibration (radulov2026visionguidedadaptivescooping pages 1-2). Accurate dispensing of small quantities of solids below approximately 20 mg is particularly challenging, and many laboratories avoid direct powder handling by preparing stock solutions (seifrid2022autonomouschemicalexperiments pages 8-9, tom2024selfdrivinglaboratoriesfor pages 4-5). A recent vision-guided adaptive scooping system achieved weighing errors as low as 1.93 mg using closed-loop camera feedback and balance measurements, with failure modes including empty scoops, excessive clump acquisition, and overshooting of target weights (radulov2026visionguidedadaptivescooping pages 3-4, radulov2026visionguidedadaptivescooping pages 6-7).

**No published precedent was found** for a system that combines a vibratory feeder/doser with an inline screen so that dosing and sieving happen in one integrated step. The pharmaceutical literature describes vibratory sieve-chute micro-dosing systems with throughputs in the range of 0.06–24 g/min depending on powder properties and feeder type (hou2024developmentofa pages 44-47, hou2024developmentofa pages 57-61), but these are dispensing devices rather than classification-plus-dosing systems.

## Q4. Using the Existing Auger Doser as a Feeder for a Small Screen

The existing auger doser is well suited to serve as a metered feeder for a 76 mm vibrated or ultrasonically excited 63 µm screen, rather than attempting to incorporate a sieve inside the doser itself. This architecture separates the fragile screen from the mechanical auger and allows the feed rate to be tuned independently of the sieving excitation.

For a 76 mm screen (active area ≈ 45 cm²), maintaining a thin bed requires that the instantaneous screen inventory remain below approximately 3–4 g (corresponding to roughly one particle layer of 50 µm aluminum powder). The rate at which undersize passes through the 63 µm screen depends on excitation intensity and powder cohesion, but for a well-vibrated or ultrasonically deblinded screen, passage of the undersize fraction should occur within seconds. Therefore, feed rates of approximately **1–5 g/min** should keep the screen in the thin-bed regime, allowing most undersize to pass before the next increment arrives. Commercial micro-feeders demonstrate that controlled low-rate feeding in this range is achievable: vibratory feeders range from 0.06–24 g/min, and screw-based micro-feeders operate at 0.03–1.25 g/min depending on powder and settings (hou2024developmentofa pages 44-47, hou2024developmentofa pages 57-61, hou2024developmentofa pages 156-161). For processing a 30 g batch at 2 g/min, sieving would require approximately 15 minutes, which is consistent with standard metal-powder sieve-analysis durations (neikov2019powdercharacterizationand pages 3-4).

## Q5. Alternatives to Dry Sieving for the Lower Cut (<20 µm)

**Air classification** is the principal alternative to dry sieving for removing sub-20 µm fines. The Bahco centrifugal air classifier is a laboratory-scale device that classifies particles in the 5–100 µm range and requires only 5–10 g of sample (neikov2019powdercharacterizationand pages 3-4, neikov2019powdercharacterizationand pages 1-3). Vortex and rotor air classifiers can achieve cut sizes of 20–100 µm but are generally industrial-scale equipment with throughputs of tonnes per hour (shapiro2005airclassificationof pages 6-7). Most air classifiers are poorly suited to the tens-of-grams scale required for this application.

**Is removing <20 µm fines even necessary for ultrasonic-atomized aluminum?** Evidence from ultrasonic atomization of AlSi12 aluminum alloy at 35 kHz shows D10 values ranging from 38.7 to 44.7 µm, D50 from 52.3 to 54.7 µm, and D90 from 66.4 to 72.7 µm (yankin2025effectofultrasonic pages 3-4). The span (D90−D10)/D50 ranges from 0.40 to 0.64, which is substantially narrower than typical gas atomization (wrobel2026enhancingniobiumpowder pages 10-12, scientific2023comparisonofultrasonic pages 4-5). Approximately 75% of the total powder produced was below 63 µm (yankin2025effectofultrasonic pages 2-3). With D10 values consistently above 38 µm, the fraction below 20 µm is expected to be very small—likely negligible. This suggests that for ultrasonic-atomized Al alloys, the No. 635 (20 µm) sieve in the planned stack may be unnecessary, and a single 63 µm upper-cut screen may suffice for the initial scalp. The lab should verify this by performing one sieve analysis with the full No. 60/No. 230/No. 635 stack to quantify the actual sub-20 µm fraction before deciding whether to omit the lower cut permanently.

## Q6. Safety Constraints for Automated Sieving of Aluminum Powder

Aluminum powder in the 2–63 µm range is among the most hazardous combustible metal dusts. Published data report minimum ignition energies (MIE) of 3–13 mJ and minimum explosible concentrations (MEC) of 45–170 g/m³ for this size range (benson2012safetyconsiderationswhen pages 3-5, taveau2018explosionhazardsof pages 10-15). For context, a human body can accumulate approximately 25–30 mJ of electrostatic energy, and a 200-litre drum approximately 40 mJ—both well above the MIE of fine aluminum powder (benson2012safetyconsiderationswhen pages 9-10).

Key safety requirements based on the literature and NFPA 484 include:

1. **Bonding and grounding**: All equipment, containers, scoops, sieves, and frames must be bonded and grounded using clips and flexible grounding leads. Personnel must be grounded. Powder must not be poured or slid across nonconductive surfaces (benson2012safetyconsiderationswhen pages 10-12, benson2012safetyconsiderationswhen pages 9-10).

2. **Ignition source control**: Potential ignition sources include static electricity, mechanically generated sparks, electrical apparatus, stray currents, hot surfaces, and ultrasonics (benson2012safetyconsiderationswhen pages 10-12, taveau2018explosionhazardsof pages 10-15). Solenoids and vibration motors in the doser are potential ignition sources if not properly rated or isolated.

3. **Inert atmosphere**: Processing is ideally conducted in an oxygen-free atmosphere, such as an argon glovebox. If inerting is not feasible, handling should occur within a suitable dust-extraction system designed to keep airborne concentrations below the MEC (benson2012safetyconsiderationswhen pages 9-10, books2003safehandlingof pages 8-10). Inerting requires a substantially enclosed system with oxygen monitoring, adequate gas supply, and automatic shutdown on oxygen exceedance (books2003safehandlingof pages 8-10).

4. **Dust accumulation and housekeeping**: Dust layers must be minimized. Compressed air must never be used for cleaning, as it can disperse deposits and create explosive conditions (benson2012safetyconsiderationswhen pages 2-3). Regular cleaning with conductive, non-sparking tools is required.

5. **3D-printed enclosures**: A standard PLA or PETG enclosure is **not acceptable** for aluminum powder handling. These materials are electrically insulating (promoting static accumulation), combustible, and cannot provide bonding/grounding continuity. An ESD-safe (carbon-filled, conductive) filament would address the static issue but remains combustible. A metal enclosure (aluminum or stainless steel sheet) with proper bonding straps is strongly preferred. Any enclosure design should be reviewed against NFPA 484 requirements as part of a facility-specific dust-hazard analysis (benson2012safetyconsiderationswhen pages 10-12, benson2012safetyconsiderationswhen pages 9-10, myers2008reducingaluminumdust pages 1-2).

6. **Explosion protection**: For enclosed sieving, consider whether explosion relief venting, suppression, or isolation is required per NFPA 68/69. Aluminum dust explosions can produce higher pressures than predicted by standard venting calculations (taveau2018explosionhazardsof pages 15-21). At the tens-of-grams scale, the mass at risk is small, but a dust-hazard analysis (DHA) is still recommended.

The complete 250/63/20 µm stack can be accommodated. Cost is approximately $3k–$10k.

3. **For automation, pursue option (c)**: mount a single conductive 63 µm screen with an ultrasonic deblinding transducer (35 kHz), fed by the existing auger doser at 1–5 g/min. This keeps the bed thin, prevents blinding, and allows Raspberry Pi/Pico control of the complete sieve-then-dose workflow. The sieved powder falls through to a collection cup, which then feeds the existing gravimetric doser. Oversize is retained on the screen for periodic removal. This approach mirrors the SLM Solutions PSM100 operating principle of combined low-frequency vibration plus ultrasonic deblinding (cordova2020effectsofpowder pages 4-6) but at laboratory scale. The key engineering challenges are: (i) tuning the ultrasonic resonance for the small screen, (ii) ensuring all components are bonded, grounded, and meet combustible-dust ignition-source requirements, (iii) detecting screen blinding or overloading via mass-balance monitoring, and (iv) managing powder recovery and minimizing hold-up losses.

4. **A commercial AM sieving station (option d)** such as the PSM100 is not recommended for this application. The tens-of-grams batch size is far below its design capacity, hold-up losses would consume a large fraction of each batch, alloy changeover is burdensome, and capital cost is disproportionate (smolina2022influenceofthe pages 4-6). It becomes appropriate only if the lab scales up to kilogram quantities or integrates with a full LPBF printer.

**Key quantitative guidance summary:**
- Standard metal-powder sieve charges: 100–300 g on standard (200 mm) sieves for 15–20 min (neikov2019powdercharacterizationand pages 3-4); for a 76 mm sieve, proportionally smaller charges of 10–30 g are appropriate.
- Ultrasonic deblinding operates at ~35 kHz; pulsing or modulation is recommended for light aluminum powder (oconnell2002ultrasonicdeblinding pages 3-4).
- Thin-bed feed rate for a 76 mm/63 µm screen: approximately 1–5 g/min (hou2024developmentofa pages 44-47, hou2024developmentofa pages 57-61).
- Aluminum powder MIE: 3–13 mJ (benson2012safetyconsiderationswhen pages 3-5); MEC: 45–170 g/m³ (benson2012safetyconsiderationswhen pages 3-5); all equipment must be bonded and grounded (benson2012safetyconsiderationswhen pages 9-10).
- UA AlSi12 powder D10 = 38.7–44.7 µm, D50 = 52.3–54.7 µm, D90 = 66.4–72.7 µm (yankin2025effectofultrasonic pages 3-4); sub-20 µm content expected to be negligible.
- LPBF Al-alloy recycling uses 60–100 µm screens, most commonly 63–75 µm (smolina2022influenceofthe pages 4-6, weiss2021investigationtowardsalsi10mg pages 1-3, weiss2021investigationtowardsalsi10mg pages 3-6).
