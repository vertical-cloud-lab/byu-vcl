**Fe-rich intermetallics are the leading intrinsic candidate.** In Al–Si alloys, Fe-rich intermetallic phases such as β-Al₅FeSi and α-Al(Fe,Mn)Si appear as the brightest features in BSE/AsB SEM images, appearing bright white against the gray Al matrix (yu2016formationofintermetallic pages 51-56, yu2016formationofintermetallica pages 51-56). This contrast arises because Fe (Z = 26) has roughly double the atomic number of Al (Z = 13), producing a strong backscattered-electron yield difference. In the Al–Si eutectic system studied by Newbury and Ritchie, a bright acicular phase containing ~22.9 wt% Fe and ~0.93 wt% Mn was clearly distinguished from the Al- and Si-rich phases in BSE imaging (newbury2011canxrayspectrum pages 6-8).

**Expected EDS signatures by candidate:**
- **β-Al₅FeSi**: Al + Fe + Si, with Fe:Si atomic ratio near 1:1; no Mn enrichment.
- **α-Al(Fe,Mn)Si**: Al + Fe + Si + Mn (±Cr). Mn is diagnostic for the α form (yu2016formationofintermetallica pages 21-25, yu2016formationofintermetallic pages 21-25).
- **π-Al₈FeMg₃Si₆**: Al + Fe + Mg + Si; Fe enrichment weaker; mean Z ≈ 13.9, so only subtly bright (stella2026processabilityofrecycled pages 74-78).
- **Eutectic/coarsened Si**: Dominant Si peak; Z = 14 vs. 13 for Al, giving only weak compositional BSE contrast (newbury2011canxrayspectrum pages 6-8, newbury2011canxrayspectrum pages 5-6).
- **Mg₂Si**: Mg + Si; mean Z ≈ 12.7, would appear slightly *dark*, not bright.
- **Oxides (Al₂O₃, MgO, MgAl₂O₄)**: Mean Z ≈ 10; would appear *dark* in BSE, not bright. EDS shows Al+O, Mg+O, or Mg+Al+O respectively.
- **Embedded alumina abrasive**: Al + O; dark in BSE (Z ≈ 10), not bright (mackenzie2005analyticalcharacterizationof pages 39-41, geller2002samplepreparationfor pages 6-9).
- **Embedded SiC**: Si + C; dark (Z ≈ 10).
- **Embedded diamond**: C only; very dark (Z = 6).
- **Metallic debris (steel, brass, Zn-plated steel)**: Very bright (Fe Z = 26, Cu Z = 29, Zn Z = 30). EDS would show Fe±Cr±Ni for stainless, Cu+Zn for brass, Zn for plating.

**Fe-rich intermetallics in as-built LPBF AlSi10Mg:** Typical commercial AlSi10Mg powder specifies Fe < 0.55 wt% (fracchia2025newprocessesin pages 71-75), with some specifications allowing up to 2.0 wt% for A360-type alloys (cattano2019feasibilitystudieson pages 93-96). During LPBF solidification, Fe has limited solubility in α-Al and is rejected at the solidification front. Fe-containing species segregate to cell boundaries and especially to melt-pool boundaries, which are the last regions to solidify (martucci2025effectofpbflbm pages 6-8). In LPBF Al-Si-based alloys, TEM-EDX has confirmed Fe, along with Si, Mg, and other transition metals, concentrated in the eutectic network at α-Al cell boundaries (cattano2019feasibilitystudieson pages 143-146, cattano2019feasibilitystudieson pages 146-150). In the heat-affected zone (HAZ) beneath melt-pool boundaries, coarsened Fe-rich acicular phases have been observed (martucci2025effectofpbflbm pages 6-8, martucci2025effectofpbflbm pages 8-10). Rapid solidification refines intermetallics: higher cooling rates produce a higher density of smaller particles (yu2016formationofintermetallic pages 21-25). Direct quantitative reports of discrete 0.3–1.5 µm Fe-rich particles at the specific number density stated (~one per 26 µm²) in standard as-built LPBF AlSi10Mg were not located in the literature searched; most studies characterize Fe as part of the boundary-segregation network or as coarser phases at melt-pool boundaries rather than as isolated particles of the described size. This represents a gap in available evidence.

**Key discriminator:** The observation that the particles are bright in *both* the Everhart-Thornley detector and a dedicated BSE detector strongly favors a compositional (atomic-number) contrast mechanism over topographic artifacts, which would produce asymmetric or reversed contrast between the two detector types. This effectively rules out oxides, embedded abrasives, and other low-Z candidates. The remaining candidates are Fe-rich intermetallics or extraneous metallic contamination (steel debris, saw-blade bond material, brass, etc.). EDS analysis is required to distinguish between these.

### (2) Bright Cellular Network

**The network is highly consistent with the Si-rich eutectic cell boundaries of as-built LPBF AlSi10Mg.** The observed spacing of 0.5–0.8 µm across an 11.8 µm field matches published measurements of primary dendrite arm spacing (PDAS) in as-built LPBF AlSi10Mg: approximately 0.5 ± 0.1 µm at melt-pool centers and 0.9 ± 0.2 µm near melt-pool boundaries (cauwenbergh2021unravellingthemultiscale pages 7-8). Other measurements report cell minor-axis dimensions of approximately 0.45–0.60 µm depending on zone (zhang2024towardsunderstandingformation pages 5-7). The network is the Si-rich eutectic phase, measured by STEM-EDX at approximately 29.6 wt% Si versus approximately 2.7 wt% Si inside the supersaturated α-Al cells (cauwenbergh2021unravellingthemultiscale pages 9-10). The directional alignment across the field of view is consistent with cellular growth aligned with the local thermal gradient within a single melt pool.

**BSE contrast mechanism:** Si (Z = 14) versus Al (Z = 13) provides only ~7.7% atomic number difference. The corresponding BSE contrast has been measured at approximately 0.069 for Al-rich versus Si-rich phases in an Al–Si eutectic alloy—a weak but detectable signal (newbury2011canxrayspectrum pages 6-8, newbury2011canxrayspectrum pages 5-6). This subtle compositional contrast may be augmented by polishing relief from the 1–2 hour napped-cloth diamond polishing step.

**Polishing relief contribution:** Differential removal rates between soft α-Al matrix and harder Si-rich boundaries are well established: softer phases are preferentially removed on napped cloths, leaving harder phases standing in relief (remond2002implicationsofpolishing pages 7-9). This topographic step adds secondary-electron edge contrast that enhances the network visibility in ETD imaging and can also modulate BSE signals via trajectory effects. The extended 1–2 hour vibratory polish on a medium-nap synthetic cloth is likely to produce measurable relief (on the order of tens of nanometers) between α-Al and Si boundary material, amplifying the intrinsic compositional contrast. A short colloidal-silica final polish (which was not used here) or ion polishing would reduce this topographic component. The Qin et al. study used colloidal silica followed by ion milling to achieve flat surfaces for their analysis (qin2020rapidsolidificationand pages 2-3).

### (3) Dark Spots (0.06–0.35 µm, median 0.09 µm)

Because the dark spots appear dark in *both* SE and BSE modes, they must represent either a low-Z material or topographic depressions (pits/pores). The leading candidates are:

**Embedded 0.1 µm polycrystalline diamond (leading candidate):** Diamond embedding in soft metals during metallographic polishing is well documented. Geller and Engle (2002) explicitly noted that indium polished with 0.1 µm diamond appeared shiny and scratch-free but was "full of 0.1 µm diamond" (geller2002samplepreparationfor pages 6-9). MacKenzie (2005) documented diamond embedding specifically in aluminum when diamond slurries are used (mackenzie2005analyticalcharacterizationof pages 39-41). Diamond (Z = 6) would appear very dark in BSE against Al (Z = 13), consistent with the observations. The median size of 0.09 µm matches the 0.1 µm diamond suspension used, and the high density (~1.8 per µm²) is consistent with abrasive embedding over 1–2 hours of vibratory polishing. EDS would show only a carbon signal (difficult to detect reliably at low concentrations in the SEM due to hydrocarbon contamination and carbon coating).

**Nanopores or polishing pits:** Small pores (gas porosity, shrinkage) are common in LPBF AlSi10Mg. However, the very high density (~1.8 per µm²) and the match to the diamond suspension size favor embedding over intrinsic porosity. Polishing-induced micropit formation is possible but would typically be less uniform in size.

**Oxide nanoparticles:** Sub-micron oxygen-rich particles have been reported throughout as-built LPBF AlSi10Mg microstructures, particularly along cell boundaries (qin2020rapidsolidificationand pages 3-5). Such oxides would have lower Z than Al and would appear dark in BSE, consistent with the observations. However, the remarkably tight size distribution centered at 0.09 µm and the high areal density are more consistent with an externally introduced population (diamond) than with intrinsic oxide inclusions.

**Mg₂Si precipitates:** Mean Z ≈ 12.7, so slightly dark. However, Mg₂Si precipitates in as-built LPBF AlSi10Mg are typically either very fine (nanometer-scale) or coarser in the HAZ (~tens of nm), and they would show Mg + Si in EDS.

**Recommended test:** Re-polish a companion sample omitting the diamond step entirely (using only alumina and colloidal silica, or ion-mill a broad area). If the dark-spot population disappears or greatly diminishes, diamond embedding is confirmed. Alternatively, Raman spectroscopy on the polished surface can detect diamond's characteristic 1332 cm⁻¹ peak.

### (4) EDS Conditions for Identifying 0.3–1.5 µm Particles in an Al Matrix

**Interaction volume considerations:** The X-ray generation volume in aluminum is governed by electron range, which depends strongly on beam energy. Using the Kanaya-Okayama approximation and Monte Carlo simulations (CASINO):

- **At 5 kV in Al:** The electron range is approximately 0.25–0.35 µm. This means the X-ray generation volume is comparable to or smaller than a 0.3–1.5 µm particle, providing excellent spatial resolution for particles ≥0.5 µm. The Fe Lα line (0.705 keV) and Si Kα (1.740 keV) are well excited. However, Fe Kα (6.404 keV) cannot be excited at 5 kV—only Fe L-lines are available, which overlap with the tail of the O Kα peak and have lower fluorescence yield (berger2014measurementandmonte pages 3-6, berger2014measurementandmonte pages 1-3, hovington2006casinoanew pages 10-12).

- **At 15 kV in Al:** The electron range is approximately 2–2.5 µm, meaning the interaction volume significantly exceeds even the largest particles. X-rays from both the particle and the surrounding Al matrix will be generated simultaneously. The Fe Kα line is well excited (overvoltage ratio ~2.1), enabling unambiguous Fe identification. However, quantitative analysis of a 0.5 µm particle will be severely compromised by matrix contributions.

**Recommended analytical strategy:**

1. **Low-voltage screening (5–7 kV):** Use the imaging voltage (5 kV) or slightly higher to acquire rapid EDS spectra with minimized matrix overlap. Fe L, Si K, Al K, Mg K, and O K are all accessible. This approach achieves measured quantitative spatial resolution approaching ~200 nm with a FEG source (berger2014measurementandmonte pages 3-6). Sufficient for determining whether a particle contains Fe, Mn, Cr, Si, or other elements at significant concentrations.

2. **Moderate-voltage confirmation (10–15 kV):** Raise voltage to excite Fe Kα for unambiguous Fe identification and to access Mn Kα (5.899 keV) and Cr Kα (5.415 keV). Accept that the interaction volume will exceed the smallest particles; center the beam carefully and compare with a matrix-only spectrum taken nearby. The "peak-minus-matrix" approach can isolate particle contributions.

3. **Spot versus area analysis:** Use point analysis on particle centers, not area/map modes, to maximize signal-to-noise from the particle. Complementary X-ray mapping at 5–7 kV can reveal spatial distribution of Fe, Si, Mn, and O across the microstructure.

4. **Standards:** Ideal standards for quantitative EPMA include pure Fe, pure Si, pure Al, MgO or periclase for Mg, and synthetic Al₂O₃ for O. For routine SEM-EDS, standardless analysis can identify major elements but is unreliable for light elements (O, C) and for quantification of sub-micron particles where ZAF corrections assume a flat, infinitely thick specimen (moro2021semedsnanoanalysisof pages 6-9). Monte Carlo-based corrections (e.g., CASINO, DTSA-II) are recommended for particles smaller than the interaction volume (hovington2006casinoanew pages 10-12).

5. **Carbon detection for diamond:** Detecting embedded diamond requires careful carbon analysis. Carbon is difficult by EDS due to ubiquitous hydrocarbon contamination, phenolic mount outgassing, and the common use of carbon coating. If diamond embedding is suspected, either use an uncoated specimen with low-kV EDS (<3 kV), or employ Raman microspectroscopy (1332 cm⁻¹ diamond peak vs. ~1350/1580 cm⁻¹ graphitic carbon).

### Summary of Evidence Gaps

Several important points could not be fully established from the literature retrieved:

- **Discrete Fe-rich intermetallic particles at the specific size (0.3–1.5 µm) and number density (~one per 26 µm²) in standard as-built LPBF AlSi10Mg** have not been quantitatively documented in the sources found. Most studies describe Fe as part of the continuous boundary segregation network or as coarser phases at melt-pool boundaries. The observed particles could represent sections through Fe-rich boundary precipitates, coarsened Fe-rich phases in HAZ regions, or extraneous metallic contamination.

- **Buehler VibroMet-specific application notes** for AlSi10Mg or similar Al–Si alloys were not retrieved. Manufacturer guidelines for aluminum preparation typically recommend short polishing times and hard cloths to minimize embedding and relief.

- **Quantitative polishing relief measurements** for the specific preparation protocol described (1–2 h napped-cloth diamond, no colloidal silica) on LPBF AlSi10Mg were not found.

The most critical next step is to acquire EDS spectra from representative bright particles at both 5 kV and 15 kV. Detection of Fe (and possibly Mn, Cr) would confirm Fe-rich intermetallics; detection of only Al and Si with no Fe would suggest coarsened eutectic Si with enhanced topographic contrast; detection of Fe+Cr+Ni would suggest stainless-steel contamination; and detection of only strong C would indicate diamond (though this would be dark, not bright). For the dark spots, omitting the diamond polishing step on a replicate sample is the definitive test for embedded diamond.
