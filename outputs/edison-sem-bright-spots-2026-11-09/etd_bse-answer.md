## SEM Imaging of As-Built LPBF AlSi10Mg: ETD Signal Composition, Z-Contrast, Charging, and BSE Coefficients at 5 kV

### (1) BSE-Derived Fraction of the ETD SE-Mode Signal

The Everhart–Thornley detector (ETD), when operated in SE mode with a positively biased Faraday cage (+300 V) and high scintillator potential (+10 kV), collects four distinct signal components: **SE1** (secondary electrons generated by the primary beam within the specimen escape depth), **SE2** (secondary electrons generated by backscattered electrons exiting the specimen surface), **SE3** (secondary electrons generated when BSEs strike the pole piece and chamber walls), and **direct BSE** (high-energy backscattered electrons that reach the scintillator directly) (goldstein2018semimageinterpretation pages 3-5, goldstein2018semimageinterpretation pages 5-7).

Of these, SE2, SE3, and direct BSE are all derived from the backscattering process. The total secondary electron yield at the specimen can be written as:

**δ_total = δ_PE (1 + β·η)**

where δ_PE is the SE yield from primary electrons alone, η is the backscatter coefficient, and β = δ_BSE/δ_PE is the relative efficiency of BSE for producing secondary electrons, typically in the range 3–6 (lin2007astudyof pages 19-25). The fraction of specimen-emitted SEs that are SE2 is therefore f_SE2 = β·η/(1 + β·η).

**SE3** has been reported to constitute approximately **10–50% of the secondary-electron signal** reaching a conventional ETD, depending strongly on working distance, pole-piece geometry, and chamber configuration (zaggout2007quantificationofse pages 50-52). Griffin (2011) demonstrated that the pole piece, with its high effective incidence angle, can produce a dominant SE3 signal at short working distances (griffin2011acomparisonof pages 4-9). Peters (1982a, 1982b) developed an absorption plate placed over the pole piece to suppress SE3, demonstrating experimentally that a large part of the ETD SE signal is BSE-mediated. Direct BSE reaching the scintillator constitute a relatively small fraction of the positively biased ETD signal because the scintillator subtends a limited solid angle; however, the remote BSE that generate SE3 from the full chamber hemisphere represent a much larger effective collection area (goldstein2018semimageinterpretation pages 3-5, goldstein2018semimageinterpretation pages 5-7).

**Effect of a light matrix (Al, Z = 13) and low beam energy (5 kV):**

For aluminum at ~20 kV, η ≈ 0.152 (goldstein2018semimageinterpretation pages 3-5). At 5 kV normal incidence, Monte Carlo calculations give η ≈ 0.171 for Al (kawano2012applicationofthe pages 203-208). With β ≈ 3–6, the product β·η for Al is approximately 0.45–0.90, meaning SE2 constitutes roughly 31–47% of the specimen-emitted SE signal. For iron (Z = 26, η ≈ 0.28 at 20 kV), β·η ≈ 0.84–1.68, and the SE2 fraction rises to 46–63% (lin2007astudyof pages 19-25, goldstein2018semimageinterpretation pages 3-5). Thus, even for a light matrix like Al, the BSE-derived SE fraction is substantial, though smaller than for heavier elements.

The total SE yield itself is Z-dependent through the SE2 term: since η increases monotonically with Z (at energies above ~2 keV), δ_total = δ_PE(1 + β·η) increases with Z via the β·η product (lin2007astudyof pages 19-25). At low beam energies below approximately 2 keV, the backscatter coefficient begins to show anomalous behavior: η decreases for heavy elements (e.g., Au falls from ~0.5 at 10 keV to ~0.2 near 400 eV) and may increase slightly for light elements, potentially causing contrast compression or even reversal in the sub-keV regime (wuhrer2016lowvoltageimaging pages 4-7, fitting2004sixlawsof pages 1-2, kuhr1999monte‐carlosimulationof pages 13-17). At 5 kV, however, the standard monotonic Z-dependence of η is well preserved, and the η values are similar to or slightly higher than their 20 kV counterparts for light elements (wuhrer2016lowvoltageimaging pages 4-7, fitting2004sixlawsof pages 4-5).

### (2) Does an ETD SE-Mode Image Show Z-Contrast on a Flat Polished Specimen?

**Yes.** Goldstein et al. (2018) explicitly demonstrate that the positively biased ETD shows atomic-number (compositional) contrast on a polished cross section of Al-Cu aligned eutectic. The higher-Z phase (CuAl₂, Z̄ ≈ 25) appears brighter than the Al-2%Cu solid solution (Z̄ ≈ 13.2) in both the dedicated semiconductor BSE detector and the ETD (positive bias) (goldstein2018semimageinterpretation pages 3-5). The text states: "although the SE1 signal of the E–T detector does not show predictable variation with composition, the BSE components of the E–T signal reveal the atomic number contrast" (goldstein2018semimageinterpretation pages 3-5).

The dedicated BSE detector **enhances** the compositional contrast relative to the ETD because it has a more predictable, monotonic response to η and suppresses topographic and edge effects. The semiconductor BSE detector shows increased response from higher-energy BSEs, which are produced in greater relative abundance from heavier elements, thus further amplifying Z-contrast beyond the simple η difference (goldstein2018semimageinterpretation pages 3-5). In the pyrite (FeS₂)/galena (PbS) example, the large Z difference is visible in both detectors, but "the topographic contrast is so strong [in the ETD image] that it overwhelms the compositional contrast" for rough specimens (goldstein2018semimageinterpretation pages 9-11).

**It is therefore incorrect to state that ETD SE images "cannot tell whether a feature is heavier than the matrix."** On a flat, polished multiphase specimen where topographic contrast is minimized, the ETD in positive-bias mode carries substantial Z-contrast information through its BSE-derived signal components (goldstein2018semimageinterpretation pages 3-5, goldstein2018semimageinterpretation pages 5-7). The Z-contrast in the ETD is, however, diluted compared to a dedicated BSE detector and can be obscured by topographic and edge effects on rough surfaces.

### (3) Other Mechanisms That Make Sub-Micron Features Bright in ETD Images

Besides higher mean atomic number, several mechanisms can produce bright contrast from sub-micron features in ETD images of a polished AlSi10Mg surface:

**a) Edge and topographic effects.** The secondary electron coefficient depends strongly on surface inclination as δ(θ) ≈ sec(θ), increasing rapidly as the beam approaches grazing incidence (goldstein2018semimageinterpretation pages 5-7). Sub-micron particles that protrude even slightly above a polished surface, or that have steep edges due to differential polishing, will produce enhanced SE emission at their periphery, appearing as bright halos. This is a well-known artifact in ETD imaging and is explicitly described by Goldstein et al. (2018) (goldstein2018semimageinterpretation pages 5-7, goldstein2018semimageinterpretation pages 7-9).

**b) Negative charging of insulating particles.** Insulating particles such as Al₂O₃, SiO₂, SiC, or diamond embedded in a conducting Al matrix can accumulate negative charge when the incident beam energy is above the E₂ crossover energy (the energy at which total emitted yield equals unity). For alumina, E₂ ≈ 2.9–4.2 keV; for quartz, E₂ ≈ 3.0 keV (wuhrer2016lowvoltageimaging pages 4-7). At 5 kV, which is near or above E₂ for these oxides, the specimen tends toward negative charging. A negative surface potential retards incoming primaries (reducing their effective landing energy) but also creates a local electric field that can accelerate SE away from the particle toward the positively biased ETD, making the particle appear anomalously bright (kawano2012applicationofthe pages 49-52, postek2015doesyoursem pages 5-6, postek2015doesyoursem pages 6-8). Charge accumulation can produce fields of order 10⁷ V/cm at the surface (lin2007astudyof pages 25-30), and the resulting bright or dark contrast can fluctuate temporally.

**c) Contamination.** Carbon contamination buildup under the beam creates a low-Z layer that generally reduces both SE and BSE yield, typically darkening features. However, differential contamination rates between phases can produce contrast artifacts.

**How strongly does BSE detector confirmation establish higher Z?**

When a dedicated BSE detector (such as the Thermo Fisher Apreo in-lens T1 or a retractable annular/CBS detector) shows the **same bright features with the same grayscale ordering** as the ETD, this provides strong evidence for compositional (Z) contrast rather than topographic or charging artifacts. The reasoning is:

- **BSE images are largely insensitive to specimen charging** because BSE have energies of keV, far exceeding any surface potential of a few volts; the low-energy SE that are most affected by charging are excluded by a BSE detector (postek2015doesyoursem pages 8-10).
- **Topographic contrast is suppressed** by large-solid-angle, annular BSE detectors operated in SUM mode, because directionality differences from tilted surfaces are integrated out (goldstein2018semimageinterpretation pages 9-11).
- **Edge enhancement is primarily an SE phenomenon** (sec θ dependence of δ), not a BSE effect; BSE detectors do not show the pronounced bright edge halos characteristic of SE images.

Therefore, when both the ETD and a dedicated BSE detector show the same bright features, the most parsimonious explanation is **higher mean atomic number** of those features relative to the matrix. However, this conclusion is not absolute: residual BSE topographic contrast from differential polishing relief remains possible even with a SUM-mode detector, and at very low kV, charging can indirectly affect BSE images by deflecting the primary beam and changing its landing energy (postek2015doesyoursem pages 8-10, wuhrer2016lowvoltageimaging pages 4-7). Confirmation by EDS point analysis or mapping is the definitive test.

**Key findings:**

At 20 kV and above, the backscatter coefficient η increases monotonically with atomic number and can be estimated from the polynomial fit η = −0.0254 + 0.016Z − 1.86×10⁻⁴Z² + 8.3×10⁻⁷Z³ for E₀ ≥ 10 keV (goldstein2018semimageinterpretation pages 1-3, goldstein2018semimageinterpretation pages 3-5). For compounds, η can be approximated from the mass-fraction-weighted average of elemental coefficients, or equivalently from the mass-fraction-averaged mean atomic number Z̄ inserted into the polynomial (goldstein2018semimageinterpretation pages 3-5, lloyd1987atomicnumberand pages 5-6, lloyd1987atomicnumberand pages 3-5).

**Is the ordering preserved at 5 kV?** Above approximately 2 keV, the backscatter coefficient is described as "approximately independent of accelerating voltage" and monotonic with Z (wuhrer2016lowvoltageimaging pages 4-7, fitting2004sixlawsof pages 4-5). Below 2 keV, η begins to show anomalous behavior: it decreases for heavy elements and may increase for light elements, with the normal Z-ordering potentially reversing in the sub-keV regime (gold at 100 eV can have η lower than aluminum) (fitting2004sixlawsof pages 1-2, kuhr1999monte‐carlosimulationof pages 13-17). At 5 kV, the standard Z-ordering is expected to be fully preserved, with at most modest contrast compression among neighboring light elements.

For the specific case of Al vs. Mg₂Si at low voltage, CASINO Monte Carlo simulations showed that the BSE contrast between Al (η ≈ 0.200 at 1 keV) and Mg₂Si (η ≈ 0.190 at 1 keV) is very small at 1 keV but improves at 5 keV, where a 10 nm Mg₂Si precipitate could be detected with a 5 nm probe (hovington2006casinoanew pages 10-12). This confirms that operating at 5 kV provides better Z-contrast than 1 kV for these light phases.

**Expected brightness ordering at 5 kV from darkest to brightest:**
C (diamond) < Al₂O₃ ≈ SiO₂ < SiC < Mg₂Si ≲ Al < Si ≪ β-Al₅FeSi ≈ α-Al₁₅(Fe,Mn)₃Si₂ ≪ Fe

The Fe-rich intermetallics (β-Al₅FeSi, α-Al₁₅(Fe,Mn)₃Si₂) have Z̄ ≈ 16–17 and should appear distinctly brighter than the Al matrix (Z = 13) in BSE images. Diamond and the oxide/carbide compounds (Z̄ ≈ 6–12) should appear darker than the Al matrix.

**Where evidence is missing:** Systematically measured, experimental BSE coefficients at exactly 5 kV for the specific compounds Al₂O₃, SiO₂, SiC, and the Al-Fe-Si intermetallic stoichiometries were not found in the literature accessed. The values in the table are estimates based on the mean-Z approximation and the well-established trends from Monte Carlo simulations. The Hunger–Küchler and Neubert–Rogaschewski empirical fits provide energy-dependent expressions for elemental η but have not been systematically validated for compounds at 5 kV (lin2007astudyof pages 133-137). Lloyd (1987) noted that for minerals, observed Z-contrasts are typically less than 20% of the theoretically possible range, requiring signal processing (black-level correction) to visualize small compositional differences (lloyd1987atomicnumberand pages 5-6). This applies particularly to the closely spaced light phases in AlSi10Mg.
