Question: What is the measurement repeatability (within-lab) and reproducibility (between-lab) of metal powder characterization used for additive manufacturing: (1) sieve analysis per ASTM B214 / ISO 4497 (standard deviation of mass fraction per sieve cut); (2) laser diffraction per ISO 13320 / ASTM B822 (coefficient of variation of D10, D50, D90); (3) SEM or dynamic image analysis of sphericity or circularity (how many particles must be measured for a stable mean, and the typical uncertainty); (4) SEM-EDS quantitative composition of aluminum alloy powders, e.g. Si and Mg in Al alloys (typical relative error and repeatability); (5) powder sampling error (riffling, spinning riffler per ASTM B215) and its contribution to the total variance. Give numeric values with citations, especially ASTM/ISO precision-and-bias statements and interlaboratory studies.

# Repeatability and reproducibility of metal-powder characterization

**The available evidence does not establish a single ASTM/ISO precision value for each requested measurement.** In particular, I could not verify an ASTM B214/ISO 4497 precision-and-bias table giving the standard deviation of *mass retained in each sieve cut*, or an ASTM B822/ISO 13320 interlaboratory table giving D10, D50, and D90 coefficients of variation for AM metal powder. The numerical studies below are useful comparators, but a variation between containers measured in one laboratory is **not** between-laboratory reproducibility, and variation between particles is **not** repeatability of a reported powder mean. (brown2016interlaboratorystudyfor pages 2-3, slotwinski2014characterizationofmetal pages 8-12, mingard2009improvingtheconsistency pages 40-44)

## 1. Sieve analysis: ASTM B214 / ISO 4497

For each cut, the appropriate result is retained mass divided by the initial test-portion mass; its repeatability standard deviation must be estimated from **independently sampled and sieved test portions**. None of the accessible evidence provides a defensible universal standard deviation, in percentage points of retained mass, for a particular cut under either standard. Cut-dependent effects matter: particles near an aperture, sieve loading, lodged particles, and the condition of the mesh can change retention. A powder-characterization handbook identifies ISO 4497 and ASTM B214 as corresponding metal-powder sieving methods and describes weighing the residue on each sieve, but does not supply an interlaboratory precision table. (neikov2019powdercharacterizationand pages 3-4)

One NIST study did sieve IN625 powder from different participant containers following **ASTM B214** and reported derived size percentiles: D10 = **18.46 ± 3.77 µm**, D50 = **34.32 ± 1.74 µm**, and D90 = **49.95 ± 1.08 µm**. These are *not* standard deviations of retained mass per sieve cut. Samples from participant containers were analyzed by NIST; this was not an interlaboratory comparison of sieve measurements, despite the interlaboratory AM-build study in which the powder was used. The implied percentile CVs are approximately **20.4%, 5.1%, and 2.2%**, respectively, and must not be presented as ASTM B214 precision limits. (brown2016interlaboratorystudyfor pages 2-3)

## 2. Laser diffraction: ISO 13320 / ASTM B822

In a NIST metal-powder study using wet laser diffraction, four **17-4 stainless-steel containers** from one heat lot gave D10 **24.4 ± 0.6 µm**, D50 **36.5 ± 0.2 µm**, and D90 **54.5 ± 0.8 µm**; the corresponding between-container CVs calculated from the reported means and one-standard-deviation spreads are **2.5%, 0.55%, and 1.5%**. Fifteen **CoCr containers** gave **8.9 ± 0.4**, **23.0 ± 0.96**, and **44.7 ± 1.5 µm**, or CVs of approximately **4.5%, 4.2%, and 3.4%**. These spreads combine container, subsampling, and analytical effects under one laboratory’s procedure; neither set is a separately estimated within-laboratory repeatability SD or between-laboratory reproducibility SD. NIST describes its dispersion medium as isopropanol and its measurement procedure as 25 measurements after sonication followed by three sets of six measurements checked for consistency. (slotwinski2014characterizationofmetal pages 8-12, slotwinski2014characterizationofmetal pages 3-4)

An actual **between-laboratory** round robin in an NPL good-practice guide used **silica, not metal powder**. For its powder extending to approximately 100 µm, D10/D50/D90 between-laboratory CVs were **30.5%/14.0%/7.4%** with a less prescribed procedure and **9.9%/5.0%/4.6%** after a more closely specified procedure. For finer silica extending to approximately 10 µm, the corresponding CVs were **43.7%/20.4%/8.8%** and **35.2%/9.6%/9.5%**. These results demonstrate the potentially large contribution of laboratory-specific sampling and dispersion; they are **not** ISO 13320 or ASTM B822 precision-and-bias limits for metal powders. (mingard2009improvingtheconsistency pages 40-44)

## 3. SEM and dynamic image analysis: circularity or sphericity

**There is no source-supported fixed number of particles that guarantees a stable shape mean.** For a *number-weighted* mean of independently sampled particle circularities, a defensible count is determined from the observed between-particle standard deviation, \(s_C\): its sampling standard error is approximately \(s_C/\sqrt n\), and an approximate 95% sampling half-width is \(1.96s_C/\sqrt n\). For illustration—not a measured AM-powder precision claim—if \(s_C=0.10\), **100, 400, and 1,600** representative particles give approximate half-widths of **0.020, 0.010, and 0.005** circularity units, respectively. Independently prepared aliquots and repeated segmentation are still required to assess *measurement* repeatability; counting more particles cannot remove sampling bias or systematic image-analysis error. The result also depends on whether the reported mean is number- or volume-weighted. Mitterlehner and colleagues explicitly use a **volume-weighted** image-analysis sphericity and show that their silhouette-based sphericity definition differs from another commonly termed circularity: in their notation, SPHT = \(4\pi A/P^2=C^2\). (mitterlehner2021comparativeevaluationof pages 3-4, mitterlehner2021comparativeevaluationof pages 4-5)

The stakes of the measurand are illustrated by a NIST SEM study of 17-4 steel powder: it estimated that **59,866 particles** were needed for a **95% probability of no more than 3% relative error in a mass-median *size***, and analyzed **more than 69,000**. That is a distribution-percentile, volume/mass-weighted sizing calculation—**not** a minimum count established for mean circularity. For three repeated dynamic-image-analysis measurements, its average standard uncertainty of *volume fraction per size bin* was **0.02 percentage points wet** and **0.06 percentage points dry**; again, neither number is uncertainty of sphericity. (whiting2019uncertaintyofparticle pages 2-3, whiting2019uncertaintyofparticle pages 3-4)

A metal-powder methods comparison provides a useful warning about **between-method differences**, rather than repeatability: volume-based sphericity for one nearly spherical powder was **0.969 by dynamic imaging**, **0.825 by optical microscopy of polished sections**, and **0.898 by micro-CT**. The authors attribute part of the micro-CT discrepancy to its 1-µm voxel resolution. Consequently, a universal claim of, for example, ±0.01 sphericity across SEM, dynamic imaging, and other geometries would not be justified by these data; no validated typical between-laboratory SD for an AM-powder shape mean was located. (mitterlehner2021comparativeevaluationof pages 10-11, mitterlehner2021comparativeevaluationof media d1ad888f)

## 4. SEM–EDS composition: Si and Mg in Al-alloy powder

For **polished, homogeneous specimens measured with standards and matrix corrections**, NIST authors describe achievable relative accuracy of approximately **±5% for major constituents** (>10 wt%), **±10% for minor constituents** (1–10 wt%), and **±25% for trace-level constituents** (0.1–1 wt%). These are accuracy guidance, **not repeatability SDs or validated limits for an unpolished AlSi10Mg particle**. Silicon near 10 wt% lies on the major/minor boundary; magnesium below 1 wt% lies in the authors’ trace category. Surface curvature and local topography of loose particles can cause substantially greater error; normalizing a standardless analysis to 100% can conceal it. (newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 23-25)

As an illustration of *best-controlled repeatability*, not of Al-alloy-powder performance, NIST measured a homogeneous reference **glass** in nine replicate standards-based EDS analyses. Si was **25.38 wt% certified versus 25.65 wt% measured** (**+1.1% relative deviation; 0.09% relative replicate SD**), and Mg was **8.85 versus 8.81 wt%** (**−0.4% relative deviation; 0.13% relative replicate SD**). These high Mg and Si concentrations and polished-glass geometry cannot establish precision for approximately 0.3–0.5 wt% Mg in loose AlSi10Mg powder. No Al-alloy-powder-specific interlaboratory SEM–EDS Si/Mg repeatability or reproducibility estimates were verified. Bulk certification of such low Mg contents should therefore be checked with a validated bulk-composition method rather than inferred from particle-surface EDS alone. (newbury2019electronexcitedxraymicroanalysis pages 5-6, newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 23-25)

## 5. Powder sampling and its share of total variance

A published powder-sampling comparison reproduced in the NPL guide gives **relative SDs/CVs** of **6.81% for coning and quartering, 5.14% for scoop sampling, 2.09% for table splitting, 1.01% for chute splitting, and 0.125% for a spinning riffler**. These characterize that comparison’s sampled property and powder, **not** a universal CV of every sieve cut or D-value and **not** an ASTM B215 precision-and-bias statement. The guide identifies B215 as a practice for sampling metal powders and recommends taking increments across a moving powder stream; it also notes that segregation adds error beyond fundamental random sampling. A separate experiment with segregating glass-particle mixtures found that a rotary divider could introduce composition-dependent bias, underscoring that owning a rotary device does not itself validate unbiased splitting. (mingard2009improvingtheconsistency pages 22-26, hawkins1988demonstratingthesampling pages 2-3, mingard2009improvingtheconsistency pages 59-64, mingard2009improvingtheconsistency pages 64-70)

For **independent, approximately unbiased** sources expressed in the *same measurand and units*, a practical variance budget is \(s^2_{\mathrm{total}}\approx s^2_{\mathrm{bulk/increment}}+s^2_{\mathrm{split}}+s^2_{\mathrm{preparation}}+s^2_{\mathrm{instrument}}\). The sampling contribution is \((s^2_{\mathrm{bulk/increment}}+s^2_{\mathrm{split}})/s^2_{\mathrm{total}}\), not the sum of their CVs. The published **0.125%** spinning-riffler CV cannot be inserted directly into an AM-powder D90 or sieve-cut budget: first measure the variation among **independently split test portions**, then compare it with repeated analyses of one portion. As a conditional arithmetic example, if a given measurand has a measured sampling CV of **1%** and analytical CV of **2%**, independent sources imply total CV **2.24%** and sampling accounts for **20% of variance**. Segregation or systematic bias must be assessed separately, not hidden inside this quadrature calculation. (mingard2009improvingtheconsistency pages 22-26, hawkins1988demonstratingthesampling pages 3-4)

**Bottom line:** the defensible numerical evidence is strongest for NIST’s *single-laboratory, between-container* metal-powder D-value spreads, the *non-metal* laser-diffraction interlaboratory comparison, calibrated-versus-rough-specimen EDS performance, and a *material-specific* splitter comparison. Requested ASTM/ISO **sieve-cut mass-fraction SDs**, metal-powder LD **between-lab CVs**, shape-mean **repeatability SDs**, and AlSi10Mg-specific **Si/Mg EDS repeatability** remain unquantified by the verified sources; assigning them universal numerical tolerances would conflate different measurands and experimental designs. (brown2016interlaboratorystudyfor pages 2-3, slotwinski2014characterizationofmetal pages 8-12, mingard2009improvingtheconsistency pages 40-44, newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 23-25, mitterlehner2021comparativeevaluationof pages 10-11)
