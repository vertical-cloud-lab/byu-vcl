## Moisture Interactions with Freshly Atomized Aluminum-Alloy Powder Below the Dew Point: Mechanisms, Consequences, and Cooling-System Management

### 1. Moisture Uptake Mechanisms When Cold Powder Meets Humid Air

#### 1.1 The Local Relative Humidity Problem

When a powder bed or powder mass is at temperature *T*_powder and is exposed to air at temperature *T*_air with a certain water-vapor partial pressure *p*_w, the relative humidity (RH) experienced at the powder surface is *p*_w / *p*_sat(*T*_powder). Because the saturation vapor pressure decreases steeply with temperature, a powder at 7–10 °C exposed to air with a dew point of 10–15 °C experiences an effective local RH that exceeds 100%, meaning bulk condensation will occur on the powder surfaces and in the interparticle spaces, not merely monolayer adsorption. Even at the enclosure's controlled conditions (27 °C, 25–26% RH, dew point 5.5–7.7 °C), powder cooled to 7 °C by the water jacket would be at or below the dew point, producing bulk condensation.

#### 1.2 Adsorption and Capillary Condensation

Water-vapor adsorption on alumina surfaces follows a Type V isotherm with H3 hysteresis, indicating weak initial adsorbate–adsorbent interaction followed by sharp capillary condensation at high RH (yang2020theadsorptionbehavior pages 6-8). For smelter-grade alumina (BET surface area 69–92 m²/g), equilibrium moisture uptake at 20 °C ranges from approximately 36 mg/g at 70% RH to 133 mg/g at 90% RH (yang2020theadsorptionbehavior pages 12-13). Although metallic aluminum powder has far less porosity and a much lower BET water-adsorption surface area—measured at only 0.125 m²/g for argon-gas-atomized pure aluminum powder (yamasaki2006changesinthe pages 1-2)—the adsorption per unit mass is correspondingly small when the powder is above the dew point. However, at the dew point or below, the distinction between adsorption and condensation vanishes: liquid water forms freely on all available surfaces and in interstices.

The practical consequence is that for untreated aluminum powder exposed to increasing humidity, bulk unconfined yield strength increases by approximately 40% from 20% to 50% RH and by a further 25% at 80% RH, driven by capillary forces from pendular water bridges between particles (stevens2009controllingunconfinedyield pages 4-5, stevens2009controllingunconfinedyield pages 3-4). At 80% RH, AlSi10Mg powder forms macroscopic agglomerates that cannot be redispersed by shaking (weiss2022investigationonthe pages 2-3).

#### 1.3 Fresh vs. Passivated Powder Reactivity

Freshly gas-atomized aluminum powder develops a native oxide passivation layer approximately 5 nm thick within its first hours of air exposure (lienhard2020surfaceoxideand pages 3-4, anderson2001determiningtherole pages 3-4). Commercial inert-gas atomizers (CIGA) commonly introduce 1–2 vol% oxygen into the atomizing gas to promote this passivation layer during atomization (anderson2001determiningtherole pages 2-3). The clean-atmosphere GARS (Gas Atomization Reaction Synthesis) process instead evacuates the system to ~6.67 Pa and backfills with ultrahigh-purity N₂ at a dew point below −67 °C, producing powder with only ~800 ppm oxygen versus ~2,600 ppm for CIGA and ~3,300 ppm for commercial air-atomized powder (anderson2001determiningtherole pages 2-3).

Fresh powder with only a thin oxide is more reactive with water vapor than aged powder with a thicker, more mature oxide. Vacuum temperature-programmed desorption (TPD) of humid-air-exposed gas-atomized aluminum powder shows substantial H₂O and H₂ evolution, while dry-air-exposed powder releases far less water and essentially no detectable hydrogen (yamasaki2006changesinthe pages 1-2, yamasaki2004effectofvacuum pages 1-2). The critical reaction is 2Al + 3H₂O → Al₂O₃ + 3H₂: bare aluminum or thin-oxide aluminum reacts exothermically with adsorbed water, generating hydrogen and thickening the oxide (yamasaki2006changesinthe pages 2-4, yamasaki2004effectofvacuum pages 2-3).

Anderson et al. (2018) describe the general concept that commercial atomizers may add a low concentration of reactive gas (oxygen) during controlled cooling in the spray chamber to passivate powder before collection, though specific published protocols (oxygen concentration, bleed rate, timing, or target oxide thickness) remain proprietary and are not found in the open literature (anderson2018feedstockpowderprocessing pages 5-7). The AMAZEMET ultrasonic atomizer atomizes under argon and does not appear to include a deliberate air-bleed passivation step, so the powder acquires its oxide layer primarily upon venting and opening the chamber.

### 2. Measured Consequences of Moisture Pickup

#### 2.1 Oxide and Hydroxide Formation

Ambient aging of AlSi10Mg powder causes oxygen content to increase from 0.067 wt% (virgin) to 0.257 wt% after 96 hours, nearly a 4× increase, while hydrogen remains approximately constant at 0.003 wt% (~30 ppm) (fedina2022influenceofalsi10mg pages 4-5). The surface oxide layer of aluminum powder exposed to humid air comprises physisorbed water, chemisorbed water (incorporated into the oxide film), hydrated aluminum oxides (bayerite, gibbsite, boehmite), and amorphous alumina (anderson2001determiningtherole pages 3-4, anderson2001determiningtherole pages 1-2). Chemisorbed water cannot be removed by exposure to a dry atmosphere at room temperature (anderson2001determiningtherole pages 7-8).

#### 2.2 Hydrogen Content and Porosity in LPBF Parts

During LPBF processing of AlSi10Mg, moisture on powder surfaces decomposes: oxygen forms stable Al₂O₃ while hydrogen dissolves into the aluminum melt. Given the extremely rapid solidification, dissolved hydrogen can become supersaturated and form gas pores. Weingarten et al. (2015) measured up to 34 ml H₂/100 g of aluminum during LPBF melting, roughly 50× the hydrogen solubility of liquid pure aluminum at its melting point (0.7 ml/100 g) (cauwenbergh2019reducinghydrogenpores pages 1-2, cauwenbergh2019reducinghydrogenporesa pages 1-2). Preheating the powder bed to 200 °C was reported to reduce hydrogen porosity by approximately 50% by evaporating moisture before melting (kramer2026investigationofthe pages 2-4).

Schulze et al. (2025) measured 230 ppm hydrogen and approximately 305 ppm oxygen in AlSi10Mg LPBF parts handled with careful low-humidity protocols. Riener et al. (2021) reported hydrogen ranging from 143 to 908 ppm depending on powder condition (new, aged, dried), with oxygen between 683 and 759 ppm (schulze2025influenceofmanufacturing pages 8-10). For LPBF AlSi7Mg0.6, printed parts contained 13 ± 4 ppm hydrogen, reduced from 45 ± 11 ppm in the powder, attributed partly to the machine's vacuum pre-cycle (cauwenbergh2019reducinghydrogenpores pages 4-6). As-built relative density was 99.93 ± 0.12%, while conventional T7 heat treatment dropped it to 96.74 ± 2.60% due to hydrogen pore expansion, and HIP restored it to 99.93% (cauwenbergh2019reducinghydrogenpores pages 4-6).

#### 2.3 Flowability and Spreadability

Moisture on powder surfaces dramatically reduces flowability and spreadability. AlSi10Mg is especially vulnerable: at 80% RH it forms large agglomerates that cannot be dispersed, and its avalanche angle increases sharply (weiss2022investigationonthe pages 2-3). Moisturized powders fail to flow freely through Hall-flowmeter funnels, and their relative density when spread as thin layers decreases significantly (cordova2020measuringthespreadability pages 8-10, cordova2020measuringthespreadability pages 1-2). The mechanism is capillary liquid bridging between particles, increasing cohesive forces (cordova2020measuringthespreadability pages 2-3, cordova2020measuringthespreadability pages 10-11, peres2024influenceofmoisture pages 1-2). Vacuum drying produces slight flowability improvements for all alloys, while air drying effects are alloy-dependent (cordova2020measuringthespreadability pages 8-10).

*Table: Temperature-resolved summary of physical drying, hydroxide dehydration, oxide conversion, and hydrogen evolution in aluminum powders. It distinguishes reversible adsorbed-water removal from irreversible conversion of hydroxide-bearing surface films.*

A critical distinction is that drying **does not reverse** hydroxide formation or restore the powder to its pre-exposure state. Low-temperature drying (below ~175 °C) removes only physisorbed water (zhang2010degassingbehaviorof pages 1-2). Heating to 230 °C is insufficient to fully decompose surface trihydroxides (bayerite, gibbsite) (ludwig2022infraredspectroscopystudies pages 8-11). Heating at 300 °C for 1 hour in air eliminates detectable trihydroxide hydroxyl signatures by decomposing them to boehmite or alumina, but this converts hydroxide to oxide rather than regenerating bare aluminum (ludwig2022infraredspectroscopystudies pages 8-11). The optimal degassing temperature for aluminum powder consolidation is identified as 623 K (~350 °C), at which hydrated alumina converts to crystalline alumina, while chemisorbed hydrogen requires temperatures above 673 K (~400 °C) for complete removal (yamasaki2006changesinthe pages 1-2, yamasaki2006changesinthe pages 4-4). However, excessive degassing temperatures can decompose rapidly solidified metastable phases in the alloy (yamasaki2004effectofvacuum pages 1-2).

Laser powder drying—applying a low-power laser scan before the melting scan—was tested for AlSi7Mg0.6 LPBF and found to be "inefficient at reducing hydrogen pores," possibly because most surface moisture had already been removed during the machine's vacuum pre-cycle, and hydrogen from other sources (supersaturated within atomized particles) was not addressed (cauwenbergh2019reducinghydrogenporesa pages 2-4). In-situ laser drying of AlSi10Mg was reported to reduce hydrogen content by up to 25% (kramer2026investigationofthe pages 2-4), though the improvement in porosity depends on scan speed and other processing parameters.

### 4. Cooling-Water Temperature Relative to Dew Point

#### 4.1 Failure Modes from Condensation

When cooling-water temperature falls below the ambient dew point, condensation forms on cooled surfaces. For induction melting systems, Gombert and Richardson (2004) documented that in a cold-crucible induction melter at Idaho National Engineering and Environmental Laboratory, chilled water circulated through vacuum-tube anodes, RF feed lines, capacitor retainers, and the primary inductor. Both the equipment manufacturer and Russian collaborators specifically recommended maintaining the water temperature "well above the dew point." Otherwise, condensation on or around high-voltage energized components creates conditions for arcing and system trips (gombert2004coldcrucibleinduction pages 16-18). The report also documented arcing incidents in the induction system, though it attributed those specific events to inadequate spacing rather than condensation alone.

In the user's scenario, the atomizer's "water too cold" alarm (originally 10 °C, lowered to 7 °C) and condensation on hoses and inside the chamber are consistent with this failure mode. Condensation inside the atomization chamber is particularly damaging because:

1. **Electrical risk**: Water films on insulating surfaces near induction coils or power electronics can cause electrical tracking, flashover, or arcing. Humidity reduces surface insulation resistance; tracking is "particularly hazardous in polluted or high-humidity environments" (gombert2004coldcrucibleinduction pages 16-18).

2. **Moisture carryover to the next melt**: Water adsorbed on chamber walls, the crucible, and the sonotrode will desorb during subsequent heating, raising the partial pressure of water vapor in the nominally argon atmosphere. This water reacts with the aluminum melt (2Al + 3H₂O → Al₂O₃ + 3H₂), directly increasing hydrogen pickup in the melt and degrading powder quality (yamasaki2004effectofvacuum pages 2-3, iwamoto2007vacuumdegassingbehavior pages 1-3).

3. **Corrosion**: Condensation on the cast-aluminum chamber body and stainless-steel cooling channels promotes galvanic corrosion over time.

#### 4.2 Recommended Margins

**Explicit published standards** for minimum cooling-water temperature relative to dew point in induction melters, vacuum furnaces, and atomizers are largely absent from the peer-reviewed literature. This is a significant gap; guidance resides primarily in equipment manufacturers' installation and operation manuals. The general engineering practice, widely cited in HVAC and industrial cooling literature, is to maintain cooling-water supply temperature at least **3–5 °C above the local dew point**. For the user's conditions:

- Enclosure air at 27 °C, 25–26% RH → dew point 5.5–7.7 °C → minimum water temperature should be ~11–13 °C.
- Surrounding room at 22 °C, dew point 7.5–12 °C → if any air leaks into the enclosure or during door openings, water should not drop below ~15–17 °C.
- Summer room air at dew point 10–15 °C → water should be ≥18–20 °C during loading/unloading if the enclosure is open.

The user's observation that the loop ran at ~23 °C with the building valve throttled is well above even summer dew-point conditions and is a sensible operating point.

### 5. Practical Recommendations for the User's System

1. **Cooling water setpoint**: Maintain cooling-water supply temperature at least 5 °C above the highest dew point the chamber surfaces will encounter. With the enclosure dehumidifier running (dew point 5.5–7.7 °C), a water temperature of 13–15 °C is sufficient; the ~23 °C operating point provides a large safety margin. Never run below 10 °C unless the enclosure dew point is below 5 °C.

2. **Chamber venting protocol**: After a run, the chamber walls and cone are already cold (near water-jacket temperature). Venting to air at this point will cause condensation if the air's dew point exceeds the wall temperature. The dehumidifier provides protection (enclosure dew point 5.5–7.7 °C vs. walls presumably at 15–23 °C), but **keep the enclosure sealed during venting**. If the surrounding room door must be opened, condensation from the higher room dew point (7.5–12 °C, or 10–15 °C in summer) could form.

3. **Powder handling**: Freshly atomized powder should not be exposed to any air whose dew point is near or above the powder's temperature. Since the powder exits the chamber cold (the furnace is cooled to ≤400 °C but the powder itself has been sitting on cold chamber surfaces), ensure the powder has equilibrated to above the ambient dew point before opening, or flush with dry gas during transfer.

4. **Powder drying if condensation occurred**: If condensation has contacted the powder, low-temperature drying (e.g., 100–200 °C under vacuum or dry inert gas) will remove physisorbed water but will not reverse any hydroxide that has already formed. To decompose surface hydroxides, vacuum degassing at 300–350 °C for at least 1 hour is required, but this also thickens the oxide layer and releases hydrogen (yamasaki2006changesinthe pages 4-4, ludwig2022infraredspectroscopystudies pages 8-11). For alloys with metastable phases (e.g., Sc/Zr-containing alloys with L1₂ precipitates), degassing temperatures must be balanced against phase stability.

5. **Passivation**: For the AMAZEMET ultrasonic atomizer, consider implementing a controlled air bleed (low-ppm O₂ in argon) into the chamber after atomization but before full venting, to build a controlled passivation oxide before the powder encounters ambient humidity. This is common practice in commercial inert-gas atomization (anderson2001determiningtherole pages 2-3, anderson2018feedstockpowderprocessing pages 5-7), though specific protocols are proprietary.

### 6. Where Evidence Is Lacking or Conflicting

- **No published water-adsorption isotherms specific to metallic Al-Si or Al-Si-Mg AM powder** were found; the Yamasaki (2006) isotherm for pure Al powder exists as a figure but was not available in tabulated form (yamasaki2006changesinthe pages 1-2). Alumina isotherms from Yang et al. (2020) are for smelter-grade porous alumina with BET areas 100–1000× higher than metallic Al powder, so quantitative transfer is inappropriate.

- **Quantitative moisture uptake as a function of sub-dew-point exposure** for AM aluminum powder is not reported in the literature. The condensation regime (powder below dew point) has not been systematically studied—it is simply known to be catastrophic.

- **Specific numerical cooling-water temperature margins** for induction melters and atomizers are absent from peer-reviewed sources and standards. The Gombert (2004) report is the only published reference documenting the "keep water above dew point" recommendation with a specific failure mode (gombert2004coldcrucibleinduction pages 16-18).

- **Weingarten et al. (2015)** (DOI: 10.1016/j.jmatprotec.2015.02.013) is the seminal study on hydrogen porosity reduction in AlSi10Mg SLM, reportedly showing ~50% porosity reduction from 200 °C powder-bed preheating. This paper was unobtainable in the present search, so the result is cited only through secondary references (cauwenbergh2019reducinghydrogenpores pages 1-2, cauwenbergh2019reducinghydrogenporesa pages 1-2, kramer2026investigationofthe pages 2-4).

- There is **conflicting evidence on the effectiveness of laser in-process drying**: Kramer et al. (2026) report up to 25% hydrogen reduction from laser drying (kramer2026investigationofthe pages 2-4), while Van Cauwenbergh (2019) found it "inefficient" for AlSi7Mg0.6 (cauwenbergh2019reducinghydrogenporesa pages 2-4). The discrepancy may reflect differences in machine vacuum pre-cycles, powder moisture levels, and alloy-specific hydrogen solubility.

- **Riener et al.
