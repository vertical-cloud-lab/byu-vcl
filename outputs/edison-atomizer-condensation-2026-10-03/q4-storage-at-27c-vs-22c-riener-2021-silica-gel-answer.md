# Powder storage in the warmer, drier enclosure

**Bottom line.** For powder that is actually exposed to the stated environments, 27.2 °C/25–26% RH is the drier condition; a 5.4 °C temperature penalty for weeks-to-months storage of these particular powders has **not** been quantified in a controlled, fixed-RH study I could verify. Once a container is closed, however, its internal RH is governed principally by the water initially enclosed with the powder and silica gel, subsequent ingress, and the gel’s condition—not by the enclosure’s RH alone. Condensation during chamber opening is a separate, potentially more consequential exposure than cabinet storage. (grubbs2022explorationofthe pages 4-6, peres2024influenceofmoisture pages 2-3, thunberg2025desiccatedmicroclimatesfor pages 1-5, legrand2022fundamentalthermodynamicproperties pages 9-14)

## 1. What can be established about Riener et al. (2021)

The requested paper is Riener *et al.*, *Additive Manufacturing* **39**, 101896, DOI **10.1016/j.addma.2021.101896**. Its primary text was not obtainable in this search. Consequently, I **cannot verify or responsibly report its exact storage temperatures, RH set points, container materials, exposure durations, or numerical changes in powder moisture, oxygen, hydrogen, flowability, and printed-part porosity**. Nor can I identify its particular successful drying temperature, time, or atmosphere from primary evidence. These are gaps in access to the requested results, not evidence that the measurements were absent from the article. (fedina2022influenceofalsi10mg pages 1-2)

One attributable *secondary* account is available: Fedina *et al.* explicitly describe Riener’s finding that humid storage produced physically adsorbed water that **could be removed by drying**, while elevated powder oxygen **largely remained after drying**, consistent with surface oxidation. This supports drying as a means of removing reversible moisture, **not** as a way to restore oxidized feedstock or necessarily recover part quality. It does not establish a numerical hydrogen or porosity response for Riener’s experiments. Fedina’s own 3.16% versus 6.5% printed-part porosities belong to **Fedina’s different, accelerated-aging experiment**, and must not be attributed to Riener. (fedina2022influenceofalsi10mg pages 1-2, fedina2022influenceofalsi10mg pages 10-11)

For a *separate, directly inspectable* AlSi10Mg experiment, Cordova *et al.* used vacuum drying at **85 °C for 12 h**, air drying at **150 °C for 20 min**, and humidification at **50 °C/80% RH for 72 h** (DOI **10.1016/j.addma.2020.101082**). Their reported AlSi10Mg moisture metric—**volume-normalized loss on heating, not a Karl-Fischer mass-ppm measurement**—was **0.057 ± 0.020%** as received, **0.063 ± 0.040%** after vacuum drying, **0.068 ± 0.059%** after air drying, and **0.437 ± 0.140%** after humidification. The near-baseline differences between the first three values are within the reported scatter; humidification also increased oxygen. These procedures and numbers are **Cordova’s, not Riener’s**. Drying powder should therefore be validated for the particular alloy and measured with an appropriate water assay rather than assumed effective from furnace settings alone. (cordova2020measuringthespreadability pages 6-8, cordova2020measuringthespreadability pages 4-5)

## 2. Is there a measured, fixed-RH effect of storage temperature near ambient?

**I found no qualifying effect size** from a study that stores isolated Al, AlSi10Mg, Al–Mg, Al–Li, Al–Zn, or magnesium powder at two temperatures in approximately **15–35 °C**, while holding RH and exposure duration fixed, and measures subsequent powder degradation. That is a bounded finding from this literature search, not a claim that such a paper cannot exist. In particular, it provides no defensible multiplier for oxidation, hydrogen pickup, or LPBF porosity per 5.4 °C increase. There is likewise no justified transfer of an AlSi10Mg result to planned Li-, Zn-, or rare-earth-containing alloys, or to silicon powder. (grubbs2022explorationofthe pages 4-6, peres2024influenceofmoisture pages 2-3, nishiwaki2018studyofthe pages 1-2, weiss2022investigationonthe pages 2-3)

The nearest comparisons do **not** answer that temperature question:

* Peres *et al.* exposed AlSi10Mg for **five days** at **23 °C/29% RH, 23 °C/90% RH, 50 °C/17% RH, or 50 °C/60% RH**; they measured Karl-Fischer water and powder-flow properties, with more agglomeration under humid conditions and preservation of flow in the 50 °C/17% condition. They have no two-temperature, **same-RH** comparison near ambient (DOI **10.1590/1980-5373-MR-2023-0490**). (peres2024influenceofmoisture pages 2-3, peres2024influenceofmoisture media 7ee343dc)
* Grubbs *et al.* followed Al 5056 in sealed, argon-packed glass vials for **91 days**, imposing brief openings under laboratory conditions held near **20 °C**, with RH allowed to vary. The Al 5056 moisture results were scattered rather than a consistent storage trend; there was **no temperature treatment** (DOI **10.3390/met12040603**). (grubbs2022explorationofthe pages 4-6, grubbs2022explorationofthe pages 2-4, grubbs2022explorationofthe pages 9-11)
* Weiss *et al.* held AlSi10Mg at **50 °C** for **14 h**, comparing **50% and 80% RH**. That is a useful *humidity* challenge, not an ambient-temperature contrast (DOI **10.1016/j.procir.2022.08.102**). Studies aging AlSi10Mg at **400 °C** likewise cannot estimate a 22-to-27 °C storage effect (DOI **10.1016/j.matdes.2022.110677**). (weiss2022investigationonthe pages 2-3, silva2022influenceofaluminium pages 2-3)
* Nishiwaki *et al.* compared temperatures and RH for a reactive **ammonium-perchlorate/Mg mixture**, mainly at **30–60 °C**. Its pure-Mg comparison does not supply two matched temperatures in the requested range. AP/Mg mixture deterioration must not be treated as a measured temperature coefficient for sealed magnesium powder. (nishiwaki2018studyofthe pages 2-3, nishiwaki2018studyofthe pages 1-2)

## 3. Silica gel, internal RH, and thermal “breathing”

**Gel capacity is conditional, not a fixed percentage.** Legrand *et al.* measured type-A silica-gel adsorption isotherms at **25, 30, and 35 °C**, stepping RH from **5% to 95%**; at fixed water activity/RH, equilibrium uptake **decreased as temperature increased** (DOI **10.1016/j.cej.2021.134058**). Their numerical temperature-separated curves are identified as supplementary Figure S3, which was unavailable here, so I cannot assign a measured percentage-point capacity change specifically from **21.8 to 27.2 °C at 10–30% RH**. A different gel’s stated capacity of **25 g water/100 g dry gel at 25 °C/50% RH** is a *50%-RH equilibrium loading*, **not** guaranteed remaining capacity at the desired low internal RH, nor a defensible 27 °C prediction (DOI **10.1038/s41598-025-14677-7**). Gel grades and adsorption/desorption histories differ. (legrand2022fundamentalthermodynamicproperties pages 9-14, legrand2022fundamentalthermodynamicproperties pages 5-9, legrand2022fundamentalthermodynamicproperties media 3091dd45, hraiech2025experimentalcharacterizationof pages 4-6, thunberg2025desiccatedmicroclimatesfor pages 5-9)

A sealed container does **not** have an RH set point merely because it contains silica gel. Its equilibrium satisfies a water balance: initial water in headspace, gel, powder and packaging, **plus** water entering later, is apportioned among headspace, gel and other surfaces according to their temperature-dependent sorption isotherms. Fresh, sufficiently exposed gel can drive internal RH well below enclosure RH; preloaded or exhausted gel need not. Without the gel’s initial loading, mass and grade, powder loading, free headspace, and seal permeability, **neither the internal RH nor its 5.4 °C change can be calculated uniquely**. Measure it in a representative closed container with a suitable low-RH sensor; keep gel physically separate from powder and accessible to the headspace. Published enclosed-microclimate work emphasizes both leakage and unobstructed air contact with the gel (DOI **10.3390/heritage8070240**). (thunberg2025desiccatedmicroclimatesfor pages 1-5, thunberg2025desiccatedmicroclimatesfor pages 5-9, thunberg2025desiccatedmicroclimatesfor pages 12-16)

The *external* readings also need interpreting by water-vapor content. Using ordinary saturation-vapor relations as an **engineering calculation**, 27.2 °C/25–26% RH corresponds to roughly **0.90–0.93 kPa** water-vapor partial pressure; 21.8 °C/40% RH is roughly **1.04 kPa**. Thus the stated enclosure air is modestly drier in **absolute** vapor pressure as well as substantially lower in RH; it is not equivalent to saying that heating the *same* air to 27 °C removes water. At unchanged vapor pressure, the stated room air would be approximately **28–29% RH** on heating to 27.2 °C. These computed comparisons assume the two measurements are representative and contemporaneous; they do not determine the RH in a sealed jar. (thunberg2025desiccatedmicroclimatesfor pages 1-5, thunberg2025desiccatedmicroclimatesfor pages 16-19)

**Breathing bound.** For a rigid container with a freely communicating but imperfect seal, an approximately constant-pressure ideal-gas calculation gives a maximum displacement per monotonic temperature change of approximately **ΔV/V ≈ ΔT/T**. At **27 °C ≈ 300 K**, a **1–3 °C** daily swing displaces **0.33–1.0% of the *free headspace***: **33–100 mL for 10 L of headspace**, expelled during warming and potentially replaced during cooling, or **0.33–1.0% of one headspace exchange per complete cycle**. This is a *conditional free-venting estimate*, **not a measured leak rate**; a genuinely tight seal may admit essentially none, whereas diffusion, poor lids, handling and pressure/weather changes can add ingress. The full volume is **not** exchanged twice merely because air moves in opposite directions on the two half-cycles. Leakage and gel buffering are recognized determinants of enclosure RH (DOI **10.3390/heritage8070240**). (thunberg2025desiccatedmicroclimatesfor pages 1-5, thunberg2025desiccatedmicroclimatesfor pages 12-16)

For scale, an inward displacement of **33–100 mL** of the stated enclosure air, at approximately **6–7 g water/m³**, carries about **0.2–0.7 mg water per cycle per 10 L headspace** *before subtracting water in any outward-moving air*. Over **90 days**, this gives roughly **20–60 mg gross inward water**, **not** a prediction of net gel consumption; net transport depends on inside versus outside vapor concentrations, leaks and exchange kinetics. These are ideal-gas/psychrometric calculations rather than measured performance of your lids. Direct permeation through packaging or gaskets and repeated opening can dominate such a small temperature-driven volume. (thunberg2025desiccatedmicroclimatesfor pages 1-5, thunberg2025desiccatedmicroclimatesfor pages 12-16)

**Operational priority.** A 23 °C coolant loop is safely above the enclosure dew point as stated, but the prior hose drips and observed *inside-chamber* condensation show that local surfaces or conditions have not always matched that simple comparison. Immediately before venting/opening under air, check the **coldest chamber wall, cone, hose and receiving-container surface against the contemporaneous local dew point**; an enclosure-average RH reading does not prove those surfaces are dry. Argon cooling does not prevent condensation after humid air enters. Collect and seal powder only after confirming dry surfaces and powder, and use validated water/oxygen checks on representative powder if an event is suspected. For weeks-to-months storage, prioritize a demonstrably tight container, adequately regenerated and isolated gel, low-RH monitoring, and consistent opening procedures; the available literature does not support declaring the 27 °C cabinet storage either harmless or damaging by a specified numerical amount for these alloys. (fedina2022influenceofalsi10mg pages 1-2, cordova2020measuringthespreadability pages 2-3, thunberg2025desiccatedmicroclimatesfor pages 1-5, legrand2022fundamentalthermodynamicproperties pages 9-14)
