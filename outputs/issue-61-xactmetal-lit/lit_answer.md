Xact Metal models include the XM200C, XM200G, XM200S, and XM300 series. For each example found, report: the citation, the Xact Metal model used, the material(s) printed (e.g., 316L, 17-4PH, AlSi10Mg, Inconel 718, bronze, copper), the process parameters reported (laser power, scan speed, hatch spacing, layer thickness, hatch rotation angle), and what the study concluded about the machine's capabilities or limitations. Also note any papers describing use of Xact Metal machines in university teaching/education or with open/custom process parameters.

## Detailed evidence

### 1. M300 maraging-steel bending tools—XM200C

**Citation:** K. Żaba, M. Balcerzak, Ł. Kuczek, M. Wiewióra, I. Różycka, T. Trzepieciński, and J. Mizera, “Application of Powder-Bed Fusion of Metals Using a Laser for Manufacturing of M300 Maraging Steel Tools Intended for Sheet Metal Bending,” *Materials*, vol. 17, article 6185, 2024. DOI: **10.3390/ma17246185**. The paper identifies an Xact Metal **XM200C** and describes printing M300/1.2709 maraging-steel working portions directly on S235 structural-steel punch substrates. The resulting punches were tested by bending Inconel 625 and AW-6061 T0 sheet; those sheet alloys were workpieces, not LPBF feedstocks. (zaba2024applicationofpowderbed pages 1-2, zaba2024applicationofpowderbed pages 3-6)

**Reported parameters:** 50 W for contours and 80 W for fill; 250 mm/s for contours and 550 mm/s for fill; 30 µm layers; 100 µm laser spot; two exposures; 0.1 mm fill-border offset; 0.1 mm transition-area offset; 0.25 mm area tolerance; and sorting against gas flow. Hatch spacing and interlayer hatch-rotation angle were not reported. The authors state that these settings were based on the printer manufacturer’s guidance for M300 powder, rather than presenting them as a newly optimized open parameter set. (zaba2024applicationofpowderbed pages 3-6)

**Capability demonstrated:** The XM200C produced functional, near-minimally porous M300 working layers. The un-heat-treated, as-printed tools were usable for bending within a defined force range. Relative to the company’s conventional process, the authors reported a reduction from 80 to 48 hours, from 18 to 7 operations, and from about EUR 3,500 to EUR 1,500. They also emphasized the ability to vary working-part geometry and to control layer morphology through the process software. (zaba2024applicationofpowderbed pages 23-25, zaba2024applicationofpowderbed pages 22-23)

**Limitations demonstrated:** The outer surface was uneven because of the printing process, local pores produced hardness reductions, and both as-printed and polished tools deformed under high forming force. The authors attributed especially important deformation to the lower strength of the S235 substrate rather than solely to the printed M300. They concluded that LPBF tooling remained less strong and durable than conventional heat-treated tooling and required further optimization, potentially including heat treatment, surface treatment, laser remelting/alloying, or coatings. (zaba2024applicationofpowderbed pages 23-25, zaba2024applicationofpowderbed pages 22-23)

### 2. 20MnCr5 tribological countersamples—XM200C

**Citation:** K. Żaba, M. Madej, B. Leszczyńska-Madej, T. Trzepieciński, and R. Sitek, “Tribological Performance of Direct Metal Laser Sintered 20MnCr5 Tool Steel Countersamples Designed for Sheet Metal Forming Applications,” *Applied Sciences*, vol. 15, article 8711, 2025. DOI: **10.3390/app15158711**. The authors printed 20MnCr5/1.7147 tool-steel countersamples on an Xact Metal **XM200C**, then tested them against Inconel 625, type 321 stainless steel, AW-6061 T0 aluminum, and pure-copper sheet specimens. Only 20MnCr5 was printed; the other materials were tribological counterpart sheets. (zaba2025tribologicalperformanceof pages 1-2, zaba2025tribologicalperformanceof pages 2-4, zaba2025tribologicalperformanceof pages 4-6)

**Reported parameters:** 110 W laser power, 600 mm/s scan speed, 20 µm layers, 100 µm beam diameter, and a meander strategy rotating the scanning angle by 67° between successive layers. The paper reports a 100 µm “scanning distance.” This appears to serve the role normally described as hatch/scan-line spacing, but the authors’ terminology should be retained rather than treating that interpretation as certain. (zaba2025tribologicalperformanceof pages 4-6)

**Capability demonstrated:** Without additional surface treatment, XM200C-produced 20MnCr5 was judged potentially suitable for stamping-tool inserts or working layers used against 321 stainless steel and Inconel 625. These pairs had stable friction, coefficients of friction of approximately 0.194–0.213, and little mass loss; the Inconel pair showed no significant mass change. The work therefore demonstrates processing of a less-standard tool steel with a fully disclosed custom parameter combination and scan strategy. (zaba2025tribologicalperformanceof pages 16-18)

**Limitations demonstrated:** Performance was strongly counter-material-dependent. AW-6061 T0 and copper underwent pronounced adhesive wear and material transfer onto the printed countersample; the aluminum pair had a COF of 1.234 and 19.96% sheet mass loss. The authors also identified the still-limited number of metal grades available for DMLS as a broader limitation, although they noted continuing powder/process development. (zaba2025tribologicalperformanceof pages 16-18)

### 3. Porous 316L microbial-fuel-cell electrodes—XM200G

**Citation:** A. Elhadad, G. Li, J. Yang, D. Liu, and S. Choi, “3D-Printed Stainless Steel Electrodes for Advancing MEMS Microbial Fuel Cells Toward Sustainable On-Chip Energy,” in *2025 IEEE 38th International Conference on Micro Electro Mechanical Systems (MEMS)*, pp. 643–646, 2025. DOI: **10.1109/MEMS61431.2025.10918293**. The authors used 316L powder and an Xact Metal **XM200G**, equipped with a nominal 200 W laser, to fabricate porous stainless-steel anode/electrode structures. (elhadad20253dprintedstainlesssteel pages 1-2, elhadad20253dprintedstainlesssteel pages 2-4)

**Reported parameters:** 180 W laser power, 400 mm/s scan speed, 100 µm hatch spacing, 30 µm layers, and an argon atmosphere. Hatch-rotation angle was not reported. The authors say that the key parameters were “optimized to achieve high-quality prints,” so this is evidence that users could apply a study-specific parameter set; however, the short paper does not describe the optimization design, parameter bounds, firmware access, or whether every machine control was open. (elhadad20253dprintedstainlesssteel pages 2-4)

**Capability demonstrated:** The XM200G fabricated diverse 3D microporous sizes and shapes with porosity suitable for bacterial attachment and electron transfer. The resulting electrodes supported easy catalyst modification, retained bioelectrochemical activity without evident degradation, and were stable and reusable over multiple cycles. The assembled microbial fuel cell reached 127 µW maximum power and 750 µA peak current, outperforming the alternative miniature-MFC substrates used for comparison. (elhadad20253dprintedstainlesssteel pages 2-4, elhadad20253dprintedstainlesssteel pages 4-4)

**Limitations evident from the publication:** The conference paper does not provide hatch rotation, contour parameters, porosity-design parameters, density, processing-window limits, or optimization methodology. Thus, it establishes successful customized 316L processing and geometric control but cannot support conclusions about the XM200G’s entire parameter envelope or repeatability across machines. (elhadad20253dprintedstainlesssteel pages 2-4)

## Teaching, education, and open/custom parameters

* **Teaching/education:** No retrieved peer-reviewed article, conference paper, or thesis explicitly described an Xact Metal machine being used as part of a university course, laboratory class, student-training program, or pedagogical intervention. All three verified studies involved university researchers, but that is not equivalent to evidence of teaching use.
* **Open/custom processing:** The 2024 M300 study exposes numerous editable build settings in Materialise Magics but says that the chosen values followed Xact Metal manufacturer guidance. The 20MnCr5 study reports a distinct material-specific parameter combination and 67° rotating meander strategy, although it does not explicitly call the machine “open.” The 316L study explicitly says its parameters were optimized, but provides neither the optimization protocol nor a claim of open firmware/control. Accordingly, these papers support **user-set or study-specific parameters**, but not the stronger assertion that Xact Metal offers a fully open-control LPBF platform. (zaba2024applicationofpowderbed pages 3-6, zaba2025tribologicalperformanceof pages 4-6, elhadad20253dprintedstainlesssteel pages 2-4)

## Overall assessment

The verified literature is small but shows two principal capabilities: the XM200C can process M300 and 20MnCr5 tool steels into functional tooling or tribological specimens, while the XM200G can produce tailored porous 316L electrochemical structures. The strongest recurring limitations are incomplete reporting of hatch variables, as-built roughness and occasional porosity, dependence on substrate and mating material, and lack of a documented broad processing window. No verified publication in the retrieved corpus establishes use of an XM200S/XM300, formal educational deployment, or fully open machine control.
