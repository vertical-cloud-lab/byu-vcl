# q5_active_learning_formulation_data_efficiency

## Question

State of the art and future directions in ACTIVE LEARNING / BAYESIAN OPTIMIZATION for MULTICOMPONENT LIQUID FORMULATION where experiments are expensive in reagent cost, including the case where the measurement is a full SPECTRUM (a high-dimensional observation) rather than a scalar. Give DOIs. Cover: batch and constrained BO on mixture/simplex design spaces, cost-aware and reagent-budget-aware acquisition functions, how many experiments real published electrolyte or formulation campaigns actually needed, benchmark evidence on whether BO beats random search and Latin-hypercube/DoE on these problems and by how much, active learning targeted at improving a SURROGATE SPECTRAL MODEL (optimal experimental design / D-optimality / uncertainty sampling for training data acquisition) rather than optimizing a property, and best practice for held-out blind validation of such models.

## Answer

# Active Learning and Bayesian Optimization for Multicomponent Liquid Formulation: State of the Art and Future Directions

## 1. Batch and Constrained Bayesian Optimization on Mixture/Simplex Design Spaces

Multicomponent liquid formulations—electrolytes, coatings, pharmaceutical blends—inhabit a compositional simplex where component fractions must sum to a constant. Published electrolyte campaigns handle this constraint by parameterizing the design space with one fewer independent variable than the number of stock solutions; for example, Yik et al. (DOI: 10.26434/chemrxiv-2024-mqb6s-v3) used eight stock solutions but treated only seven as free inputs, with the eighth fixed by the total well volume of 1.1 mL (yik2025acceleratingaqueouselectrolyte pages 13-15). Dave et al. (DOI: 10.1038/s41467-022-32938-1) used the Dragonfly optimizer coupled to the Clio robotic platform, discretizing a ternary-solvent single-salt space and optimizing solvent mass fraction and salt molality as two effective continuous variables (dave2022autonomousoptimizationof pages 1-7).

More generally, constrained Bayesian optimization on mixture spaces can be addressed through (i) reparameterization into a reduced unconstrained space (e.g., isometric log-ratio or softmax transforms), (ii) probabilistic reparameterization for mixed discrete/continuous domains, and (iii) constrained acquisition-function optimization that directly enforces the sum-to-one or inequality constraints during candidate selection (yu2026bocodeengineeringcenteredbenchmarking pages 11-12). Batch acquisition—selecting multiple formulations per iteration for parallel experimentation—is handled by methods such as q-Expected Hypervolume Improvement (qEHVI) and q-Expected Improvement (qEI), which have been successfully deployed in multi-component alloy and materials optimization (mamun2025accelerateddevelopmentof pages 12-15). The review by Wang et al. (DOI: 10.1002/aidi.202500087) discusses both classical simplex-centroid mixture designs and advanced Bayesian strategies including active learning within this design-space framework (wang2025advancedexperimentdesign pages 6-6).

## 2. Cost-Aware and Reagent-Budget-Aware Acquisition Functions

When reagent cost varies across the design space, cost-aware acquisition functions replace standard Expected Improvement (EI) with cost-normalized variants. The general family is EI_α(x) = EI(x) / c(x)^α, where c(x) is the estimated cost of evaluating formulation x and α ∈ [0, 1] controls the cost–improvement trade-off. Setting α = 1 yields EI per unit cost (EI_pu), which strongly favors cheap experiments; α = 0 recovers standard EI (guinet2020paretoefficientacquisitionfunctions pages 2-5, guinet2020paretoefficientacquisitionfunctions pages 1-2). The cost function c(x) is typically modeled with a warped Gaussian process fitted to log-cost observations (guinet2020paretoefficientacquisitionfunctions pages 1-2).

Guinet et al. (DOI: 10.48550/arxiv.2011.11456) proposed a "cost-cooling" variant in which α decreases from 1 toward 0 as the experimental budget is consumed, starting with cheap experiments and transitioning to expensive but high-value evaluations as the campaign progresses (guinet2020paretoefficientacquisitionfunctions pages 1-2). They also introduced Contextual Expected Improvement (CEI), which selects the lowest-cost candidate among those whose EI exceeds a fraction (1 − λ) of the maximum EI, directly controlling how much improvement can be sacrificed for lower cost (guinet2020paretoefficientacquisitionfunctions pages 2-5).

For multi-objective settings with heterogeneous costs, Abdolshah et al. (DOI: 10.48550/arxiv.1909.03600) introduced CA-MOBO, which encodes ordered cost constraints on input dimensions (e.g., that nickel is more expensive than chromium) and uses Chebyshev scalarization with cost-aware bias that is strongest early and gradually relaxes (abdolshah2019costawaremultiobjectivebayesian pages 2-3, abdolshah2019costawaremultiobjectivebayesian pages 1-2). Ray and Srinivas (DOI: 10.48550/arxiv.2607.23896) proposed a two-stage policy: first discriminate among candidate pathways using expected uncertainty reduction per dollar, then optimize within the selected pathway under a capped budget (ray2607costawarerecoverypathwayidentification pages 1-2). This approach explicitly prices every experimental action and uses the total campaign budget as a hard constraint, rather than relying solely on per-experiment cost normalization (ray2607costawarerecoverypathwayidentification pages 9-11). Wakabayashi and Otsuka (DOI: 10.48550/arxiv.2608.26016) provide a tutorial-style overview of cost-normalized acquisition a_CES(u) = a(u)/c(u) for multi-fidelity settings, where the action u = (x, ℓ) includes both the experimental condition and the fidelity or information source (wakabayashi2608bayesianoptimizationfor pages 26-28).

Adesiji et al. (DOI: 10.1039/d5dd00337g) compiled acceleration factors (AFs) across numerous published SDL studies and found a range of 1.3–100× over random search, with a median of approximately 6× (adesiji2026benchmarkingselfdrivinglabs pages 10-11). Specific examples include 20× for catalyst activity optimization using GP-EI, 6× for electrolyte ionic conductivity, 61× for photovoltaic film power-conversion efficiency, and 5× for drug solubility (adesiji2026benchmarkingselfdrivinglabs pages 10-11). The study also reported that BO identified 4× as many high-performing perovskite films as Latin hypercube sampling (LHS) within 85 process conditions, and that multi-objective qEHVI reached the performance of 10,000 random samples in only 100 evaluations (adesiji2026benchmarkingselfdrivinglabs pages 7-10, adesiji2026benchmarkingselfdrivinglabs pages 4-7).

Liang et al. (DOI: 10.1038/s41524-021-00656-9) benchmarked BO across five experimental materials domains and found that GP with automatic relevance detection (ARD) and Random Forest surrogates had comparable and superior performance to isotropic GP baselines. They emphasized that BO's advantage is most pronounced when the goal is to discover multiple top candidates rather than a single good one, because random search can occasionally stumble on a good candidate early (liang2021benchmarkingtheperformance pages 1-3, liang2021benchmarkingtheperformance pages 3-6, liang2021benchmarkingtheperformance pages 6-9). Mamun et al. (DOI: 10.1088/2632-2153/ada47d) showed that BO found global optima in multi-component alloy datasets (>13,000 points) in fewer than 50 total computations from 25 seeds, while random acquisition failed entirely in multiple runs (mamun2025accelerateddevelopmentof pages 12-15, mamun2025accelerateddevelopmentof pages 15-19).

An important caveat from the benchmarking literature is that LHS is almost always used as the initialization method for BO itself rather than as a competing sequential strategy; thus, direct head-to-head comparisons of sequential BO versus sequential LHS or factorial DoE are uncommon (liang2021benchmarkingtheperformance pages 6-9).

## 5. Active Learning for Surrogate Spectral Models (High-Dimensional Observations)

When the experimental observation is a full spectrum rather than a scalar, the active-learning objective shifts from property optimization to training-data acquisition for a multi-output surrogate model. Several frameworks address this:

**Ensemble-based uncertainty sampling.** Pestourie et al. (DOI: 10.1038/s41524-020-00431-2) trained ensembles of neural-network surrogates to predict complex optical transmission as a function of device geometry. The ensemble disagreement provides both a predicted mean and uncertainty estimate. Active learning using this uncertainty reduced the number of required simulations by over an order of magnitude compared to uniform random sampling, while the resulting surrogates were over two orders of magnitude faster than direct solves (pestourie2020activelearningof pages 13-17, pestourie2020activelearningof pages 1-5). Critically, the authors found that purely exploitative acquisition—adding data only at the current optimum—destabilized learning; exploration of uncertain regions was essential (pestourie2020activelearningof pages 13-17).

**Quality-controlled spectral active learning (ActiveQC).** Chowdhury et al. (DOI: 10.1038/s41524-026-02248-x) introduced a gated active-learning framework for piezoresponse spectroscopy data. An encoder–decoder surrogate predicts full spectra, while a separate error model identifies compositions where spectral prediction is poorest. A physics-informed quality filter based on harmonic-oscillator fits gates out low-fidelity spectra before they enter the training pool. This curiosity-driven approach outperformed random sampling, standard active learning, and multitask learning strategies for image-to-spectrum translation tasks (chowdhury2026qualitycontrolledactivelearning pages 1-4, chowdhury2026qualitycontrolledactivelearning pages 4-8).

**Multi-output GP and coregionalization.** For moderate numbers of wavelength channels, multi-output Gaussian processes with intrinsic coregionalization model cross-wavelength correlations through a shared input kernel multiplied by an output covariance matrix. Independent GPs over each channel provide a simpler baseline. The review by Wakabayashi and Otsuka notes that correlated models require sufficient data to estimate inter-output covariance reliably and that deep-kernel learning can transform raw spectra into learned features before GP uncertainty quantification (wakabayashi2608bayesianoptimizationfor pages 16-17, wakabayashi2608bayesianoptimizationfor pages 17-19).

**Multivariate variance aggregation.** Lu et al. (DOI: 10.48550/arxiv.2606.17233) proposed a generalized multi-output criterion that aggregates normalized variance contributions across all outputs into a single acquisition decision, balancing exploitation (high uncertainty) with exploration (distance from existing data). They suggest applying PCA to spectral outputs, performing active learning on dominant components, and mapping back to wavelength space (lu2606uncertaintyquantificationof pages 15-17).

**Classical optimal design criteria.** D-optimality (maximizing the determinant of the Fisher information matrix) and A-optimality (minimizing the trace of the inverse) remain relevant for selecting informative formulations to train spectral models. Wang et al. (DOI: 10.1002/aidi.202500087) discuss these covariance-based criteria alongside Bayesian optimal experimental design (BOED) that maximizes expected information gain about model parameters (wang2025advancedexperimentdesign pages 6-6). The review by Greenhill et al. (DOI: 10.1109/access.2020.2966228) surveys acquisition strategies including prediction variance, cross-validation disagreement, and ensemble disagreement as candidates for non-scalar-objective active learning (greenhill2020bayesianoptimizationfor pages 2-3).

## 6. Best Practice for Held-Out Blind Validation of Spectral Surrogate Models

Validation of spectral surrogate models requires careful attention to the data-generation structure. Three key principles emerge from the literature:

**Structure-aware data splitting.** Kenett et al. (DOI: 10.1002/asmb.2701) introduced befitting cross-validation (BCV), arguing that train/test splits should reflect the experimental design structure—batch, instrument, time, compositional regime—rather than relying on random row-wise partitioning. Observations linked by the same formulation family, instrument run, or experimental campaign should be kept together; splitting them artificially inflates apparent predictive performance (kenett2022self‐supervisedcross pages 2-5, kenett2022self‐supervisedcross pages 1-2, kenett2022self‐supervisedcross pages 13-15).

**Leakage-free preprocessing.** All preprocessing—feature standardization, baseline correction, dimensionality reduction—must be fitted solely on the training partition of each fold and then applied to the validation portion. This avoids information leakage and is critical when spectral data undergo normalization or smoothing (alqurashi2026comparativemachinelearningmodelling pages 10-15, alqurashi2026comparativemachinelearningmodelling pages 15-20).

**Grouped versus record-level validation.** Alqurashi and Khan (DOI: 10.1038/s41598-026-68034-3) demonstrated the consequences of ignoring grouping: record-level 10-fold cross-validation gave R² ≈ 0.45, while leave-one-source-out validation—holding out all measurements from each independent study—dropped to R² ≈ 0.05 (alqurashi2026comparativemachinelearningmodelling pages 26-34, alqurashi2026comparativemachinelearningmodelling pages 34-36). This dramatic difference indicates that intra-source correlations can dominate apparent performance, and the appropriate validation strategy depends on the intended deployment scenario (new samples from an existing batch vs. entirely new material systems). For spectral surrogates of formulations, the strongest recommendation is to reserve a final held-out test set composed of genuinely unseen composition families or experimental batches and use grouped k-fold cross-validation for model selection and hyperparameter tuning (kenett2022self‐supervisedcross pages 13-15, kenett2022self‐supervisedcross pages 15-16).

## 7. Future Directions and Open Challenges

Several directions emerge as high-priority gaps:

1. **Integrated cost-and-composition-constrained BO.** While simplex constraints and cost-aware acquisition exist separately, their joint treatment for reagent-budget-limited mixture optimization remains ad hoc. Future work should unify sum-to-one composition constraints with per-reagent cost functions in a single acquisition framework.

2. **Multi-output BO on spectral objectives.** Most published BO campaigns reduce spectra to scalar figures of merit before optimization. Directly optimizing spectral shape—e.g., maximizing a spectral overlap integral—while accounting for wavelength-resolved uncertainty is an open methodological challenge (liang2021benchmarkingtheperformance pages 3-6, wakabayashi2608bayesianoptimizationfor pages 19-21).

3. **Active learning for spectral model improvement on the simplex.** The combination of composition-space constraints, high-dimensional spectral outputs, and information-theoretic acquisition criteria (e.g., mutual information or expected model change across all wavelengths) has not been demonstrated in published formulation campaigns. This represents a clear gap between the AL/OED literature and practical formulation science.

4. **Standardized benchmarks for formulation BO.** The benchmarking review by Adesiji et al. (DOI: 10.1039/d5dd00337g) notes that acceleration factors are sensitive to endpoint definition, noise level, and landscape shape, making cross-study comparisons unreliable (adesiji2026benchmarkingselfdrivinglabs pages 7-10, adesiji2026benchmarkingselfdrivinglabs pages 10-11). Community-standard mixture-formulation benchmarks—with known optima, realistic noise, and simplex constraints—would substantially improve the field's ability to evaluate methods.

5. **Validation protocols for small-data spectral surrogates.** When only tens to low hundreds of formulations are available, grouped leave-one-batch-out validation may have high variance. Bayesian approaches to uncertainty quantification in the surrogate itself—e.g., posterior predictive checks—may complement cross-validation when group counts are small (kenett2022self‐supervisedcross pages 13-15).
