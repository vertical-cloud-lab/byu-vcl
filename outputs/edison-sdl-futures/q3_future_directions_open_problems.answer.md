# q3_future_directions_open_problems

## Question

What do recent (2023-2026) reviews, perspectives and roadmap papers identify as the OPEN PROBLEMS and FUTURE DIRECTIONS for self-driving laboratories, specifically in materials chemistry and electrochemistry? Give DOIs. I want the concrete, named challenges rather than generalities: orchestration/interoperability standards (SiLA2, LabOP, AnIML, Bluesky, Chemputer/chi), FAIR data and metadata capture, reproducibility and round-robin benchmarking between labs, the shortage of negative results, closing the loop with multiple ORTHOGONAL characterization modalities rather than a single scalar objective, multi-fidelity and multi-objective optimization, transfer/meta-learning across chemistries, human-in-the-loop and error recovery, and cost/accessibility. ## Answer

# Open Problems and Future Directions for Self-Driving Laboratories in Materials Chemistry and Electrochemistry (2023–2026)

## Overview

A systematic survey of recent reviews, perspectives, and roadmap papers (2023–2026) reveals a clear consensus: while self-driving laboratories (SDLs) have demonstrated impressive domain-specific capabilities—from nanoparticle synthesis to electrocatalyst screening—the transition from narrow, single-loop demonstrations to general-purpose, networked, trustworthy autonomous discovery platforms remains incomplete. The SDL 2.0 vision articulated by Lee et al. (DOI: 10.1039/d5mh01984b) defines six pillars—interoperability, collaboration, generalizability, orchestration, safety, and creativity—all of which remain aspirational rather than realized (lee2026towardselfdrivinglaboratory pages 17-19, lee2026towardselfdrivinglaboratory pages 1-2). The comprehensive Chemical Reviews survey by Tom et al. (DOI: 10.1021/acs.chemrev.4c00055) and the Trustworthy Autonomous Science roadmap by Silva et al. (DOI: 10.2172/3377973) further confirm that the field has not yet achieved end-to-end, experimentally self-validating autonomous discovery (silva2026towardtrustworthyautonomous pages 6-9, tom2024selfdrivinglaboratoriesfor pages 7-8, tom2024selfdrivinglaboratoriesfor pages 56-57).

Below, the nine concrete named challenges are assessed individually. **All nine remain unsolved as of 2026**, although each has seen partial progress.

## 1. Orchestration and Interoperability Standards (SiLA2, LabOP, AnIML, Bluesky, Chemputer/XDL)

**Status: UNSOLVED.** No universally adopted standard spans instruments, workflow descriptions, analytical formats, and orchestration layers. Tom et al. identify the absence of standardized APIs from instrument manufacturers as a fundamental bottleneck, presenting SiLA2 as a promising device-communication protocol modeled on ROS but requiring much broader adoption (tom2024selfdrivinglaboratoriesfor pages 7-8). Lee et al. recommend SiLA and OPC UA for standardized interfaces alongside modular, open-source orchestration software, while acknowledging that hardware-specific communication protocols, absence of shared digital infrastructure, and lack of common calibration benchmarks continue to impede scalability and reproducibility (lee2026towardselfdrivinglaboratory pages 17-19, lee2026towardselfdrivinglaboratory pages 20-21). Steensen et al. (DOI: 10.1002/aidi.202500067) document that materials-science ontologies remain immature and that semantic data integration has in practice been post hoc rather than built into live campaigns (steensen2026thenecessityof pages 3-3, steensen2026thenecessityof pages 2-3). Jiang et al. (DOI: 10.4208/cicc.2025.90.03) specifically call for universal communication protocols such as SiLA2 and AnIML to standardize cross-platform compatibility (jiang2025recentprogressin pages 11-12). Critically, syntactic portability of a protocol file does not guarantee chemically equivalent execution across heterogeneous platforms—a point emphasized as a fundamental reproducibility risk (hao2026doselfdrivinglaboratories pages 4-5).

## 2. FAIR Data and Metadata Capture

**Status: UNSOLVED.** Data remain fragmented across bespoke instrument-specific files and laboratory-specific schemas. Gauthier et al. (DOI: 10.1039/d5dd00297d) demonstrate a FAIR research data infrastructure for the Swiss Cat+ platform using Matryoshka files, ontology-driven metadata modeling, ASM-JSON analytical outputs, RDF/Turtle conversion, and SHACL validation—but this remains a single-site exemplar rather than a community standard (gauthier2025afairresearch pages 3-5, gauthier2025afairresearch pages 1-2, gauthier2025afairresearch pages 2-3). Tali et al. (DOI: 10.1039/d5dd00175g) present SEARS, a cloud-native platform for multi-lab FAIR data, but similarly serve as a proof of concept. Hao et al. (DOI: 10.51847/i178allztd) emphasize that a reusable experimental record must bind raw analytical traces to reagent identities, batch IDs, preparation history, environment, calibration state, software/model versions, branching decisions, manual interventions, and failures—metadata that current SDL records routinely omit (hao2026doselfdrivinglaboratories pages 6-8). Steensen et al. confirm that ontologized data integration into optimization campaigns has not been achieved systematically (steensen2026thenecessityof pages 3-3).

## 3. Reproducibility and Round-Robin Benchmarking Between Labs

**Status: UNSOLVED.** No accepted multi-lab round-robin protocol for SDLs exists. Hao et al. call for prospective challenge sets involving noise, constraints, missing measurements, failed runs, distributional change, and long-duration campaigns that reveal instrument drift, reagent degradation, software changes, and maintenance burdens—none of which have been standardized (hao2026doselfdrivinglaboratories pages 6-8, hao2026doselfdrivinglaboratories pages 8-10, hao2026doselfdrivinglaboratories pages 8-8). Silva et al. (DOI: 10.2172/3377973) highlight that LLM-based orchestration is nondeterministic, making run-to-run reproducibility difficult, and that verification infrastructure has not kept pace with agent capabilities; they recommend end-to-end reproduction benchmarks, uncertainty quantification, and full provenance recording of prompts, responses, decisions, data, tools, and model versions (silva2026towardtrustworthyautonomous pages 17-18, silva2026towardtrustworthyautonomous pages 15-17). Mallia and Caputo (DOI: 10.69709/caic.2026.133382) propose cross-platform transfer benchmarks and reference datasets for stress-testing candidate schemas, but note that poisoning-resistant model-exchange protocols and formal inter-lab protocols remain absent (mallia2026aiguidedselfdrivinglaboratories pages 22-27). Lee et al. call for reference experiments and calibration materials but do not describe established round-robin procedures (lee2026towardselfdrivinglaboratory pages 19-20).

## 4. Shortage of Negative Results

**Status: UNSOLVED.** Chemical datasets disproportionately retain successful outcomes. Gauthier et al. explicitly note that chemical datasets emphasize successes while omitting failed experiments, and their infrastructure is designed to capture both raw data and metadata for every experimental step including failures (gauthier2025afairresearch pages 1-2). Hao et al. emphasize that failed or negative experiments must be interpretable and systematically captured, distinguishing genuine null chemistry from analytical or robotic failure—an important distinction that current systems rarely make (hao2026doselfdrivinglaboratories pages 6-8, hao2026doselfdrivinglaboratories pages 8-8, hao2026doselfdrivinglaboratories pages 4-5). The SDL community has not established incentive structures, data formats, or publication venues that routinely encourage sharing of negative autonomous-experiment outcomes, despite their importance for model training and avoiding selection bias.

## 5. Closing the Loop with Multiple Orthogonal Characterization Modalities

**Status: UNSOLVED.** Most operational SDLs optimize a single scalar objective (e.g., photoluminescence peak, conductivity, yield). Wakabayashi and Otsuka (DOI: 10.48550/arXiv.2608.26016) frame multimodal characterization—XRD, spectroscopy, microscopy, electrochemistry, process logs—as a major open problem requiring data fusion, uncertainty calibration, and decision models that can handle heterogeneous noise, cost, delay, scale, and missingness across modalities (wakabayashi2608bayesianoptimizationfor pages 1-3, wakabayashi2608bayesianoptimizationfor pages 45-48). Müller et al. (DOI: 10.48550/arXiv.2609.04564) demonstrate this concretely: a zero-yield result was ambiguous until the agent accessed raw NMR records, reconstructed the chemical-shift axis, and distinguished a real absence of product from an analytical failure—illustrating that scalar optimization collapses the evidence needed for diagnosis (muller2026laagente\optimatowards pages 11-13). Foadian et al. (DOI: 10.1021/acsmaterialsau.4c00096) emphasize that closing the characterization loop requires identifying rapid, reliable proxy signals that connect high-dimensional measurements to material compositions and functionalities (foadian2024fromsunlightto pages 8-9). Hao et al. note that molecular plausibility, synthesis, assay activity, mechanism, cellular relevance, and stability are successive but non-equivalent evidence layers that a single scalar objective cannot represent (hao2026doselfdrivinglaboratories pages 8-8).

## 6. Multi-Fidelity and Multi-Objective Optimization

**Status: UNSOLVED.** Wakabayashi and Otsuka provide the most detailed treatment, identifying that low-fidelity measurements are valuable only when sufficiently correlated with the high-fidelity target and inexpensive relative to the information they provide; weak correlation or systematic bias can waste the experimental budget (wakabayashi2608bayesianoptimizationfor pages 26-28). Acquisition functions must jointly balance expected improvement against source-specific costs and determine when further low-fidelity sampling is no longer worthwhile. For multi-objective campaigns, Pareto hypervolume methods become costly, scalarization can compress trade-offs, and batch methods must handle conflicting properties, feasibility constraints, and asynchronous execution (wakabayashi2608bayesianoptimizationfor pages 16-17, wakabayashi2608bayesianoptimizationfor pages 1-3). Hippalgaonkar et al. (DOI: 10.21203/rs.3.rs-3578558/v1) developed EGBO specifically to handle constrained multi-objective problems in SDLs, confirming that standard acquisition functions are insufficient. The battery domain adds long cycling times and substantial cell-to-cell variability, making exhaustive exploration impractical (wakabayashi2608bayesianoptimizationfor pages 40-42, wakabayashi2608bayesianoptimizationfor pages 42-44).

## 7. Transfer/Meta-Learning Across Chemistries

**Status: UNSOLVED.** Transfer is promising within closely related campaigns (e.g., across precursor batches for quantum-dot synthesis, yielding at least twofold acceleration) but unproven across genuinely different chemical systems (hao2025autonomousmaterialssynthesis pages 53-56). Wakabayashi and Otsuka warn that negative transfer is a realistic risk when source and target systems differ in descriptors, objectives, scales, or failure semantics (wakabayashi2608bayesianoptimizationfor pages 33-36, wakabayashi2608bayesianoptimizationfor pages 31-33). Experimental drift—source depletion, chamber evolution, catalyst aging, batch replacement, calibration changes—can make nominally comparable measurements non-transferable, requiring time-, state-, or change-point-aware surrogate models and reference experiments to assess whether historical data remain reusable (wakabayashi2608bayesianoptimizationfor pages 45-48). Mallia and Caputo identify the cold-start problem and platform specialization as fundamental barriers, proposing foundation models and transfer learning as solutions subject to data-poisoning risks (mallia2026aiguidedselfdrivinglaboratories pages 17-22). Bennett et al. (DOI: 10.1038/s41467-026-74425-x) note that transfer learning and descriptor-based approaches represent future directions for their autonomous catalysis platform (muller2026laagente\optimatowards pages 9-11). Systematic meta-learning demonstrations across genuinely different chemistries remain scarce.

## 8. Human-in-the-Loop and Error Recovery

**Status: UNSOLVED.** Cheng et al. (DOI: 10.20944/preprints202608.0273.v1) provide the most detailed treatment, arguing that autonomous systems must handle silent execution failures, instrument drift, biological variability, sample mix-ups, incomplete troubleshooting, and discrepancies between protocol intent and actual execution (cheng2026towardshumanledagentdriven pages 7-8, cheng2026towardshumanledagentdriven pages 8-11). They call for continuous verification, observability, provenance logs, quality-control artifacts, anomaly feedback, recovery mechanisms, abstention, and escalation to human judgment. Hao et al. emphasize that platforms may lack the perception and adaptability required for unfamiliar samples and failure states, and that co-design must establish admissible actions, stopping rules, and escalation pathways before autonomous operation begins (hao2026doselfdrivinglaboratories pages 6-8, hao2026doselfdrivinglaboratories pages 8-8). Lee et al. note that SDL safety requires contextual hazard detection, predictive diagnostics, automatic recovery, safety-aware planning, and real-time mitigation for unattended operation—capabilities beyond simple threshold alarms (lee2026towardselfdrivinglaboratory pages 17-19). Silva et al. frame reliable human override, screening, auditing, and independent measurements as essential research concerns, noting that agents perform near or below chance on some reproducibility benchmarks (silva2026towardtrustworthyautonomous pages 17-18, silva2026towardtrustworthyautonomous pages 15-17).

## 9. Cost and Accessibility

**Status: UNSOLVED.** Cost remains a decisive barrier to broad adoption. Lo et al. (DOI: 10.1039/d3dd00223c) document the full range: from frugal-twin setups at ~$50–$450 (e.g., MIK-I at ~$450, Jubilee at ~$2,000) to research-grade platforms at $10,000–$30,000, up to industrial systems like the Maholo LabDroid at ~$890,000 (lo2024reviewoflowcost pages 22-23, lo2024reviewoflowcost pages 5-6, lo2024reviewoflowcost pages 6-7, lo2024reviewoflowcost pages 10-11, lo2024reviewoflowcost pages 4-5). A benchtop NMR alone costs ~$40,000, and robust low-cost alternatives to NMR or HPLC-MS under $10,000 do not currently exist (lo2024reviewoflowcost pages 6-7). Mallia and Caputo describe current SDLs as requiring multi-million-euro bespoke investments concentrated in elite institutions, and propose Laboratory-as-a-Service, cloud access, secure tele-operation, standardized experiment descriptions, metered pricing, and open-source hardware to broaden equitable access (mallia2026aiguidedselfdrivinglaboratories pages 22-27). Jiang et al. recommend decentralized resource-sharing, cloud simulation, and shared equipment hubs to reduce capital and operating costs for smaller institutions (jiang2025recentprogressin pages 11-12). Lo et al. emphasize that affordability is context-dependent: prices considered low-cost in developed countries may be unaffordable in the Global South due to limited funding, delivery expenses, and foreign-exchange difficulties (lo2024reviewoflowcost pages 4-5). Multi-fidelity strategies—where inexpensive frugal-twin SDLs explore the parameter space before costly high-fidelity systems refine promising regions—are proposed as a practical cost-reduction mechanism (lo2024reviewoflowcost pages 17-18).

## Conclusion: All Nine Challenges Remain Unsolved

Based on this survey of 15+ recent reviews, perspectives, and roadmap papers, **all nine named challenges remain open problems as of 2026**. None is described as fully resolved by any source consulted. Partial progress includes: SiLA2 and OPC UA gaining adoption as device-level protocols; Matryoshka files and SEARS as FAIR-data exemplars; EGBO and related algorithms for constrained multi-objective optimization; within-campaign transfer learning for nanocrystal synthesis; HeinSight 4.0 for vision-guided error detection; and frugal-twin systems for democratized prototyping and education. However, the gap between these individual advances and the integrated, interoperable, reproducible, accessible, and trustworthy SDL 2.0 envisioned by the community remains substantial.
