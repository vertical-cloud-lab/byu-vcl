# Survey of Low-Cost, Open-Source, and DIY Mechanical Test Frames for Autonomous Self-Driving Laboratories in Metallurgy and 3D-Printed Materials Research

## 1. Open-Source / Open-Hardware Tensile and Compression Testing Machines

Several recent open-source mechanical testing designs have been documented, though none yet demonstrate full standards compliance for metals testing (ASTM E8/E8M or ISO 6892-1).

**MT-02 (Demmel, 2024, TU Wien).** This is the most capable recent open-source UTM identified. It uses a 40×80 mm aluminum profile frame, two NEMA 17 stepper motors with 19.203:1 gearboxes driving twin SFU 1605 ball-screw assemblies (500 mm spindles), and a 5 kN load cell. The controller is Arduino-based with a touchscreen display. The rated load capacity is 3.5 kN. Displacement is inferred from motor steps at 5 Hz logging. Testing is displacement-controlled at speeds such as 1 mm/min, with 30–40 N preload. Crucially, validation against a ZwickRoell RetroLine with video extensometer showed that tensile strength agreed within ~1%, but the crosshead-derived strain differed by approximately a factor of two, causing a **48% underestimate of Young's modulus** (demmel2024designmanufacturingand pages 1-6, demmel2024designmanufacturingand pages 64-68). The design is open-source using FreeCAD, KiCad, PrusaSlicer, and Arduino IDE, though the total BOM cost was not explicitly stated (demmel2024designmanufacturingand pages 25-30). It references DIN 50125 and ISO 527-1 specimen geometries but does not claim ASTM E8 or D638 compliance (demmel2024designmanufacturingand pages 13-17).

**DIY-EMTT (Wiranata et al., 2024, HardwareX).** This modular design targets stretchable/soft materials. It integrates a NEMA 23 stepper motor with TB6560 driver on a 500 mm aluminum linear stage, a 10 kg (≈98 N) load cell with HX711 amplifier, a VL53L0X time-of-flight displacement sensor, and a built-in resistance meter for simultaneous electromechanical testing. Four Arduino Nano boards coordinate with a LabVIEW GUI. The stated BOM cost is **US$173.28**, and designs are released under CC BY-SA 4.0 via a Mendeley Data repository. GUI speeds of 500, 200, and 100 mm/min are associated with JIS K 6251 / ASTM D412 standards for elastomers, but ASTM E8 or D638 compliance is not demonstrated (wiranata2024economicallyviableelectromechanical pages 9-11, wiranata2024economicallyviableelectromechanical pages 3-6, wiranata2024economicallyviableelectromechanical pages 1-3).

**Camelot Micro-Extensometer (Trozzi et al., 2025, BMC Biology).** This is a low-cost system designed for plant biomechanics rather than engineering materials, but its architecture is instructive. It uses interchangeable parallel-beam load sensors (from ~10 g to several kg capacity, ≈£10 each; the 10 g sensor provides ~10 µN resolution), a stepper motor with a 1 mm pitch screw producing 5 µm steps, and camera-based optical tracking providing ~5 µm displacement resolution. The actuator costs approximately £50. The open-source MorphoRobotX software runs on Linux (trozzi2025camelotacomputerautomated pages 2-4, trozzi2025camelotacomputerautomated pages 1-2).

**Arduino UTM (Geasa, 2021).** A minimal design using a steel frame, NEMA 23 stepper motor (19 kg·cm torque, 1.8° step angle), TB6600 driver, lead-screw actuator, and a load cell with Arduino logging. Total cost was below 1,000 Egyptian pounds (approximately US$20–30 at the time). Displacement is controlled through motor steps with no independent sensor (geasa2021developmentofan pages 1-3).

An additional notable design is the **open-source flexible material tensile testing apparatus by Woods et al. (2024)** published in the journal *Hardware* (DOI: 10.3390/hardware2010002), though full details could not be retrieved in this search.

## 2. In-Situ / Miniature Tensile Stages

**Commercial in-situ stages.** The best-documented commercial stage in recent literature is the **Deben MT2000EH**, a single-leadscrew miniature tensile/compression stage designed for use inside SEM, optical microscopy, AFM, and XRD systems. As characterized by the UK National Physical Laboratory (NPL), it features a 2 kN load cell with ±0.1% full-scale accuracy, supports tensile, compression, and horizontal bending modes, and uses stainless-steel slide bearings with specimens mounted on 3.25 mm tool-steel rods. NPL found its system compliance to be approximately 0.8 µm/N when using dowel-pin fixturing, reducing to <0.001 µm/N with a very stiff specimen (koko2024uncertaintiesofit pages 13-17, koko2024uncertaintiesofit pages 8-13). The Deben was validated against an Instron 5969: peak loads differed by only ±0.8 N for lattice specimens with five and two unit cells, and by ~4.6 ± 2.2 N for single-cell specimens (koko2024uncertaintiesofit pages 23-28). A separate assessment of a 5 kN single-leadscrew rig (likely also Deben/NewTec family) found that recorded versus image-tracked displacement showed only R² = 0.79, representing more than 20% inconsistency (koko2024uncertaintiesofit pages 31-35).

Other major commercial in-situ stage manufacturers include **Deben Microtest** (200 N to 10 kN range; typical price ~$15,000–50,000+), **Kammrath & Weiss** (tensile/compression/bending stages for SEM, 1–10 kN, with symmetric dual-actuator designs that keep the gauge section stationary), **Psylotech** (µTS micro-tensile stages, 100 N–5 kN), **MTI Instruments SEMtester** (various load ranges), **Linkam TST350** (temperature-controlled tensile stage for optical/X-ray, ≤2.2 kN), **Alemnis** (micro/nano-mechanical testing in SEM), and **Bruker/Hysitron PI series** (nanomechanical testing). Price bands typically range from ~$15,000 for basic stages to >$100,000 for fully featured systems with environmental control.

**Symmetric/dual-opposed actuator designs** that keep the gauge section stationary during testing are commercially available from Kammrath & Weiss and some Psylotech models. This is particularly important for in-situ DIC or SEM observation, where the region of interest should remain in the field of view throughout deformation.

**Open-source in-situ stages** specifically designed for SEM or microscopy use were not identified in the 2024–2026 literature search, representing a significant gap. The Camelot system (Trozzi et al., 2025) could potentially be adapted for microscopy-compatible use given its compact form factor and camera-based tracking.

## 3. Miniature and Sub-Size Specimens for AM Metals

### Small Punch Test (SPT)

The small punch test per **EN 10371:2021** uses a miniature disc specimen of **8 ± 0.1 mm diameter and 0.5 ± 0.005 mm thickness**, clamped between dies and loaded by a hemispherical punch (1.25 mm radius) at 0.5 mm/min until rupture (simonovski2025prequalificationofadditively pages 1-2, keshavlal2023investigationofmechanical pages 11-16, keshavlal2023investigationofmechanical pages 8-11). Yield strength is estimated from the elastic-plastic transition force F_e using the empirical correlation σ_YS = β_y × F_e / h₀², where the EN 10371 best-fit constant is β_y = 0.510 for steels with YS between 200–1000 MPa. Ultimate tensile strength is estimated from maximum force F_m and displacement at F_m. Recent work has improved these correlations to achieve R² > 0.95 with errors limited to ±10% (keshavlal2023investigationofmechanical pages 8-11).

For **additively manufactured 316L stainless steel**, Simonovski et al. (2025) used SPT creep testing at 650°C and found good correlation between SPT and uniaxial creep for time-to-rupture, but identified that LPBF 316L exhibits multiple minima in deflection-rate curves—unlike the single minimum in forged 316L—attributed to possible micro-cracking, which challenges the EN 10371 interpretation procedure (simonovski2025prequalificationofadditively pages 1-2).

Cannon et al. (2026) demonstrated SPT repeatability on cold-drawn mild steels, using sub-size ASTM E8 dog-bone specimens (8 mm outer diameter, 4 mm reduced diameter, 16 mm gauge length) as reference. They found EN8 had YS = 289 MPa, UTS = 574 MPa, while EN3B had YS = 413 MPa, UTS = 719 MPa (cannon2026evaluationofthe pages 3-4).

### Size Effects

The thickness-to-grain-size ratio (t/d) is critical for SPT validity: ratios below 25 produce unrepresentative results due to limited geometrically necessary dislocations and low-strain substructural effects. Tested steels had t/d ratios of 45–74, considered representative (cannon2026evaluationofthe pages 3-4). Decreasing SPT disk thickness from 0.5 to 0.2 mm increased measured strength in Incoloy alloy, attributed to grain-size and dislocation-confinement effects (cannon2026evaluationofthe pages 2-3). A heterogeneous spatial distribution of strain emphasizes the critical requirement for metallographically homogeneous samples (cannon2026evaluationofthe pages 1-2).

### Sub-Size ASTM E8 Specimens for AM

Sub-size ASTM E8/E8M tensile specimens (e.g., 4 mm reduced diameter, 16 mm gauge length) are routinely used for AM metals, as evidenced by Kumar et al. (2026) testing AA6061 from additive friction stir deposition and Tello et al. (2026) testing ER308L WAAM stainless steel. These are important because AM build volumes often cannot accommodate full-size specimens, and sub-size specimens enable localized property mapping across a build.

## 4. Automated, Robot-Compatible, and SDL-Integrated Mechanical Testing

### LLNL APEX Platform

The most advanced documented self-driving laboratory for metallurgy is **APEX (Autonomous Alloy Prediction and EXperimentation)** at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory, developed with Cornell University. APEX combines robotic handling, directed-energy-deposition (DED) additive manufacturing, automated grinding and polishing, optical microscopy, diamond indentation for hardness, and compression testing. The system can process up to **48 samples per day** with data collected throughout fabrication, preparation, and testing, fed into a Materials Acceleration Platform (MAP) where machine-learning tools guide subsequent compositions (conn2025selfdrivinglabto pages 1-8). A vibration sensor on the grinding machine converts the operator's tactile judgment into numerical process data. APEX is initially being trained on stainless-steel alloys and is planned to become extensible by 2027 (conn2025selfdrivinglabto pages 8-8).

### Autonomous AM Laboratory Concepts

Collins and Ales (2026) describe an AM-enabled autonomous laboratory concept integrating agentic AI with Model Context Protocol servers, in-process sensors (cameras, eddy-current sensors, laser-induced phased arrays), and experimental cells for manufacturing, machining, and testing. For mechanical screening, they specifically advocate **hardness indentation and strain-hardening rate as accelerated surrogates for ultimate tensile strength**, avoiding the time cost of full tensile testing (collins2026additivemanufacturingenabled pages 18-20). Mallia and Caputo (2026) proposed a three-axis taxonomy (Material System State, Hardware Autonomy Level, Software Autonomy Level) for classifying SDLs; bulk metallurgy platforms like APEX are classified as MSS-3/HAL-2/SAL-2 (mallia2026aiguidedselfdrivinglaboratories pages 6-12).

### Robot-Assisted Experimental Mechanics

Strohmann et al. (2023) demonstrated the closest approach to self-driving mechanical testing, combining a servo-hydraulic fatigue test rig (sinusoidal cycling at 20 Hz, 4.5–15 kN) with a **KUKA LBR iiwa collaborative robot** carrying a Zeiss STEMI 206C optical microscope for high-resolution DIC. The cobot autonomously positions the microscope at crack-tip regions identified by convolutional neural networks (ParallelNets for crack-tip detection, UNetPath for crack-path detection) operating on global GOM Aramis 12M DIC data. The hardware is fully automated for uniaxial testing and has been adapted for biaxial rigs. CrackPy, an open-source Python library, manages the entire workflow (strohmann2023towardsselfdrivinglabs pages 1-4, strohmann2023towardsselfdrivinglabs pages 4-6, strohmann2023towardsselfdrivinglabs pages 9-11, strohmann2023towardsselfdrivinglabs pages 6-9).

### High-Throughput Nanoindentation Mapping

Chawla et al. (2026) proposed **random combinatorial libraries** where heterogeneous metallic machining particulates are embedded in conductive epoxy, creating a specimen with hundreds to thousands of discrete compositions in one mount. These are mapped by SEM-EDS (15 kV, 6.4 nA, 20–30 minute acquisitions) and characterized by automated nanoindentation using a KLA iMicro system at 200 nm target depth and 0.2 strain rate. Each indent takes approximately **5 seconds**, with a 5×5 grid requiring ~1280 seconds including positioning (~45 s/indent). Bayesian optimization with Gaussian-process surrogates guides site selection, and the approach is expected to provide **orders-of-magnitude throughput improvement** over sequential library approaches (chawla2026randomcombinatoriallibraries pages 6-9, chawla2026randomcombinatoriallibraries pages 13-16, chawla2026randomcombinatoriallibraries pages 16-18, chawla2026randomcombinatoriallibraries pages 9-13). High-throughput nanoindentation on diffusion multiples and sputtered combinatorial libraries has also been demonstrated for alloy screening (mooraj2023areviewon pages 28-30).

### Key Findings

**Crosshead displacement vs. extensometry.** The single most important accuracy lesson is that crosshead displacement cannot reliably measure strain for modulus determination. The MT-02 open-source UTM underestimated Young's modulus by 48% compared to a commercial machine with video extensometry, because crosshead displacement includes deformation of the load train, grips, and fixtures (demmel2024designmanufacturingand pages 1-6, demmel2024designmanufacturingand pages 64-68). Meyer et al. (2023) demonstrated that a compliance correction ΔL = ΔX − C_total × P, combined with an effective gauge-length correction, can bring crosshead-derived strain into agreement with extensometer strain within the linear elastic region (up to ~0.5% strain for aluminum and ~1% for PA6), but the correction breaks down at larger strains (meyer2023testmethodsfor pages 6-8, meyer2023testmethodsfor pages 5-6). The machine compliance measured by Meyer et al. was 2.5 µm/N using a stiff steel reference specimen (meyer2023testmethodsfor pages 10-11).

**In-situ stage compliance.** The Deben MT2000EH in-situ rig showed approximately 0.8 µm/N compliance when using dowel-pin specimen fixturing, with the initial bedding-in displacement masking the elastic response. Independent image tracking confirmed that during approximately the first 100 seconds, rig displacement increased while specimen elongation remained negligible (koko2024uncertaintiesofit pages 23-28, koko2024uncertaintiesofit pages 13-17). Clamped grips (as on an Instron) showed a much more rapid initial force rise (koko2024uncertaintiesofit pages 23-28).

**Alignment effects.** A 2° specimen misalignment in the Deben rig changed the fracture mode from tensile to shear, even though the UTS changed only marginally (46.8 to 46.6 MPa). This demonstrates that UTS agreement alone cannot validate alignment quality (koko2024uncertaintiesofit pages 28-31). ASTM E1012 classifies alignment quality by the ratio of bending strain to axial strain; for metals testing per ASTM E8/ISO 6892-1, maximum permissible bending strain is typically 5% of the axial strain.

**Dimensional uncertainty.** For small AM specimens, diameter variation of up to 70 µm led to an estimated 14% uncertainty in calculated stress (koko2024uncertaintiesofit pages 17-23). This becomes increasingly problematic as specimens miniaturize.

**Practical recommendations for DIY frames in an SDL context:** (1) Always include independent extensometry or DIC—a camera-based system or clip-on extensometer is essential; (2) Characterize and subtract load-train compliance using a stiff reference specimen; (3) Use clamped rather than pinned grips; (4) Measure every specimen's cross-section individually; (5) Verify alignment under preload; (6) Include a warm-up period and tare procedure to address load-cell drift; (7) Consider nanoindentation hardness as a high-throughput surrogate for tensile properties in early screening, with full tensile testing reserved for promising candidates.

## Summary and Recommendations for SDL Integration

For an autonomous self-driving laboratory performing high-throughput metallurgy and 3D-printed materials research, the following architecture emerges from the literature:

1. **Screening tier:** Use automated nanoindentation mapping on polished cross-sections or combinatorial libraries, as demonstrated by Chawla et al. (2026), to rapidly screen compositions at ~45 seconds/indent with Bayesian optimization guiding site selection (chawla2026randomcombinatoriallibraries pages 6-9, chawla2026randomcombinatoriallibraries pages 16-18).

2. **Intermediate tier:** Use small punch testing (SPT per EN 10371) with 8 mm × 0.5 mm disc specimens to estimate yield and tensile strength from minimal material, particularly valuable for AM builds where material is limited (simonovski2025prequalificationofadditively pages 1-2, keshavlal2023investigationofmechanical pages 11-16).

3. **Validation tier:** Use sub-size ASTM E8 tensile specimens with a properly instrumented load frame (>5 kN capacity, video extensometer or DIC, clamped grips) for quantitative stress-strain characterization of selected compositions.

4. **Automation:** The APEX platform (Conn & Baker, 2025) demonstrates that closed-loop DED → grinding → characterization workflows achieving 48 samples/day are feasible (conn2025selfdrivinglabto pages 1-8). Robot-assisted DIC positioning with cobots (Strohmann et al., 2023) shows how mechanical testing can be integrated with autonomous data collection (strohmann2023towardsselfdrivinglabs pages 1-4, strohmann2023towardsselfdrivinglabs pages 9-11).

5. **Open-source hardware:** Current open-source UTMs (MT-02 at ~3.5 kN, DIY-EMTT at ~98 N) are not yet adequate for metals testing without significant upgrades—particularly the addition of independent extensometry and higher-capacity load cells. The 48% modulus error from crosshead displacement alone underscores that any SDL-integrated tensile frame must include DIC or a video extensometer (demmel2024designmanufacturingand pages 1-6, demmel2024designmanufacturingand pages 64-68).

No fully open-source, SDL-ready mechanical testing platform with closed-loop Bayesian optimization driving composition selection through tensile testing has been published as of late 2026. This represents a significant gap and opportunity in the field.
