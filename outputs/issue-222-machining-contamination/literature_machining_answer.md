# Machining contamination of 6063 cups for Al–Si atomization

**Bottom line.** Published measurements show that an apparently wiped aluminum surface can retain oil at tens to roughly 150 mg/m², but they do **not** establish the residual loading after the particular IPA/Kimwipe procedure described here. For a cup having 60 cm² of exposed surface, 1 mg/m² corresponds to 6 µg per cup, or 0.12 ppm by mass in a 50 g cup. Oil is likely to outgas or decompose rather than enter the powder quantitatively; oxide, entrained solids, and some carbon- or hydrogen-bearing reaction products are more credible surviving hazards. There is no defensible published *per-cup* survival fraction for this process. (powell1995surfaceinspectionusing pages 1-2, powell1995surfaceinspectionusing pages 2-4, gobber2025productionandproperties pages 2-4)

## (a) Cutting-fluid films and wiping

An FTIR study provides **analogues, not measurements on drilled 6063**. Applying oil to aluminum foil and thoroughly wiping off the excess with *dry tissue* left approximately **150 mg/m²** (15 µg/cm²). An aluminum rod contaminated with silicone lubricant gave **49–77 mg/m²** by two spectral bands; a mineral-oil stain on sandblasted 7075 corresponded to approximately **280 mg/m²**. The same investigators reported detection of hydrocarbon films around **1 mg/m²** under favorable conditions. None of those values is an IPA-wipe endpoint or a representative distribution for an uncontrolled shop lathe. (powell1995surfaceinspectionusing pages 4-5, powell1995surfaceinspectionusing pages 7-9, powell1995surfaceinspectionusing pages 2-4, powell1995surfaceinspectionusing pages 1-2)

| Surface loading, illustrative | µg/cm² | Residue on 60 cm² cup | If divided by 50 g cup | If four cups supply 200 g melt |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 mg/m², approximately the favorable FTIR detection level | 0.10 | 6 µg | 0.12 ppm | 0.12 ppm |
| 49–77 mg/m², silicone on an aluminum rod | 4.9–7.7 | 0.294–0.462 mg | 5.9–9.2 ppm | 5.9–9.2 ppm |
| 150 mg/m², oil after *dry-tissue* wiping of foil | 15 | 0.90 mg | 18 ppm | 18 ppm |
| 280 mg/m², deliberate mineral-oil stain on 7075 | 28 | 1.68 mg | 34 ppm | 34 ppm |

The arithmetic is **total surface residue divided by metal mass**, not measured dissolved carbon, hydrogen, or final-powder concentration. For the actual charge, use the **measured** masses of the cups, plugs, and contained powder: the stated 50 g per cup and approximately 200 g per four-cup run are approximate. An estimated film thickness can also be misleading inside a blind, rough bore: pooled coolant or trapped chips need not resemble a uniform film. (powell1995surfaceinspectionusing pages 4-5, powell1995surfaceinspectionusing pages 7-9, powell1995surfaceinspectionusing pages 1-2)

**After IPA/Kimwipes:** no study retrieved measured µg/cm² remaining after that procedure on an equivalently deep aluminum bore. IPA may remove accessible, soluble oil, but wiping does not certify removal of recessed pools, dried water-soluble-coolant additives, adherent inorganic particles, or native oxide. As a useful *contrast*, optimized **distillation of 7075 chips removed 99.7% of oil and water**; that result cannot be assigned to a brief IPA wipe. Neither “zero residue” nor a particular cleaning percentage is supported for these cups. (chen2025recentadvancesin pages 4-5, powell1995surfaceinspectionusing pages 2-4, taborelli2020cleaningandsurface pages 10-12)

## (b) Tool metal, built-up edge, and shop particles

A dry-drilling study on **harder, Si-rich die-cast AlSi9Cu3**, using W6Mo5Cr4V2 HSS drills, documented adhesive, abrasive, and severe thermal wear. It measured drill-flank wear after batches of **90 holes**, but reported a normalized wear map—not a reliable drill **mass loss per hole** or Fe/W/Mo/Cr/V transferred into each workpiece. Its numerical wear-map indices must not be interpreted as milligrams of HSS in a cup. The observed material adhering to the drill was **aluminum from the workpiece**. In another Al–Si drilling study, adhered aluminum on an uncoated HSS drill reached a reported **342 mg after 49 holes**; this is aluminum *on the tool*, emphatically **not** HSS deposited in 49 parts. Neither test predicts a mass-loss rate for drilling soft 6063 with a 12.7 mm drill. (zhang2001wearmechanismmaps pages 1-2, zhang2001wearmechanismmaps pages 2-4, bhowmick2007neardrymachiningof pages 139-142, bhowmick2007neardrymachiningof pages 118-121)

WC–Co wear could introduce W and Co if a carbide turning tool chips or wears, whereas the specified HSS bore drill principally poses Fe and its actual grade-specific alloying elements; **Co is not universal to HSS**. Steel/brass swarf or particles from shared equipment can add Fe, Cu, and Zn. Aluminum-hole studies directly describe smeared metal and adhering debris; however, no applicable study retrieved quantifies WC–Co transfer, entrapped steel/brass swarf, or SiC/Al₂O₃ grit per cleaned 6063 cup. These are plausible *discrete-particle* risks, especially after filing or emery use, not justified ppm estimates. A single **1 mg** particle retained in **200 g** of charge would represent **5 ppm of that particle’s mass** if fully incorporated; its constituent-element ppm would depend on its composition and retention. Because a particle may remain as an inclusion, even a low bulk ppm need not establish LPBF acceptability. (zhang2001wearmechanismmaps pages 4-5, zhang2001wearmechanismmaps pages 2-4, bhowmick2007neardrymachiningof pages 118-121)

## (c) Altered aluminum surface

Fresh aluminum rapidly passivates; an often-reported room-temperature air-film range is **2–5 nm**. Specific Al–Mg alloy surfaces measured by XPS carried approximately **4 nm** and **7 nm** films. These are analogues, not a measured oxide thickness on this machined 6063. Water can change *chemistry as well as thickness*: aluminum micropowder film thickness was approximately **5–6 nm** in a dry/control condition and approximately **8 nm** after exposure to **95% relative humidity**, with hydroxide/OH evidence; 50% humidity did not measurably increase its thickness. Brief hydration of Al–Mg surfaces also changed their outer layer toward hydroxides. IPA wiping cannot undo that oxide. (lienhard2020surfaceoxideand pages 4-6, scottosheriff1999physicochemicalcharacterisationof pages 2-4, scottosheriff1999physicochemicalcharacterisationof pages 7-8, czech2010hydrogengenerationfrom pages 23-27)

A water-based coolant therefore adds a credible route to hydrated oxide, retained salts/additives, and localized staining **if it remains wet or dries on the metal**; it does not imply that every coolant-treated cup acquires an 8 nm film or significant corrosion. A finishing investigation describes aluminum’s approximate passive **pH 4–9** range, chloride-associated pitting, effects of copper-containing rinse water, and halo-like corrosion where coolant dries. Separately, drilling **319 Al–Si** produced a **15–20 µm** near-surface fragmentation layer in one condition and damage extending to **80 µm** with severe chip clogging. These are workpiece damage depths in a different alloy and cutting regime, **not** a measured cold-worked depth or new chemical contaminant inventory in 6063; cold work itself loses its deformed microstructure on complete melting. (bhowmick2007neardrymachiningof pages 118-121, fullen2014aluminumsurfacefinishing pages 3-7)

A simple scale calculation puts the oxide in perspective: taking **60 cm²**, a *uniform* **2–5 nm Al₂O₃** layer, density approximately **3.9 g/cm³**, and Al₂O₃ oxygen mass fraction **0.471** gives approximately **22–55 µg oxygen per cup**. If all four films’ oxygen entered a **200 g** melt, the *maximum mass-balance contribution under these assumptions* would be approximately **0.44–1.1 ppm O**. Roughness, patchy/hydrated films, other exposed surfaces, oxide removal into dross, and the powder already inside the cups invalidate this as a measured melt-O increment. The calculation underscores why a small **particle or retained coolant deposit**, rather than a pristine native film, may dominate contamination from machining. (scottosheriff1999physicochemicalcharacterisationof pages 2-4, czech2010hydrogengenerationfrom pages 23-27)

## (d) What remelting can—and cannot—remove

Pump-down favors removal of **volatile** IPA, water, and lighter hydrocarbons *provided they can escape before the sealed or tightly packed charge melts*. The small **1 mm vent** and a powder-filled, blind cavity make this a process-dependent condition, not evidence of complete degassing. Nonvolatile lubricant additives, salts, and grit will not evaporate merely because the chamber was pumped down. Upon heating, residual organics can volatilize or decompose; water and hydrated surface species can supply hydrogen through reactions with aluminum. Insoluble oxides may collect in surface dross or be entrained as films/particles; an argon backfill is not a chemical deoxidizer. Aluminum-chip recycling literature explicitly requires cutting-fluid removal and warns about gases during uncleaned remelting, but gives no IPA-wipe-to-powder survival percentage. (chen2025recentadvancesin pages 2-4, chen2025recentadvancesin pages 4-5, gobber2025productionandproperties pages 2-4)

There is **direct but non-identical feedstock-to-powder evidence**. In one vacuum-induction/argon **gas-atomization** comparison, 20–63 µm powder from Al–Si casting-alloy scrap contained **0.023 ± 0.006 wt% C**, versus **0.013 ± 0.003 wt% C** from ingot; oxygen was **0.036 ± 0.010** versus **0.049 ± 0.016 wt%**, respectively, and H was approximately **5 ppm in both**. For a *different*, modified AA2024 alloy, scrap/ingot powders contained **0.021 ± 0.005/0.011 ± 0.001 wt% C**, **0.068 ± 0.013/0.048 ± 0.013 wt% O**, and **53 ± 13/17 ± 3 ppm H**. Authors suspected scrap process fluids as one cause of elevated carbon, while noting some contamination entered slag. Starting scrap and ingot compositions differed; these are **associations, not a controlled oil-dose experiment**, and the atomizer was not this BN-washed graphite/40 kHz ultrasonic apparatus. They show why neither “vacuum removes everything” nor “all surface oil becomes carbon in powder” follows from evidence. (gobber2025productionandproperties pages 2-4, gobber2025productionandproperties pages 1-2)

Likewise, oxygen is not interchangeable with hydrogen or carbon: some oxide is separated, some can remain entrained, and fresh droplets subsequently oxidize on exposure. Hydrogen is a porosity concern because its solubility falls on solidification, while oxide films/inclusions can act as defect sites. A moisture-exposure/remelting investigation measured hydrogen increases for its specific aluminum specimens, including **0.04 ppm H** on heating commercially pure Al in air and **0.279 ppm H** for Mg-containing 5083 in air; these values are **not** predicted pickup in the present charge. Powder-processing studies provide a closer LPBF warning: AlSi10Mg reused for more than 30 months rose from roughly **0.05 to 0.12 wt% O** and **80 to 130 ppm H**, with part porosity changing from **<1% to approximately 3%** under virgin-powder-qualified parameters. In a separate, deliberately severe **400 °C/96 h air-aging** test, powder oxygen rose from **0.072 to 0.274 wt%** and manufactured-part porosity from **3.52 to 6.50%**; oxygen aging, changed laser absorption, and process behavior confound a simple causal attribution to oxygen alone. These are **powder aging/reuse**, not tests of dirty cups. (fedina2022influenceofalsi10mg pages 4-5, fiegl2021effectofalsi10mg0.4 pages 1-2, fiegl2021effectofalsi10mg0.4 pages 3-4, fedina2022influenceofalsi10mg pages 6-8, gerrard2014inclusionsandhydrogena pages 357-360)

**Carbon/carbide qualification:** a graphite/Si–Al melt study found graphite attack and carbide formation at **1500 °C** under vacuum, not at **800–840 °C** in a BN-washed crucible. It does not establish an Al₄C₃ production rate here, nor prove that residual WD-40 or cutting oil converts quantitatively to Al₄C₃. A BN wash may reduce direct graphite contact, but its actual coverage and integrity matter. Abrasive **Al₂O₃** should be treated as a possible refractory inclusion; **SiC** can remain particulate or react depending on melt contact and residence time. No retrieved measurement establishes the amount surviving this particular nozzle/sonotrode route. (hoseinpur2020mechanismsofgraphite pages 1-5)

## (e) Alloy chemistry versus powder oxygen

A published table of generally used **AA6063 compositional limits** gives **Si 0.20–0.60 wt%, Mg 0.45–0.90 wt%, Fe <0.35 wt%, Cu <0.10 wt%, Mn <0.10 wt%, and Cr <0.10 wt%**. Published AA4047 work identifies its characteristic **11–13 wt% Si**; one actual AA4047 wire contained **11.85% Si, 0.199% Fe, and 0.006% Cu**. Those **wire analyses are not AA4047 impurity limits**: the exact 4047 Fe/Cu maximum applicable to the purchased AA4047 powder should be checked against its certificate and governing specification rather than inferred from that wire. Nor do 6063 *limits* reveal the composition of the particular 6063 bar. (filippov2021microstructureandcorrosion pages 2-4, hsu2020processperformanceanalysis pages 1-3, kolubaev2022micromesoand pages 7-8)

**Most importantly, the cups are part of the charge, not just contaminated containers.** Using the question’s approximate masses, **200 g of 6063 cups/plugs** and **36–56 g of 4047 powder** would produce approximately **78–85 wt% 6063** before accounting for any other metal. Its nominal Si concentration would then be only approximately **1.9–3.5 wt%** if the 6063 contains 0.20–0.60% Si and the 4047 contains 11–13% Si—not Al–12Si. The range combines endpoints and is an arithmetic illustration, not a melt analysis. Even at the 6063 Fe ceiling, cups could contribute up to approximately **0.27–0.30 wt% Fe to the combined charge**; at its Cu ceiling, up to approximately **0.078–0.085 wt% Cu**, *before* contributions from 4047 or foreign swarf. By contrast, **1 mg** of fully incorporated pure Fe in a **200 g** run is **5 ppm Fe = 0.0005 wt%**. Bulk alloy mixing can therefore outweigh ordinary trace tool wear, while a localized steel/brass or abrasive inclusion remains a separate defect issue. If “~200 g total” instead denotes *all* metal including the 4047, reweighing each input is essential before applying those illustrative fractions. (filippov2021microstructureandcorrosion pages 2-4, hsu2020processperformanceanalysis pages 1-3, kolubaev2022micromesoand pages 7-8)

Finally, the powder’s **own surface area** is much larger than the cups’. A spherical-particle calculation for 200 g of aluminum-density material, density approximately **2.7 g/cm³** and diameter **15–45 µm**, gives approximately **10–30 m²** of powder surface, against approximately **0.024 m²** for four nominal 60 cm² cups. With a hypothetical uniform **3–5 nm Al₂O₃** shell, oxygen would be approximately **0.06–0.30 wt%** across those diameter/thickness endpoints; *not* a measured value for this Al–Si lot. Direct hot-gas-extraction data provide the better benchmark: fresh AlSi10Mg was **0.067 wt% O** in one study and approximately **0.05 wt% O** in another, whereas gas-atomized Al–Si casting-alloy powder was **0.036–0.049 wt% O** in the scrap/ingot comparison. These are **hundreds of ppm oxygen in powder**, versus the approximately **0.4–1.1 ppm O upper-bound arithmetic** for four idealized pristine cup oxides. The cup calculation must not be read as an upper bound for coolant corrosion, inclusions, air exposure during atomization, or the original **36–56 g** of 4047 powder; obtain an actual O/N/H/C analysis of both incoming and produced size fractions for this system. (fedina2022influenceofalsi10mg pages 5-6, fiegl2021effectofalsi10mg0.4 pages 3-4, fedina2022influenceofalsi10mg pages 4-5, gobber2025productionandproperties pages 2-4)

**Evidence limit:** No located publication measures the combined sequence—uncontrolled manual turning/deep HSS drilling of 6063, the stated IPA/Kimwipe wipe, BN-washed graphite melting at 800–840 °C, and ultrasonic atomization—by surface extraction, tool mass balance, and before/after powder O/H/C/metal analysis. Thus the film examples and geometric ppm conversions are useful *scale estimates*, not a defensible prediction of actual contaminant concentrations or final LPBF porosity. (chen2025recentadvancesin pages 4-5, zhang2001wearmechanismmaps pages 1-2, gobber2025productionandproperties pages 2-4)
