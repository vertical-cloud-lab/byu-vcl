# Vibratory Polishing of LPBF AlSi10Mg with Diamond Suspension: Literature Review and Practical Guidance

## 1. Do the Literature and Manufacturer Guidance Recommend Diamond Suspensions on Vibratory Polishers?

**Short answer: No.** The published literature and manufacturer application notes overwhelmingly recommend **colloidal silica** (or, less commonly, alumina) as the vibratory final-polishing medium. No published example of a water-based polycrystalline diamond suspension used on a vibratory polisher for aluminum or Al-Si alloys was identified in this review.

Buehler's own application guidance (Blann and Vander Voort, *Contemporary Concepts in Specimen Preparation*) specifies MasterMet colloidal silica on a VibroMet II for 30 minutes as the final step for low-hardness and copper-based alloys, producing publication-quality micrographs (blannUnknownyearcontemporaryconceptsin pages 2-3, blannUnknownyearcontemporaryconceptsina pages 2-3). Vander Voort's EBSD preparation guide lists MicroCloth with 0.05 µm MasterMet colloidal silica on a VibroMet 2 for ≥20 minutes as an optional sixth step, improving EBSD band contrast by at least 10% (voort2006metallographicpreparationfor pages 2-3). MacKenzie and Totten's *Analytical Characterization of Aluminum, Steel, and Superalloys* recommends vibratory polishing with colloidal silica, acidic alumina, or neutral alumina for difficult specimens, noting that these produce "the highest quality results" (mackenzie2005analyticalcharacterizationof pages 46-48, mackenzie2005analyticalcharacterizationof pages 48-50). For diamond, the same text advises that when using diamond on a rotary polisher, the cloth should first be charged with diamond paste and lubricated, then diamond suspension added during the cycle—but this guidance pertains to rotary, not vibratory, systems (mackenzie2005analyticalcharacterizationof pages 48-50).

The PACE Technologies GIGA 0900 vibratory polisher was documented in published research using 50 nm colloidal silica for 12 hours on Al6061 (zeng2022microstructureevolutionof pages 2-3) and similar silica-based protocols in other studies, but none used diamond suspension.

The single documented exception involving diamond on a vibratory polisher is the U-10Mo (uranium–molybdenum alloy) protocol of Prabhakaran et al. (2016), which uses **1 µm monocrystalline diamond paste in lapping oil** (not water-based diamond suspension) on a nylon cloth for 24 hours on a Buehler VibroMet 2, followed by a second 24-hour step with 50/50 colloidal silica/DI water (prabhakaran2016u10mosamplepreparation pages 30-34, prabhakaran2016u10mosamplepreparation pages 34-38). This is a fundamentally different system from a water-based 0.1 µm diamond suspension: the oil carrier, the paste format, and the much larger particle size (1 µm vs. 0.1 µm) all alter embedding risk and lubrication behavior.

**Critically, Vander Voort's study of cast Al-Si alloys specifically warns against diamond suspensions on aluminum alloys.** An experimental three-step procedure using 9 and 1 µm diamond suspensions produced "heavily embedded" diamond in the aluminum-alloy surfaces, particularly in the primary α-Al phase (voort1999preparationofcasta pages 3-5, voort1999preparationofcast pages 3-5, voort1999preparationofcasta pages 2-3). By contrast, 9 µm polycrystalline diamond slurry (MetaDi Supreme) followed by 1 µm diamond paste avoided the observed embedding (voort1999preparationofcasta pages 3-5, voort1999preparationofcast pages 3-5). This is a strong caution against using 0.1 µm diamond suspension on Al-Si alloys, even on a rotary polisher, let alone for hours on a vibratory polisher.

**Reports on LPBF AlSi10Mg:** Published LPBF AlSi10Mg studies generally describe "standard metallographic procedures" involving grinding and diamond polishing on rotary systems, but none document vibratory final polishing with diamond suspension for this alloy.

**Specific parameter guidance, drawing on all available sources:**

- **Amplitude (% setting on VibroMet 2):** No retrieved source reports a numerical VibroMet 2 amplitude percentage. The instrument manual (not directly available in the literature) provides the calibration; in practice, the specimen must have enough vibratory excursion to slide and slowly rotate in the bowl. A moderate setting (typically reported anecdotally as 30–50%) is used; start low and increase only enough to achieve smooth, random specimen motion (jr.1958preparationofmetallographic pages 1-8).

- **Run time:** Published colloidal-silica vibratory polish times for aluminum range from ≥20 minutes (voort2006metallographicpreparationfor pages 2-3) to 30 minutes (blannUnknownyearcontemporaryconceptsina pages 2-3), 2–4 hours (smith1993acomparisonof pages 4-7), and up to 12–24 hours (zeng2022microstructureevolutionof pages 2-3, prabhakaran2016u10mosamplepreparation pages 34-38). For a 0.1 µm diamond step following 1 µm alumina, an initial trial of 1–2 hours is prudent, with endpoint inspection every 30 minutes.

- **Specimen weight/load:** Smith et al. used 400 g per specimen (smith1993acomparisonof pages 4-7). The Prabhakaran protocol uses a Buehler weighted specimen holder (part 67-1527) but does not report the mass (prabhakaran2016u10mosamplepreparation pages 34-38). Long and Gray (1958) state that the specimen must be heavy enough that its inertia causes slippage during the bowl's spring-back but not so heavy that it fails to rotate (jr.1958preparationofmetallographic pages 1-8). For a 1.25-inch Bakelite mount, the Buehler weighted holder or added weights of 200–400 g total are typical.

- **Suspension amount and top-up:** The DeHoff protocol fills the bowl with a 50/50 mixture of colloidal silica and DI water (dehoff2020qualityproceduresfor pages 98-102). For WPC-0110 diamond suspension, contact PACE to confirm if dilution is permitted; many water-based diamond suspensions are designed for use at supplied concentration. Charge the cloth generously so that the surface is uniformly wet, and top up as evaporation dictates (check hourly for multi-hour runs).

- **Cloth choice:** For aluminum alloys, low-nap or napless cloths are preferred to minimize relief (voort2011metallographicspecimenpreparation pages 1-2, remond2002implicationsofpolishing pages 7-9). Specific cloths used on vibratory polishers include nylon (prabhakaran2016u10mosamplepreparation pages 30-34), MicroCloth (voort2006metallographicpreparationfor pages 2-3), ChemoMet (blannUnknownyearcontemporaryconceptsin pages 3-4), MasterTex (dehoff2020qualityproceduresfor pages 98-102), and Final B (prabhakaran2016u10mosamplepreparation pages 34-38). For the user's 0.1 µm diamond suspension, a low-nap synthetic cloth (e.g., ChemoMet or a similar PSA-backed final polish pad) would minimize relief while retaining the diamond.

- **Endpoint:** The practical endpoint is a scratch-free surface at 20× optical magnification (jr.1958preparationofmetallographic pages 8-20). More rigorously, the surface should be free of visible 1 µm alumina scratches under reflected-light DIC at 200–500×. Inspect periodically by removing the specimen, rinsing, and examining. For EBSD-quality surfaces, Vander Voort recommends verifying by band-contrast improvement (voort2011metallographicspecimenpreparation pages 1-2).

Key highlights from the evidence:

**Diamond embedding** is the most critical documented risk. Vander Voort demonstrated that fine diamond suspensions produced heavy abrasive embedment in cast Al-Si alloys, with particles visible chiefly in the soft α-Al phase (voort1999preparationofcasta pages 3-5, voort1999preparationofcast pages 3-5). This embedding problem was particularly severe with fine particle sizes. The risk with 0.1 µm polycrystalline diamond is, by analogy, high and potentially worse than with 1 µm because finer particles can embed more readily. Paste formulations and oil-based carriers reduce this risk relative to water-based suspensions (voort1999preparationofcasta pages 3-5, voort1999preparationofcast pages 3-5).

**Relief around Si particles** depends on cloth nap and polishing conditions. In cast Al-Si alloys, relief around primary silicon was produced by nylon, Ultra-Pad, and Trident cloths; switching to complementary rotation and lower-nap surfaces improved flatness (voort1999preparationofcasta pages 2-3, voort1999preparationofcast pages 2-3). In LPBF AlSi10Mg, the eutectic Si network is much finer than cast primary Si, but the same differential polishing mechanism applies.

**Carbon contamination for EDS** is a significant concern. Rémond et al. (2002) documented carbon-bearing contamination layers on polished mineral surfaces and demonstrated that polishing procedures affected quantitative X-ray microanalysis accuracy (remond2002implicationsofpolishing pages 9-12, remond2002implicationsofpolishing pages 12-14). Embedded diamond particles are pure carbon and will produce spurious C Kα signals, especially at 5 kV where the interaction volume is shallow. The polishing medium itself, whether diamond or colloidal silica, affects the surface oxide and contamination layer within the EDS sampling volume (remond2002implicationsofpolishing pages 1-3, remond2002implicationsofpolishing pages 19-21, remond2002implicationsofpolishing pages 14-15).

**Corrosion/pitting in long water-based runs:** Aluminum is reactive in aqueous media. Vander Voort warned against copper or copper-alloy polishing platens for aluminum due to galvanic copper deposition (voort1999preparationofcast pages 1-2). During multi-hour water-based polishing, localized corrosion or oxide formation is possible. Colloidal silica (pH ~9.5) actively polishes through a chemical-mechanical mechanism that may protect the surface, whereas a neutral-to-acidic diamond suspension may not. The pH and inhibitor package of WPC-0110 should be verified.

**Suspension drying and evaporation:** Over 4–5 hours, significant evaporation can occur in an open vibratory bowl. The DeHoff procedure used 50/50 colloidal silica/DI water to prevent drying (dehoff2020qualityproceduresfor pages 98-102). With diamond suspension, drying can cause local dry polishing, scratching, and abrasive concentration changes. Covering the bowl (if the instrument design permits) or hourly top-up is advised.

## 4. Cleaning the Bowl and Cloth When Changing Abrasives, and Cloth Storage

**Bowl and equipment cleaning when switching abrasives:** No specific published protocol for changing a vibratory polisher bowl from colloidal silica or alumina to diamond was identified. However, general cross-contamination prevention principles are well established:

- Residual colloidal silica can produce streaks or white haze if allowed to dry on equipment; it should be rinsed promptly with cold water, as hot water crystallizes the silica and may require repolishing (westin2025enhancingmicrostructuralanalysis pages 9-10). An ethanol wipe can remove persistent residues.
- Wiping with tissue or fabric can reintroduce fibers or static charge, which is especially problematic for SEM specimens (westin2025enhancingmicrostructuralanalysis pages 9-10).
- The bowl should be thoroughly flushed with deionized water, wiped clean with lint-free wipes, inspected for dried residues (which appear as a white haze), and allowed to dry before introducing the diamond suspension.
- Dedicated cloths must be used for each abrasive size and type to prevent cross-contamination (prabhakaran2016u10mosamplepreparation pages 23-27, prabhakaran2016u10mosamplepreparation pages 27-30). Never use the same cloth for both colloidal silica and diamond.

**Cloth storage between runs:** Prabhakaran et al. recommend storing used polishing cloths in properly labeled storage bags, using separate bags for cloths charged with different abrasive types and sizes (prabhakaran2016u10mosamplepreparation pages 23-27, prabhakaran2016u10mosamplepreparation pages 27-30). For diamond-charged cloths, the cloth should be rinsed gently with the recommended carrier fluid (or DI water for water-based suspensions), wrapped in clean polyethylene, and labeled with the abrasive type and date. Vander Voort noted that MgO-charged cloths should be washed and stored in dilute 1:1 aqueous HCl to prevent carbonate formation (voort1999preparationofcast pages 1-2), although this specific guidance applies to MgO, not diamond. For colloidal silica cloths, Vander Voort recommends stopping silica addition ~20 seconds before the end of the cycle and turning on water for the last 10 seconds (voort1999preparationofcast pages 5-5, voort1999preparationofcasta pages 5-5). Colloidal silica that dries on a cloth is essentially irreversible, so the cloth should either be stored wet (sealed) or discarded after drying.

**Specimen cleaning after vibratory polishing:** After removal from the vibratory polisher, specimens should not be allowed to dry with suspension on the surface. Rinse immediately with DI water, follow with ethanol or isopropanol, and dry with warm filtered air or clean compressed gas (westin2025enhancingmicrostructuralanalysis pages 9-10, prabhakaran2016u10mosamplepreparation pages 34-38). Ultrasonic cleaning in DI water for 2–5 minutes is strongly recommended before SEM-EDS to remove any loosely attached abrasive or slurry residues (prabhakaran2016u10mosamplepreparation pages 23-27).

## Summary Assessment

Using a 0.1 µm water-based polycrystalline diamond suspension (PACE DIAMAT WPC-0110) on a VibroMet 2 for LPBF AlSi10Mg is an **undocumented, non-standard procedure** that carries substantial and well-documented risks of diamond embedding, carbon contamination for EDS, and surface relief around the eutectic Si network. The published literature strongly favors colloidal silica for the vibratory final-polish step on aluminum alloys, and Vander Voort's Al-Si work explicitly warns against fine diamond suspensions on these materials. If this approach is pursued, it should be treated as experimental, validated against colloidal-silica controls, and carefully monitored for embedding by BSE imaging and EDS at the intended analysis conditions.
