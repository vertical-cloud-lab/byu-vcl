### Question 1: Does 0.1 µm Water-Based Diamond Suspension Require the Same Continuous Alcohol Spray Handling as Colloidal Silica?

**Short answer: No.** The urgent, continuous-spray protocol used for colloidal silica is chemically unnecessary for a water-based diamond suspension. The explanation follows from the fundamentally different surface chemistries of the two abrasive systems.

#### Mechanism of Colloidal Silica Drying, Gelation, and Bonding

Colloidal silica for metallographic polishing consists of amorphous SiO₂ nanoparticles (typically 20–600 nm) suspended in an alkaline aqueous medium at pH 9.5–10 (mackenzie2005analyticalcharacterizationof pages 43-46, remond2002implicationsofpolishing pages 7-9). The bonding and gelation mechanism proceeds through the following stages:

1. **Sol-gel condensation of silanols.** The surface of each SiO₂ nanoparticle is covered with silanol (Si–OH) groups. When the water carrier evaporates, increasing the local particle concentration, adjacent silanols undergo condensation reactions to form siloxane (Si–O–Si) bridges, releasing water (gaishun2008solgelmethodsfor pages 1-5, landau2008sol–gelprocess pages 7-9, landau2008sol–gelprocess pages 4-6, cini2019solgelcoatingsfor pages 42-46). This is the same fundamental chemistry used to produce sol-gel coatings and ceramic films.

2. **pH dependence.** The condensation rate is strongly pH-dependent. At the metallographic operating pH of ~9.5–10, silica is more soluble and condensation/dissolution equilibria are displaced so that polycondensation is rapid once evaporation concentrates the sol, producing a highly cross-linked particulate gel (landau2008sol–gelprocess pages 7-9, cini2019solgelcoatingsfor pages 42-46, cini2019solgelcoatingsfor pages 38-42). The alkaline conditions ensure that many surface silanols are deprotonated (SiO⁻), which accelerates nucleophilic attack on adjacent Si atoms and promotes rapid network formation.

3. **Particle size and surface area.** The nanoscale diameter of colloidal silica particles gives them extremely high specific surface areas (hundreds of m²/g) and a large density of reactive surface silanols per unit mass of abrasive (rahman2012synthesisofsilica pages 4-5, rahman2012synthesisofsilica pages 1-2). This means that even a thin residual film contains a very large number of reactive sites available for condensation and bonding.

4. **Si–O–metal bonding.** A freshly polished aluminum surface is covered by a hydroxylated native oxide (Al–OH groups on the surface of the amorphous Al₂O₃ layer). Silanol groups from the colloidal silica can hydrogen-bond to these surface hydroxyls, and under evaporative concentration, condensation to form interfacial Si–O–Al linkages is chemically plausible by analogy with sol-gel film adhesion on aluminum and silicon substrates (gaishun2008solgelmethodsfor pages 1-5, gaishun2008solgelmethodsfor pages 5-9). Although direct experimental quantification of Si–O–Al bond formation during metallographic polishing residue drying was not found in the primary literature, the general sol-gel adhesion framework is well established and explains the observed strong adhesion of dried silica to polished metal.

5. **Gelation and drying.** As water evaporates, the colloidal particles form a three-dimensional gel network throughout the liquid. Continued drying increases cross-linking, the gel stiffens, and capillary stresses develop; the final product is a dense xerogel film that is mechanically and chemically bonded to the surface (landau2008sol–gelprocess pages 7-9, cini2019solgelcoatingsfor pages 42-46). This film appears as the characteristic white haze and is notoriously difficult to remove once dried (mackenzie2005analyticalcharacterizationof pages 43-46). On polishing cloths, dried silica crystallizes and produces scratching on subsequent specimens (mackenzie2005analyticalcharacterizationof pages 43-46, remond2002implicationsofpolishing pages 7-9).

#### Why Diamond Suspension Does Not Share This Behavior

A water-based polycrystalline diamond suspension (such as PACE DIAMAT WPC-0110) contains discrete diamond particles in water with trace polyethylene glycol (PEG, ≤0.1%) and surfactant (≤0.2%), and no silica or alumina.

- **Diamond is chemically inert at room temperature.** Diamond surfaces do not possess silanol groups and cannot undergo silanol condensation. The diamond–metal interface in the absence of extreme temperature and carbide-forming elements exhibits primarily van der Waals interactions and mechanical interlocking, not covalent chemical bonding (zhao2018areviewof pages 1-3, zhao2018areviewof pages 3-5). Strong diamond–metal bonding requires elevated temperatures and specific elements (Ti, Cr, Mo, W) that form carbides at the interface (zhao2018areviewof pages 3-5). At room temperature, adhesion of diamond particles to an aluminum surface is weak and physical rather than chemical (sumant2007surfacechemistryand pages 7-8).

- **No sol-gel network formation.** When the water carrier evaporates from the diamond suspension, the diamond particles simply deposit loosely on the surface. There is no hydrolysis, no condensation, no cross-linking, and no gel or xerogel formation. The deposit does not harden or bond covalently to the substrate.

- **PEG and surfactant residues.** The ≤0.1% PEG and ≤0.2% surfactant are water-soluble organic compounds. If dried, they leave a thin organic film that can be readily dissolved and removed by water or alcohol rinse. They do not form covalent bonds with aluminum oxide surfaces under these conditions and do not produce the hard, adherent, inorganic deposit characteristic of dried colloidal silica. No primary literature was found that specifically documents PEG or surfactant residue from metallographic diamond suspensions on aluminum; however, the chemistry of these water-soluble polymers and surfactants is well understood and fundamentally different from sol-gel silica.

- **Residue removal.** A dried water-based diamond suspension residue, if it occurs, should consist of loose diamond particles plus a thin organic (PEG/surfactant) film. Prompt water rinsing should remove the vast majority of this material. If deliberately allowed to dry, gentle swabbing with detergent or a brief ultrasonic treatment should be effective. This is qualitatively much easier than removing dried colloidal silica, which requires detergent scrubbing and sometimes repolishing (mackenzie2005analyticalcharacterizationof pages 43-46, westin2025enhancingmicrostructuralanalysis pages 9-10).

**Evidence gap:** No direct experimental comparison of dried residue removal difficulty for diamond suspension versus colloidal silica on aluminum alloys was identified in the primary literature. The conclusions above are based on established sol-gel chemistry and the known inertness of diamond surfaces at room temperature.

### Question 2: Recommended Cleaning Sequence After the Final Diamond Step

#### Literature and Manufacturer Guidance

The metallographic preparation literature provides cleaning guidance primarily for colloidal silica, which is the more problematic abrasive. The same principles apply to diamond suspension cleaning, but with less urgency and severity.

**Buehler (MacKenzie & Totten 2005; Blann & Vander Voort, Buehler Tech-Notes):** For colloidal silica, the recommended protocol is to stop adding abrasive 15–20 seconds before the end of polishing, direct a water jet onto the cloth with ~10 seconds remaining, then rinse specimens under running water, squirt ethanol onto surfaces to displace residual water, and blow dry under hot air. Cotton soaked in detergent solution can remove persistent white haze, though the water-flush procedure usually makes this unnecessary (mackenzie2005analyticalcharacterizationof pages 43-46). For aluminum alloys specifically, Buehler's procedure (Table 4 in the Blann & Vander Voort Tech-Notes) uses the same final step (MASTERMET colloidal silica on ChemoMet cloth), with no aluminum-specific cleaning variation noted beyond the standard protocol (blannUnknownyearcontemporaryconceptsina pages 3-4).

**Westin et al. (2025), best practices for duplex stainless steels and AM deposits:** The recommended cleaning sequence after polishing is: (1) rinse under running water using dishwashing soap with gentle rubbing using fine tissue or cotton; (2) if colloidal silica residue persists, rinse with cold water and wipe gently with ethanol-moistened soft cotton; (3) final rinse with deionized water; (4) final ethanol rinse to displace water and prevent water stains; (5) immediate drying with warm air or clean compressed air. Hot water should be avoided because it can crystallize silica. Ultrasonic cleaning in ethanol or isopropanol is described as highly effective for SEM preparation and useful for removing residual silica between steps (westin2025enhancingmicrostructuralanalysis pages 9-10, westin2025enhancingmicrostructuralanalysis pages 23-25).

**General metallographic practice (Rémond et al. 2002, NIST):** Surface composition and polishing residues can measurably affect quantitative X-ray microanalysis. Specimens should be analyzed immediately after preparation; the same polishing procedure should be used for both unknowns and standards (remond2002implicationsofpolishing pages 14-15, remond2002implicationsofpolishing pages 19-21). Polishing with colloidal silica is recognized as a chemo-mechanical process that modifies surface composition (remond2002implicationsofpolishing pages 7-9, remond2002implicationsofpolishing pages 19-21).

#### Recommended Cleaning Sequence for the Diamond Suspension Final Step on AlSi10Mg

Based on adapting the above guidance to a non-silica, water-based diamond suspension:

1. **Stop diamond suspension delivery** ~15–20 seconds before removing the specimen from the vibratory polisher. If the VibroMet 2 does not allow timed suspension cutoff, lift the puck gently while the bowl is still wet.
2. **Immediate water rinse:** Rinse the specimen face generously under a gentle stream of room-temperature deionized water while holding it face-down.
3. **Detergent swab:** Apply a small amount of dilute, pH-neutral laboratory detergent (e.g., Micro-90, Alconox Liquinox, or common dishwashing soap) to the face and gently swab with clean, lint-free cotton or a foam-tipped applicator to dislodge any residual diamond, PEG, or trapped debris from pores or the mount–specimen gap.
4. **Thorough DI water rinse:** Rinse thoroughly to remove all detergent.
5. **Alcohol displacement:** Squirt clean ethanol or isopropanol over the face to displace residual water.
6. **Immediate drying:** Blow dry with clean nitrogen or warm, filtered compressed air. Do not wipe the face after this step.
7. **Optional ultrasonic cleaning:** If residual particles are suspected (especially in LPBF porosity or mount gaps), immerse the specimen in DI water with a trace of neutral detergent in an ultrasonic bath for 30–120 seconds. Follow with DI water rinse, alcohol rinse, and immediate drying.

#### Is Methanol Specifically Needed?

**No.** Methanol is not uniquely required or specifically recommended over ethanol or isopropanol in any manufacturer application note or primary publication identified in this review. Ethanol is the solvent most frequently mentioned in the metallographic cleaning literature for water displacement and final rinsing (westin2025enhancingmicrostructuralanalysis pages 9-10, mackenzie2005analyticalcharacterizationof pages 43-46). Isopropanol is also explicitly recommended for ultrasonic cleaning steps (westin2025enhancingmicrostructuralanalysis pages 23-25). All three C₁–C₃ alcohols are effective at displacing water films, evaporating cleanly, and preventing water staining on aluminum. Ethanol and isopropanol are preferable to methanol on toxicity and safety grounds (methanol is acutely toxic by inhalation and skin absorption). The critical requirements are solvent purity (reagent or semiconductor grade), complete rinsing of prior cleaning solutions, and rapid evaporation to prevent staining — not the specific identity of the alcohol.

### Question 3: Does Water in the Suspension, or a Delay Before Cleaning, Cause Staining, Pitting, Hydroxide or Oxide Growth That Would Change Quantitative EDS?

#### Native Oxide Formation on Aluminum

Aluminum reacts essentially instantaneously with oxygen in air to form a thin, amorphous native oxide (Al₂O₃). At room temperature after polishing, this oxide reaches approximately 1.5 nm (15 Å) within hours and is self-limiting in dry air (olefjord1994surfaceanalysisof pages 1-2, olefjord1994surfaceanalysisof pages 6-7). XPS measurements on aluminum powder particles report passivation layer thicknesses of approximately 4–6 nm including some hydroxide contribution (li1997aninsitu pages 4-6, lienhard2020surfaceoxideand pages 3-4). This native oxide is unavoidable and forms before any EDS measurement can be performed.

#### Humid Air and Water Exposure

At 80% relative humidity, the native oxide on pure aluminum grows from ~15 Å to ~22 Å (2.2 nm) after one week, with the additional growth attributed to hydration of Al₂O₃ and formation of Al(OH)₃; this growth saturates and does not increase further after one month (olefjord1994surfaceanalysisof pages 1-2, olefjord1994surfaceanalysisof pages 2-3). On technically pure (alloy) aluminum, humidity also causes localized corrosion and hydroxide formation around Fe- and Si-containing intermetallic precipitates such as Al₃Fe and α-AlFeSi (olefjord1994surfaceanalysisof pages 1-2).

Immersion in liquid water at 40°C produces much more dramatic growth: a 2.5–3 µm boehmite layer plus an outer bayerite layer was measured after 100 hours, corresponding to a weight gain of 88 mg/dm² (thomas1993anevaluationof pages 5-6). At approximately 50°C for 112 days, hydroxide films of 2.7–5.8 µm were observed on Al 5052 alloy (dentremont2018preparationofaluminum pages 10-14, dentremont2018preparationofaluminum pages 14-18).

#### Practical Implications for Vibratory Polishing with Water-Based Diamond

During the 4–5 hours of vibratory polishing with the water-based suspension, the specimen face is continuously in contact with water. However, the surface is also being continuously abraded by diamond, which removes oxide as fast as it forms. The relevant oxidation concern is not during polishing but during the interval between removing the specimen from the bowl and completing the cleaning/drying sequence.

**At room temperature in ordinary laboratory air**, the native oxide re-forms within seconds to a thickness of ~2–4 nm and then grows extremely slowly (olefjord1994surfaceanalysisof pages 1-2, olefjord1994surfaceanalysisof pages 6-7). A delay of minutes to an hour before drying should not meaningfully increase the oxide thickness beyond the native level, provided the surface is not immersed in standing water.

**Standing water on the surface** is the primary risk. If a pool of water sits on the polished aluminum for extended periods (hours), hydroxide growth and localized corrosion become possible, particularly around Si particles and intermetallics. Staining and pitting can occur, especially on alloys with noble second-phase particles that set up galvanic cells (olefjord1994surfaceanalysisof pages 1-2).

#### Effect on Quantitative SEM-EDS at 5–15 kV

At 15 kV, the electron interaction volume in aluminum extends to roughly 2–3 µm in depth. A 2–5 nm native oxide represents less than 0.3% of this depth and contributes a negligible oxygen signal and an essentially immeasurable change in the Al, Si, or Mg concentrations. At 5 kV, the interaction volume shrinks to approximately 200–400 nm, and a surface layer becomes proportionally more significant. Even at 5 kV, however, a 2–5 nm native oxide is still a very small fraction (~1–2.5%) of the sampled depth, and EDS ZAF or phi-rho-z corrections do not explicitly account for a nanometer-scale surface layer; this is a systematic contribution that is present on both standards and unknowns and effectively cancels in standardized quantification.

The concern arises if: (a) a thicker-than-native oxide or hydroxide grows because of prolonged water contact or humid storage, or (b) the oxide/hydroxide is inhomogeneous (e.g., preferentially thick near Si or Fe-bearing intermetallic precipitates). In such cases, the O Kα signal could be elevated and Al concentration could be biased, particularly at low kV where the sampled volume is shallow (remond2002implicationsofpolishing pages 14-15).

**How quickly does this become a problem?** Based on the available data:

- **Seconds to minutes in air:** Only the unavoidable native oxide forms (~2–4 nm). No significant EDS impact.
- **Hours with a wet surface at room temperature:** Minimal additional growth beyond native oxide if promptly rinsed and dried. Possible localized staining if water pools.
- **Hours immersed in standing water at room temperature:** Hydroxide growth begins but remains thin (nanometers to tens of nanometers); risk of localized corrosion around intermetallics increases.
- **Days immersed at elevated temperature:** Micrometer-scale hydroxide films form; these would measurably affect EDS, especially at low kV (dentremont2018preparationofaluminum pages 10-14, thomas1993anevaluationof pages 5-6).

**Practical recommendation:** For quantitative EDS of LPBF AlSi10Mg at 5–15 kV, the operator should complete the water rinse → detergent swab → DI rinse → alcohol displacement → drying sequence within a few minutes of removing the specimen from the vibratory polisher. There is no need for a frantic continuous-spray protocol as with colloidal silica, but standing water should not be allowed to remain on the surface for extended periods. The specimen should be stored in a desiccator if there will be any delay before SEM analysis.

### Summary of Evidence Gaps

1. **No direct experimental study** was found comparing dried residue from a water-based diamond suspension versus dried colloidal silica on polished aluminum, including comparative EDS contamination measurements. The conclusions regarding diamond suspension residue are based on first-principles chemistry.

2. **No manufacturer application note** specific to PACE Technologies DIAMAT WPC-0110 cleaning on aluminum alloys was identified. The cleaning recommendations above are adapted from general metallographic best practices.

3. **No quantitative EDS study** was found that specifically measures the effect of a thin native Al₂O₃ film on quantitative Al, Si, Mg, or O results at 5–15 kV. The conclusion that the native oxide is negligible for bulk EDS at these voltages is based on the known interaction volume geometry and the film-to-volume thickness ratio.

4. **The equivalence of methanol, ethanol, and isopropanol** for final rinsing is supported by their similar physical properties and the absence of any literature requiring methanol specifically, but no controlled comparison on AlSi10Mg EDS outcome was found.
