## Query

Context: our lab in Provo, Utah (about 1,400 m elevation) makes and stores aluminum-alloy powders for additive-manufacturing research. A walk-in enclosure inside the lab is supplied with air from a refrigerant (compressor) dehumidifier and exhausted continuously to building exhaust. The surrounding room sits at 21.7-22.2 C and 35-60% relative humidity (RH), dew point 6-13 C, tracking outdoor humidity. On the first night with the dehumidifier on, air inside the enclosure was 24 C at 35-42% RH (dew point 8-10 C) while the room was expected at about 22 C and 56% RH (dew point about 13 C); a hand-held reading showed about 27 C (80 F) at about 30% RH. So the compressor heat raises the enclosure temperature by roughly 2-5 C while the dehumidifier removes about 15-30% of the water vapor. Powders stored there, in sealed containers with silica-gel packets inside chemical storage cabinets, include gas-atomized AlSi10Mg (15-45 um, from MSE Supplies), silicon powder (98-99.5% Si), stainless-steel powder, and soon ultrasonically atomized aluminum alloys containing Mg, Cu, Zn, Li, Sc, Zr, Ce and Er, made in an argon-purged atomizer (AMAZEMET rePowder). Question: what do published powder-storage, powder-ageing and powder-reuse studies on laser powder bed fusion AlSi10Mg (and other aluminum AM powders such as AlSi7Mg, Scalmalloy, Al-Cu and Al-Ce alloys) report about oxygen pickup, moisture or hydrogen pickup, flowability (Hall flow, avalanche angle, cohesion) and hydrogen porosity in printed parts after storage at controlled temperature and humidity? Report conditions (temperature, RH, container, duration), measured changes with numbers, and DOIs. Which drying procedures (temperature, time, vacuum) were shown to restore the powder? What storage temperature and RH do aluminum AM powder suppliers (e.g., Valimet, Carpenter Additive, IMR Metal Powder Technologies, ECKART, Oerlikon, EOS, SLM Solutions) and standards or guides (ASTM F3049, ISO/ASTM 52907, ASTM F3318, ASTM F3571 or others) recommend? Finally, what is known about the risks introduced by temperature differences and cycling during storage: condensation when a container stored in a cooler place is opened in warmer humid air or vice versa, pressure-driven air exchange ('breathing') of imperfectly sealed containers, and the reduced water capacity of silica gel at higher temperature? Could silica gel or its dust contaminate aluminum powders stored with it? Give quantitative values with DOIs, and say explicitly where evidence is lacking.

Published studies on AlSi10Mg and related aluminum AM powders report that storage and aging under humid or oxidative conditions both significantly affect powder oxygen content, moisture response, flowability, and porosity in printed parts. Below are detailed, quantitative, and referenced findings for the topics of interest:

1. **Oxygen, Moisture, and Hydrogen Pickup During Storage/Aging**:
   - **Oxygen Content**: In Fedina et al. (2022), thermal powder aging (96 h in muffle furnace; details for RH/container not specified) caused oxygen to increase from 0.067 wt% (virgin) to 0.257 wt% (aged) for AlSi10Mg. When recycled (“R0”) powder was aged 96 h, O increased from 0.072 wt% to 0.274 wt%. Hydrogen content did not rise significantly (0.003 wt% to 0.004 wt%) (fedina2022influenceofalsi10mg pages 4-5, fedina2022influenceofalsi10mg pages 5-6).
   - **Moisture Pickup**: Cordova et al. (2017) and Peres et al. (2024) show AlSi10Mg is highly sensitive to humidity; after 72 h at 50°C and 80% RH, flowability is destroyed (does not flow through Hall funnel). Moisture content was measured (Karl Fischer, TGA) but not always reported as numbers; strong qualitative and visual changes occurred. Moisture can be removed by drying, but oxygen content persists unless chemically reduced (peres2024influenceofmoisture pages 2-3, cordova2017assessmentofmoisture pages 5-6, cordova2017assessmentofmoisture pages 1-3).
   - **Hydrogen Pickup**: Both Fedina et al. (2022) and Da Silva et al. (2022) (DED, not LPBF) found little change in hydrogen in the powder state with aging, though significant increases in porosity in printed parts suggest possible hydrogen involvement, especially when ambient humidity is high (silva2022influenceofaluminium pages 1-2).

2. **Flowability Changes**:
   - **Hall Flow/Avalanche Angle**: Cordova et al. (2017) show that after high-humidity exposure, AlSi10Mg does not flow in a Hall funnel, while vacuum drying restores flow partially. Muñiz-Lerma et al. (2018) found Hall flow for AlSi7Mg at 33.0 ± 0.4 s/50g (powder A), but only under 40% RH; finer, less spherical powders have worse flow, particularly after humid storage. Carney funnel tests predictably worsen after aging/humidification for AlSi10Mg and improve with vacuum drying. Agglomeration and increased static and dynamic repose angles are observed at higher humidity (cordova2017assessmentofmoisture pages 5-6, munizlerma2018acomprehensiveapproach pages 8-11, munizlerma2018acomprehensiveapproach pages 5-8).

3. **Hydrogen Porosity in Printed Parts**:
   - **Correlation to Storage**: Fedina et al. (2022) show part porosity rises from 3.16% for virgin-powder parts to 6.5% for aged-powder parts with higher oxygen content, but do not prove hydrogen is responsible for all added pores. Da Silva et al. (2022), using DED, found 3.5–4.2× more porosity in parts made from aged powder. Yield strength decreased by 19.0%, UTS by 14%, and elongation increased 99%—effects attributed mainly to hydrogen pores and oxide inclusions in DED, but direct measurements for LPBF are lacking (fedina2022influenceofalsi10mg pages 1-2, silva2022influenceofaluminium pages 1-2).

4. **Restoration (Drying) Procedures**:
   - **Protocols**: Vacuum drying (85°C/6 h), air drying (150°C/20 min), and high-temperature low-humidity storage (50°C/17% RH) restored AlSi10Mg flowability after moisture damage, though not always to as-received values. Tonelli et al. (2020) used 60°C/3 h or 200°C/1 h (ambient air). Restoration is often qualitative in reports; no universal recipe fits all lots and damage levels, and not all samples regain full flow. Details like vacuum level or final moisture/flow numbers are generally omitted (cordova2017assessmentofmoisture pages 1-3, peres2024influenceofmoisture pages 2-3, tonelli2020effectsofpowders pages 4-8).

5. **Guidance from Standards/Suppliers**:
   - ISO/ASTM 52928 and ISO/ASTM 52907 recommend storing metal powders at 15–25°C and below 55% RH, preferably in dry, inert atmospheres (e.g., argon for reactive or oxidation-prone alloys), and acclimatizing to local dew point before opening. Regular re-checks (after ~6 months) of O2, moisture, and flow are advised. No direct storage recipe from ASTM F3049, F3318, or F3571 found. Supplier-specific protocols are not available in published literature or standards-reported evidence (gomes2023analysisanddevelopment pages 42-44, gomes2023analysisanddevelopment pages 38-42).

6. **Risks from Temperature Differences and Cycling; Silica Gel and Contamination**:
   See the following detailed blockquote for a technical summary:
> **Condensation threshold.** Condensation is controlled by **dew point, not RH alone**. With room air at a dew point near **13°C**, any powder, container, or lid surface below about **13°C** can acquire liquid water when opened there. Enclosure air with an **8–10°C dew point** would require a surface below **8–10°C** for condensation. Therefore, powder equilibrated at **22–27°C** will not condense water merely because it is moved between the stated room and enclosure; the significant case is a container arriving from storage or transport below the receiving-air dew point. Guidance to keep powder and container under the same environmental conditions is consistent with minimizing condensation during transfer. (gomes2023analysisanddevelopment pages 42-44, gomes2023analysisanddevelopment pages 38-42)
>
> **Warm-to-cool transfer.** Opening a warm container in cooler air does not ordinarily condense water on the still-warm powder. Risk appears later if humid replacement air is admitted and the closed container subsequently cools below that air’s dew point. Thus, acclimatization should occur while the container remains sealed, followed by opening only after container temperature exceeds the local dew point with a safety margin.
>
> **Pressure-driven “breathing.”** For a rigid headspace containing ideal gas, heating from **22°C to 27°C** raises absolute pressure by approximately **27/295 = 1.7%** if perfectly sealed—about **1.4 kPa** at Provo’s approximate **85 kPa** atmospheric pressure. An imperfect seal may vent roughly this order of headspace volume while warming and draw humid room air back while cooling; repeated cycles can progressively defeat an argon purge or desiccant. The actual exchanged fraction depends strongly on seal leakage, headspace, heating rate, and lid compliance. **No retrieved aluminum-AM study directly measured breathing rates, oxygen/water ingress, or powder degradation under 2–5°C storage cycles.** Standards-oriented guidance instead favors stable conditions, inert gas for oxidation-prone powders, and temperature/RH/O₂ monitoring. (gomes2023analysisanddevelopment pages 42-44, gomes2023analysisanddevelopment pages 38-42)
>
> **Why small cycles still matter.** At constant water-vapor content, cooling raises RH; warming lowers RH but does not by itself remove water from a closed container. Consequently, the enclosure’s lower displayed RH partly reflects its **2–5°C higher temperature**, while its lower dew point demonstrates that the dehumidifier also removes water. Published AlSi10Mg conditioning studies show severe flow degradation at **50°C/80% RH for 72 h**—including failure to flow—and recurrent agglomeration at high RH, but they do not establish a harmless threshold for repeated modest thermal cycles. (cordova2017assessmentofmoisture pages 5-6, peres2024influenceofmoisture pages 1-2, cordova2017assessmentofmoisture pages 1-3)
>
> **Silica-gel temperature effect.** Silica gel’s equilibrium water loading generally decreases as temperature rises, so warming can reduce its remaining adsorption capacity and can desorb part of previously held water into a closed headspace. Cooling reverses the equilibrium but may temporarily expose powder to higher RH. **The retrieved sources provide no quantitative silica-gel isotherm, packet-sizing rule, or measured capacity at 22 versus 27°C**, so a numerical capacity penalty for this enclosure cannot be supported here. The relevant AlSi10Mg experiment used silica gel to obtain approximately **23°C/29% RH**, but did not quantify gel loading, lifetime, or higher-temperature capacity. (peres2024influenceofmoisture pages 1-2)
>
> **Possible contamination from packets.** Intact, low-dusting, vapor-permeable packets kept physically separate from powder present little direct-transfer opportunity. A torn packet, friable granules, or dusty packet exterior could introduce silica particles or indicator chemicals; these foreign particles could alter flow, chemistry, or inclusion populations. **No retrieved LPBF aluminum study demonstrated silica-gel dust contamination, quantified a tolerable silica concentration, or linked packet use to printed-part defects.** Therefore, contamination is plausible but presently unquantified; loose desiccant should not contact powder, packets should be inspected and restrained outside the powder bed, and indicating gels containing cobalt salts should be avoided where chemical purity matters.
>
> **Practical implication for this laboratory.** The reported enclosure condition is not intrinsically a condensation hazard for powder already near room temperature, and its **8–10°C dew point** is better than the room’s approximately **13°C** dew point. The more defensible controls are stable temperature, genuinely airtight containers with minimal headspace, sealed acclimatization before opening, dew-point logging, protected/non-dusting desiccant, and periodic moisture, oxygen, and flow testing rather than reliance on cabinet RH alone. General guidance cited in the retrieved record recommends approximately **15–25°C and <55% RH**, inert handling for reactive powders, and rechecking oxygen, moisture, and flow after extended storage; however, the provenance of those numerical limits should be verified against the current full standard before treating them as normative requirements. (gomes2023analysisanddevelopment pages 42-44, gomes2023analysisanddevelopment pages 38-42)

**Limitations and Gaps**:
- No study provided a fully quantitative isotherm or packet-sizing for silica gel at 22–27°C; nor did any directly link silica gel dust to AM aluminum part contamination.
- Air exchange (“breathing”) and its O2/moisture ingress during 2–5°C storage cycles were not measured directly in reviewed AlSi10Mg studies.
- Most Hall flow, water content, and restoration results are qualitative rather than quantitative for AlSi10Mg; some protocols (e.g., container type, precise storage duration) are missing from most reports.
- Supplier-specific recommendations (for Valimet, Carpenter, IMR, ECKART, Oerlikon, EOS, SLM Solutions) are absent from academic literature; only generic standards are cited.
