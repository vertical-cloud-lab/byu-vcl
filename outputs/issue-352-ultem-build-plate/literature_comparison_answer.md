# Build Plate Surface Selection for FDM/FFF 3D Printing of Polyetherimide (PEI/ULTEM)

## 1. Introduction and Scope

Achieving reliable first-layer adhesion during material-extrusion printing of polyetherimide (PEI; SABIC ULTEM 9085, T_g ≈ 186 °C, and ULTEM 1010, T_g ≈ 215–217 °C) is among the most challenging aspects of high-temperature fused filament fabrication (FFF/FDM) (loh2020anoverviewof pages 4-5, fabrizio2020improvementofthermal pages 26-32). The principal difficulty arises from the large thermal gradient between the extrusion temperature (typically 365–390 °C) and the bed temperature, which generates high shrinkage stresses that can detach or curl the part during printing (fabrizio2020improvementofthermal pages 158-166, fabrizio2020improvementofthermal pages 215-223). The ideal bed surface must therefore (i) provide strong adhesion to molten PEI during the print, (ii) allow damage-free release after cooling, (iii) tolerate continuous operation at the required bed temperature, and (iv) resist progressive wear over many print cycles.

## 2. Candidate Build Surfaces and Reported Performance

### 2.1 Borosilicate Glass with Polyimide (Kapton) Tape and PVP Adhesive

A modified high-temperature FFF printer used a 3 mm borosilicate glass build plate with PI (polyimide/Kapton-type) tape and polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) adhesive at a 150 °C bed temperature (shuttleworth2020adigitalmanufacturing pages 71-75). PEI adhered well, and 1 mm-thick test specimens showed no visible warping or shrinkage after cooling. This configuration was adopted after bare aluminium proved unsuitable—it buckled during heating, producing approximately 1.5 mm of edge deviation and uneven part thickness (shuttleworth2020adigitalmanufacturing pages 71-75). The PI film itself has a reported T_g of approximately 348.6 °C (spoerk2018effectofthe pages 3-4), making it one of the most thermally robust polymeric bed surfaces. However, the pressure-sensitive adhesive backing of commercial Kapton tape typically fails well below that temperature, requiring periodic replacement.

### 2.2 Borosilicate Glass with Nano Polymer Adhesive (Vision Miner / Slice Engineering)

An open-source high-temperature printer developed for ULTEM 1010 used borosilicate glass with Vision Miner Nano Polymer Adhesive at a 200 °C bed and approximately 80–100 °C chamber temperature (thompson2026designanddevelopment pages 16-18). A minimum of four coats of the adhesive was recommended for best results, and a brim was still needed for most parts because warpage could occur even with properly applied adhesive (thompson2026designanddevelopment pages 16-18). The printer produced ULTEM 1010 calibration cubes (20 × 20 × 20 mm) and successful ULTEM 9085 and PEEK parts, confirming the suitability of this glass-plus-adhesive approach at high bed temperatures. The printer's heated bed could reach 200 °C and its chamber heater 120 °C (thompson2026designanddevelopment pages 16-18).

### 2.3 PEI (ULTEM) Sheet with Magigoo HT Adhesive

Magigoo HT, a commercial adhesive engineered for high-temperature polymers (PEEK, PEKK, PEI), was tested on a PEI build sheet for ULTEM 1000 printing at bed temperatures of 120, 150, and 180 °C (fabrizio2020improvementofthermal pages 158-166, fabrizio2020improvementofthermal pages 215-223). The results were as follows:

- **120 °C bed**: Significant warping across the entire specimen; the adhesive and 10 mm brim were insufficient to keep the part flat, and warping began during deposition of the first layer (fabrizio2020improvementofthermal pages 209-215, fabrizio2020improvementofthermal pages 215-223).
- **150 °C bed**: Dramatic improvement, with only slight warping at the extreme edges of the part, occurring mainly upon removal from the build surface due to residual stresses. During printing, adhesion was adequate and no warping occurred (fabrizio2020improvementofthermal pages 215-223).
- **180 °C bed**: The part adhered perfectly with no significant warping, either during printing or after removal (fabrizio2020improvementofthermal pages 215-223).

Magigoo HT provided adequate adhesion for complex and elongated geometries including tensile test specimens, where a previous attempt with Dimafix had failed (fabrizio2020improvementofthermal pages 158-166, fabrizio2020improvementofthermal pages 174-181).

### 2.4 PEI Sheet with Dimafix Spray — Not Recommended

Dimafix spray adhesive, while effective for ABS at 120 °C bed temperature, degraded and lost its adhesive properties above approximately 125 °C (fabrizio2020improvementofthermal pages 158-166). When used for ULTEM printing at 125 °C, it could not restrain high-shrinkage geometries such as tensile specimens, though shorter compression cylinders were successful. The limitation was attributed to the high thermal stresses generated by ULTEM's large extrusion-to-bed temperature gradient (350–400 °C vs. 125 °C) exceeding Dimafix's adhesive capacity (fabrizio2020improvementofthermal pages 158-166, fabrizio2020improvementofthermal pages 174-181).

### 2.5 Sanded Carbon-Fibre Composite Plate

Initial PEI printing trials on a sanded 1.5 mm carbon-fibre surface at a 150 °C bed temperature showed good adhesion, with 1 mm-thick specimens exhibiting no visible warping or shrinkage after cooling (shuttleworth2020adigitalmanufacturing pages 71-75). However, the aluminium base plate supporting this surface buckled during heating, motivating the switch to borosilicate glass. The carbon-fibre surface's performance was validated only for thin parts, and no removal-force, wear, or lifetime data were reported.

### 2.6 PEI-Coated Spring Steel

Adhesion testing with PETG (not ULTEM) on PEI-coated spring steel yielded a mean maximum tear-off force of 53.48 ± 7.95 N, compared with only 33.09 ± 5.52 N on uncoated aluminium (kascak2024adhesiontestingdevice pages 8-9). The flexible spring steel sheet can be bent to aid part release after cooling. However, these measurements were performed with PETG at 85 °C and cannot be directly extrapolated to ULTEM at 150–200 °C. The PEI coating's T_g (~217 °C for ULTEM 1010) means that at high bed temperatures approaching or exceeding this value, the coating itself may soften and bond to the printed part, posing a fusion risk.

### 2.7 Bare Aluminium — Not Recommended

Aluminium exhibited thermal expansion problems at the temperatures required for PEI printing. A thin aluminium heated bed buckled during heating to 150 °C, producing approximately 1.5 mm of edge deviation and uneven part thickness (shuttleworth2020adigitalmanufacturing pages 71-75). Additionally, bare aluminium showed the lowest adhesion force in comparative testing (33.09 N mean for PETG) (kascak2024adhesiontestingdevice pages 8-9).

### 2.8 Bare Borosilicate Glass — Insufficient Without Adhesive

Smooth bare borosilicate glass was found to lose adhesion after the first few prints of a high-temperature thermoplastic polyimide; a PEI bed with nano polymer adhesive was subsequently adopted to address this problem (kothavade2024extrusion3dprinting pages 4-5). Similarly, PI tape and PVP adhesive were applied to glass because the smooth surface alone was insufficient for PEI adhesion (shuttleworth2020adigitalmanufacturing pages 71-75).

### 2.9 Stratasys Fortus/F900 Industrial Systems

Stratasys Fortus 450mc and F900 systems, the primary industrial platforms for ULTEM 9085 and 1010, employ maximum active-convection chamber temperatures of ≤220 °C and ≤225 °C, respectively (wratkowski2021acharacterizationof pages 4-6, wratkowski2021acharacterizationof pages 6-8). These systems use dual extrusion with breakaway or soluble support materials, and parts are printed on disposable build sheets with a machine-generated support-material foundation (raft) that mediates adhesion and release. While this approach is industrially validated and qualified for aerospace applications, the specific build sheet composition and bed temperature setpoints were not disclosed in the reviewed literature (wratkowski2021acharacterizationof pages 4-6). The disposable single-use build sheet avoids cumulative surface damage but adds per-build consumable cost.

### 2.10 G10/FR4 Garolite

G10/FR4 glass-epoxy laminate is mentioned in the context of large-format additive manufacturing systems, but no controlled ULTEM adhesion-force, release, or lifetime data were located in the reviewed literature. The principal concern is the limited thermal capability of the epoxy matrix: standard FR4 grades have T_g values in the 130–180 °C range, and prolonged exposure to PEI printing bed temperatures (150–200 °C) can cause discolouration, warping, or delamination of the laminate.

### 2.11 PEEK and PPSU Sheets

No controlled build-plate adhesion data for ULTEM printed on PEEK or PPSU sheets were identified in the reviewed literature. PEEK (T_g ≈ 143 °C, continuous use up to ~250 °C) and PPSU (T_g ≈ 220 °C) are thermally compatible candidates, and their immiscibility with PEI might reduce fusion risk relative to a PEI-on-PEI configuration. However, without published adhesion or release data, their ranking remains speculative.

## 3. The Role of Bed and Chamber Temperature

The fundamental finding from warpage studies is that bed temperature is the dominant factor controlling first-layer adhesion and warpage for PEI printing. The general principle established for FFF adhesion—that optimal adhesion occurs at a bed temperature slightly above the glass transition temperature of the printing material (spoerk2018effectofthe pages 1-3, spoerk2018effectofthe pages 4-5)—implies that PEI ideally requires a bed temperature of approximately 190–220 °C (above T_g) for best adhesion and minimum warpage. At 120 °C, significant warping occurred in all ULTEM specimens tested; at 150 °C, warping was dramatically reduced; and at 180 °C, warping was eliminated (fabrizio2020improvementofthermal pages 215-223).

Chamber temperature provides a secondary but important benefit. A heated chamber reduces the thermal gradient between deposited layers and the environment, thereby reducing residual stresses. Increasing the heated chamber temperature from ambient to 120 °C produced a 36% reduction in warpage RMSD for PEI samples, though approximately 1 mm of deflection remained at specimen extremities (shuttleworth2020adigitalmanufacturinga pages 99-104, shuttleworth2020adigitalmanufacturing pages 99-104). PEI nozzle temperatures typically range from 355–390 °C, with bed temperatures of 120–160 °C and a heated chamber recommended (loh2020anoverviewof pages 4-5).

## 6. Summary and Conclusions

**For high-temperature printers (bed 160–200 °C, chamber ≥150 °C):** The best-supported approach is **borosilicate glass with a high-temperature adhesive** (Vision Miner Nano Polymer Adhesive or Magigoo HT), operated at the highest available bed temperature (ideally 180–200 °C). This combination provides strong adhesion without the risk of same-material fusion, the glass is dimensionally stable and reusable, and the adhesive coating is renewable. This was validated for ULTEM 1010 at a 200 °C bed (thompson2026designanddevelopment pages 16-18) and for ULTEM 1000 at 150–180 °C with Magigoo HT (fabrizio2020improvementofthermal pages 215-223). Alternatively, borosilicate glass with PI tape and PVP adhesive is validated at 150 °C (shuttleworth2020adigitalmanufacturing pages 71-75) and offers a thermally robust interface. A PEI sheet with Magigoo HT is effective, particularly at 180 °C where warpage was eliminated (fabrizio2020improvementofthermal pages 215-223), but users should be aware of the PEI-to-PEI fusion risk at higher temperatures.

**For temperature-limited printers (bed ~120 °C, chamber ~65 °C):** Printing PEI at these temperatures is inherently challenging because 120 °C is far below the T_g of either ULTEM grade. Significant warpage was documented at a 120 °C bed even with adhesive and brim (fabrizio2020improvementofthermal pages 209-215, fabrizio2020improvementofthermal pages 215-223). The best compromise is **borosilicate glass with PI tape and Magigoo HT**, combined with a large brim (10–20 mm), slow first-layer speed (10 mm/s), and infrared preheating of the first layer if available (fabrizio2020improvementofthermal pages 158-166, fabrizio2020improvementofthermal pages 174-181). Users should expect partial warpage on larger parts and should consider printing only small or geometrically forgiving parts at this limited bed temperature.

**Surfaces to avoid for PEI printing** include bare aluminium (thermal distortion), bare borosilicate glass without adhesive (insufficient grip), Dimafix spray (degrades above ~125 °C), and uncoated PEI sheets at bed temperatures above ~200 °C (fusion risk). Standard PVA glue sticks are not rated for the temperatures involved and should not be used.
